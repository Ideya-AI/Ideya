# Effective Altruism — research

## Overview

Effective altruism is a movement and a way of reasoning about doing good. It asks two questions at once: how much a person in a rich country is morally required to give to strangers far away, and, once they decide to give, how to choose where the money goes. Its founding answer to the first, drawn from the philosopher Peter Singer’s 1972 essay “Famine, Affluence, and Morality,” is that distance makes no moral difference and that if we can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything morally significant, we ought to. Its answer to the second is to compare causes and charities by how much good each dollar does, using evidence and cost-effectiveness estimates, and to give where the estimate is highest. This page covers both questions and the rival answers to them: those who think morality asks less, or asks us to favor the people and places we are tied to; those who think the best giving targets the political and economic systems that produce need rather than the needs themselves; those who think good giving runs through trust and relationships with the people closest to a problem rather than through a funder’s metrics; and those who think the real question is not how private wealth should give but whether so much of it should be private at all. The page deliberately leaves to their own pages the case for prioritizing the far future and existential risks from AI and other technologies (what we owe future generations, AI existential risk), the moral status of animals (moral status of animals), and the wider fight over taxing the very rich (economic inequality, how to tax extreme wealth). It touches those only where they bear on how to give.

Why now: in mid-September 2026 effective altruism became a target of the Trump administration. A Pentagon technology account posted an image of the president with the slogan “Americanism, Not Effective Altruism!” as part of a government social media campaign against calls to slow AI development (Semafor, 17 September 2026, https://www.semafor.com/article/09/17/2026/behind-the-trump-administrations-pro-ai-meme-campaign); in February Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had attacked the AI company Anthropic for what he called the “sanctimonious rhetoric of ‘effective altruism’” (Fortune, 23 September 2026, https://fortune.com/2026/09/23/effective-altruism-went-from-fringe-philosophy-to-trumps-ai-villain/). The same month, the Institute for Policy Studies published Gilded Giving 2026: Philanthropy Under Oligarchy (15 September 2026, https://ips-dc.org/philanthropy-under-oligarchy/), arguing that big philanthropy is warehousing wealth and concentrating power. Meanwhile the movement’s giving side has grown fast in response to cuts in government aid: GiveWell raised $616 million in its 2025 metrics year, up 49 percent from 2024 (GiveWell, 31 August 2026, https://blog.givewell.org/2026/08/31/givewells-2025-metrics-and-impact/), and Giving What We Can estimates that donations moved by the effective giving ecosystem as a whole rose from about $1.2 billion in 2024 to about $2 billion in 2025 (EA Forum, 28 July 2026, https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/T7CKswFYSgjePAwZh/updates-on-the-effective-giving-ecosystem-2026). Official development aid from the rich-country donors of the OECD fell 23.1 percent in 2025, the largest annual drop on record, with the United States accounting for three-quarters of the decline (OECD, 9 April 2026, https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2026/04/international-aid-fell-sharply-in-2025-says-oecd.html).

The debate is live because the stakes and the money are both rising. Large new fortunes, many of them in technology, are deciding where to go, and some of their holders are tied to the movement: Open Philanthropy, funded mainly by Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna, renamed itself Coefficient Giving in November 2025 to raise money from other donors, and MacKenzie Scott gave $7.1 billion in 2025 under the opposite philosophy of large, unrestricted gifts with few strings. At the same time the movement still carries the reputational damage of Sam Bankman-Fried, its most prominent donor until the 2022 collapse of his FTX exchange, whose 25-year sentence for fraud was upheld on appeal in June 2026. The participants are philosophers (Singer, William MacAskill, Amia Srinivasan, Alice Crary), charity evaluators (GiveWell, Giving What We Can, The Life You Can Save), the large foundations (Gates, Ford, Coefficient Giving), trust-based and community-centered funders, the conservative tradition of local and religious giving, critics of philanthropic power (the Institute for Policy Studies, Anand Giridharadas), development economists, and, now, the US government.

Note: GiveWell’s models estimate that a gift to its top charities saves a life, on average, for between $3,000 and $5,500.
Context: This is the number at the heart of the movement’s case that a modest donation can do something enormous. GiveWell, a charity evaluator founded in 2007, writes that its models for grants to its top charities “have typically estimated an average cost between $3,000 and $5,500” per life saved. Its four top charities deliver seasonal malaria medicine to children, insecticide-treated bed nets, vitamin A supplements, and cash incentives for childhood vaccination. The page gives a worked example of a 2024 grant to Malaria Consortium in Nigeria where about $3,000 is estimated to save one life, and warns that the same program can vary widely in cost-effectiveness from place to place and that costs are expected to rise over time. The estimate counts deaths averted, mostly of young children, and does not count other benefits. Supporters treat it as the best available answer to “what can my money do?”; critics, including the philosopher Leif Wenar (Wired, 2024), argue that such figures rest on long chains of uncertain assumptions and leave out harms and side effects that are hard to measure.

Source label: Caviola, Schubert, Teperman, Moss, Greenberg and Faber, “Donors vastly underestimate differences in charities’ effectiveness,” Judgment and Decision Making 15(4), 2020
Source URL: https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/20/200504/jdm200504.html
Note: Asked how much more effective the most effective charity helping the global poor is than the average one, lay people’s median answer was 1.5 times; 45 surveyed experts said 100 times.
Context: The study asked samples of people to estimate the ratio between the cost-effectiveness of the most effective charity and the average charity among those helping the world’s poor. The median lay estimate was 1.5; effective altruists estimated a factor of about 50, and a survey of 45 experts in global health, development economics and charity evaluation gave a median of 100. In a later experiment with 423 US participants, telling people the ratio was 100 raised the share who gave a whole hypothetical $100 to the more effective charity to 55.6 percent, from 37 to 38 percent in the comparison conditions. Effective altruists read this as evidence that ordinary donors leave most of their potential impact on the table because they do not realize how much charities differ. The experts’ figure is itself an estimate, and those skeptical of cost-effectiveness comparisons argue that the differences shrink, or become unknowable, once hard-to-measure effects and the work of political and community organizations are counted.

The misperception bears on the question of how much rich societies already do for distant strangers: Singer and effective altruists argue that both governments and individuals give far less than people believe, while others argue that a nation’s first obligations are to its own citizens whatever the true share.

## Questions

Question: How much does morality require well-off people to give to strangers in need?
Tag: value
Poles: Give a great deal / Giving is a choice
Why contested: Almost everyone agrees that helping people in desperate need is good. The disagreement is whether it is required, and how much. Peter Singer’s 1972 argument, that if we can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything morally significant we ought to, implies that most people in rich countries are failing a serious duty every day. Most people, and most moral traditions, treat generous giving as admirable but beyond what anyone can demand, and many philosophers argue that a morality that asks for nearly everything cannot be the right one.
Case for give a great deal: If you walked past a child drowning in a shallow pond, you would wade in even if it ruined your clothes, and no one would think that heroic; failing to do it would be monstrous. A child dying of malaria far away is no less a child, and the cost of saving one through an effective charity, a few thousand dollars, is for many people in rich countries comparable to a holiday or a newer car. The fact that millions of others could also help does not reduce your responsibility, any more than a crowd at the pond would. On this view the conventional idea that charity is optional is a convenient habit, not a moral insight, and a 10 percent pledge is a modest, achievable floor rather than a heroic sacrifice.
Case for giving is a choice: Morality has to leave room for a person to have a life of their own: to raise children, pursue projects and keep commitments that are not merely instruments for maximizing welfare. A principle that demands giving until one is nearly as badly off as those one helps would condemn almost every decent person who has ever lived, which is a sign the principle is wrong. The drowning-child case is a single, immediate emergency in which one person is uniquely placed to help; global poverty is a permanent, structural condition shared among billions of potential helpers, and the obligation to address it falls on institutions and governments, not on each individual without limit. Generosity is a virtue precisely because it is freely given.

Question: Should people give first to their own family, community and country, or treat all strangers alike?
Tag: value
Poles: Distance doesn’t matter / Near comes first
Why contested: Effective altruism holds that a person’s welfare counts the same wherever they live, and so a dollar should go where it helps most, usually in the poorest countries. Most actual giving goes to congregations, schools, local services and causes donors are personally connected to: in 2025 religion received about a quarter of US giving and international affairs about 5 percent (Giving USA 2026). The question is whether that pattern is a moral failure or a legitimate expression of the ties that make a good life.
Case for distance doesn’t matter: Where a person happens to be born is a matter of luck, and luck cannot make their suffering count for less. A dollar spent in a rich country’s community already rich in services buys far less relief than the same dollar spent where children die of preventable disease. Special obligations to family are real, but they do not extend to favoring a comfortable neighbor’s museum over a stranger’s child’s life. Impartiality is the core of the moral point of view, and the instinct to give close to home is a bias that a thoughtful giver should correct, as people learned to correct other biases about who counts.
Case for near comes first: Human beings learn to care through particular attachments, to family, neighbors, congregations and towns, and a giving culture built on those ties is what sustains civil society. Local giving is informed by knowledge a distant donor cannot have, is accountable to people the giver will see again, and builds the habits of citizenship. William Schambra of the Hudson Institute argued that treating local loyalty as an error to be corrected reflects a failure of moral imagination, enlarging the faculties used to perceive the far while eclipsing those used to care for those closest. Obligations of membership and reciprocity are real moral facts, not biases, and a world in which everyone gave impartially would be a world in which no community could count on its own.

Question: Can cost-effectiveness estimates reliably tell donors which charities do the most good?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Estimates guide well / Estimates mislead
Why contested: The whole effective altruism method depends on comparing the expected good per dollar of different interventions. GiveWell estimates its top charities save a life for $3,000 to $5,500; surveyed experts think the best global poverty charities are about 100 times as cost-effective as the average one (Caviola and others, 2020). Critics argue the estimates rest on chains of uncertain assumptions, miss side effects and harms, and systematically favor what is easy to measure.
Case for estimates guide well: Charities differ enormously in what they achieve, and donors who do not compare them leave most of their potential impact unused. The interventions effective altruists favor, such as bed nets, malaria medicine and vitamin A, are among the most studied in development, backed by randomized trials and decades of data. GiveWell publishes its models line by line, revises them when evidence changes (it raised its estimate for cash transfers three- to fourfold in 2024 when new spillover evidence came in), and discounts uncertain benefits. An imperfect estimate is still better than giving by habit or sentiment, and the alternative to explicit numbers is implicit ones that no one can check.
Case for estimates mislead: Spreadsheets give a false precision to judgments that are deeply uncertain. Leif Wenar, a Stanford philosopher, argued in Wired in March 2024 (https://www.wired.com/story/deaths-of-effective-altruism/) that effective altruist charity evaluations understate uncertainty and neglect the side effects and harms of aid, including its political effects on the governments of poor countries, which are real but hard to count. Studies in one place do not reliably transfer to another; outcomes like dignity, political voice and the strength of local institutions are left out because they cannot be expressed in lives per dollar; and the method steers money toward narrow, measurable programs and away from the slower, harder-to-quantify work that often matters most.

Question: Does funding political and systemic change do more good than funding proven direct help?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Change the system / Fund proven help
Why contested: Critics on the left have long charged that effective altruism treats symptoms of poverty while leaving its causes in place, and that its preference for measurable interventions blinds it to movements, organizing and policy change. Effective altruists answer that many of them do fund policy and systemic change, judged by expected value, but that most systemic bets fail and their payoffs are speculative. Whether systemic work has higher expected returns than direct aid is in principle an empirical question, but one with very little reliable evidence on either side.
Case for change the system: Malaria, child mortality and extreme poverty persist because of how economies, governments, trade and tax rules are arranged, and the largest gains in human welfare, from the abolition of slavery to the expansion of public health and the fall in global poverty, came through political and economic change rather than charity. A dollar that helps a movement win a minimum wage, a land reform or a public health system can outlast and outscale any number of bed nets. Darren Walker, the Ford Foundation’s president until November 2025, framed this as moving “from generosity to justice”: addressing the root causes that create the need for charity in the first place.
Case for fund proven help: Systemic change sounds larger but usually fails, and when it succeeds, it is hard to know whether any particular donation made the difference. Many proposed systemic changes are contested, and a donor who funds one side of a political fight may be doing harm by the lights of half the population. Proven programs save identifiable lives now, with high confidence, and their benefits do not depend on winning a political battle. Where systemic bets look good on expected value, such as lead paint bans or corporate campaigns for farm animal welfare, effective altruist funders already make them; the disagreement is about evidence, not ideology.

Question: Who should decide how charitable money is used: donors guided by evidence, or the people and communities it is meant to help?
Tag: value
Poles: Evidence-led donors / Communities decide
Why contested: Effective altruism puts the donor, or an evaluator acting for the donor, in charge of choosing among interventions by their measured results. Trust-based and community-centered philanthropy holds that funders should give unrestricted, long-term money and let the organizations and communities closest to a problem decide, because they know more and because the power imbalance between funders and recipients is itself a problem. Cash transfers, which let recipients choose, sit in between and are championed by both.
Case for evidence-led donors: Good intentions and local knowledge are not enough; many well-loved programs have turned out to do nothing or to do harm when tested. A donor owes it to the people they want to help to find out what works, and a funder who gives without evaluating is choosing comfort over results. Evidence-led giving is not paternalistic when the evidence is about what recipients themselves value, such as their children surviving and their incomes rising; GiveWell’s own models draw on surveys of how recipients in poor countries weigh outcomes.
Case for communities decide: Funders far from a problem routinely misjudge it, and reporting requirements, restricted grants and metrics chosen by donors drain small organizations and bend their work toward what funders can count. The people living with a problem understand it best and are accountable to their neighbors in a way a distant donor is not. A Center for Effective Philanthropy study of MacKenzie Scott’s unrestricted gifts found that nearly 90 percent of grantee leaders said the money strengthened their organizations’ finances. Trust is not naïveté: it is a recognition that philanthropy’s power over grantees distorts the work, and that sharing that power produces better and more durable results.

Question: Does outside aid help poor countries in the long run, or weaken the institutions they need?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Aid helps / Aid undermines
Why contested: Development economists have argued for decades about whether foreign aid speeds or slows development. Effective altruist giving concentrates on health programs whose short-term effects are well measured, but critics, including the Nobel laureate Angus Deaton, argue that large flows of outside money can weaken the relationship between governments and the citizens who pay taxes to them. The 2025 aid cuts have turned the question from theory into a live test.
Case for aid helps: Health aid has a strong record: global child deaths have fallen by more than half since 2000, malaria control has averted an estimated 14 million deaths since 2000 (WHO), and programs against HIV, malaria and vaccine-preventable diseases have measurable, large effects. A modeling study in The Lancet estimated that USAID-funded programs prevented 91 million deaths between 2001 and 2021. Whatever the debate about aid and growth, there is little doubt that bed nets, vaccines and antiretroviral drugs keep people alive, and that people who survive childhood and stay healthy are better placed to build their economies.
Case for aid undermines: When governments depend on donors rather than on their citizens, they answer to donors. Deaton argues that the countries that have escaped poverty did so through their own growth and institutions, and that aid can prop up bad governments, distort local markets and draw skilled workers away from public services. On this view the most useful things people in rich countries can do for the poor abroad are to stop harming them, for example through arms sales, trade barriers and tax havens, and to support the political conditions in which poor countries govern themselves, rather than to substitute outside programs for domestic responsibility.

Question: Is large-scale private philanthropy a legitimate use of private wealth in a democracy?
Tag: value
Poles: Legitimate private choice / Undemocratic power
Why contested: Effective altruists, trust-based funders and justice-oriented foundations all assume that private giving is good and argue about how to do it. A further camp questions the premise: that billionaires, subsidized by tax deductions worth about $70 billion a year in the United States (Joint Committee on Taxation), should be the ones deciding priorities in global health, education and politics. The question is whether private philanthropy is a pillar of a free society or a privately run alternative to democratic choice.
Case for legitimate private choice: People have a right to give their own money as they see fit, and a plural society benefits when thousands of donors fund thousands of different causes, including unpopular ones that a government responding to majorities would never fund. Private philanthropy can take risks, move quickly and experiment in ways public budgets cannot; many public programs began as private experiments. And the alternative is not a perfectly democratic government but one that may be captured, gridlocked or uninterested in foreigners, who cannot vote in it.
Case for undemocratic power: When a handful of the very rich direct billions to their preferred causes, they shape public priorities without being elected or accountable, and they do so with tax subsidies that make every taxpayer a silent co-funder. Anand Giridharadas argues that even a well-run fortune, given wisely, is “still too much power for one person in a democratic society.” The Institute for Policy Studies documents donor-advised funds and foundations accumulating assets faster than they give them away. On this view the question “how should the rich give?” distracts from the better one, how much they should pay in taxes, so that democratically accountable governments can decide.

Question: Should giving aimed at doing the most good prioritize preventing future catastrophes over relieving present suffering?
Tag: value
Poles: Present suffering first / Future risks first
Why contested: Many of the movement’s most engaged members have shifted from global health toward reducing risks of catastrophe, especially from advanced AI and engineered pandemics, on the reasoning that the number of people who could live in the future is vast; in the 2024 community survey, 35 percent rated AI risk a top priority against 30 percent for global health and poverty. Critics inside and outside the movement argue that this replaces solid evidence with speculation. The full argument about the long-term future has its own pages; here it matters because it divides how effective altruists themselves give.
Case for present suffering first: We know with high confidence that malaria medicine and bed nets save children now. Estimates of how much a donation reduces the chance of a far-future catastrophe are close to guesses, and multiplying a tiny, unknowable probability by an enormous number of future people can justify almost anything. Giving that cannot be checked invites self-deception and capture by the interests of the people doing the estimating, and the shift toward AI risk has tied the movement’s reputation to the technology industry.
Case for future risks first: If the same reasoning that favors bed nets over local museums is followed consistently, it leads to the risks that could kill billions or end humanity’s future, which are badly neglected compared with their stakes. Pandemics and powerful technologies are concrete dangers within present lifetimes, not abstractions, and work on them protects people alive today as well as those who come later. A movement that only funded what could be measured with certainty would miss the largest stakes of all.

Question: Does reasoning about how to do the most good make ethical disasters like FTX more likely?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Built-in danger / Individual failing
Why contested: Sam Bankman-Fried, a self-described effective altruist who promoted “earning to give,” was convicted in 2023 of defrauding FTX customers, and his conviction and 25-year sentence were upheld on appeal in June 2026. His foundation had pledged large sums to effective altruist causes. Critics argue that a philosophy which tells people to maximize good outcomes, and which celebrated making a fortune in order to give it away, made the fraud more likely; the movement’s leaders say he betrayed its principles, which explicitly reject breaking moral rules for the greater good.
Case for built-in danger: A philosophy that ranks actions by their expected outcomes, and treats very large numbers as decisive, gives clever people a license to believe that rules do not apply to them when the stakes are high enough. Effective altruism encouraged young people to seek great wealth in order to give it away, concentrated influence in a few rich funders and leaders, and fostered an insular culture that trusted its own. Leif Wenar and others argue that FTX was not an aberration but an expression of these tendencies.
Case for individual failing: Fraud by a donor says no more about the moral views he professed than fraud by a churchgoer says about Christianity. William MacAskill wrote when FTX collapsed in November 2022 that the community had for years emphasized “integrity, honesty, and the respect of common-sense moral constraints” and that “a clear-thinking EA should strongly oppose ‘ends justify the means’ reasoning” (EA Forum, https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/WdeiPrwgqW2wHAxgT/a-personal-statement-on-ftx), and the movement has since tightened its governance and distanced itself from the people involved. Thousands of ordinary pledgers who give a tenth of their income to malaria charities had nothing to do with FTX, and the movement’s main charitable work, which kept growing after 2022, stands or falls on its own evidence.

## Positions

Order note: effective altruism comes first because the whole debate is organized around it; every other camp is defined partly by what it rejects in it. The second camp, giving to the causes and communities one cares about, describes how most donors actually give and is close in weight to the first; an editor could reasonably lead with it. The systemic change and trust-based camps overlap in membership, since many progressive funders hold both, but they answer different questions (what to fund versus who decides) and their adherents sometimes disagree, so they are kept apart. The fifth camp rejects the question as posed. No distinct hybrid camp with its own institutions was found: the closest hybrid, using cost-effectiveness reasoning to fund policy and systemic change, is now a mainstream strand inside effective altruism and is described there.

Camp: Give where the evidence says it does the most good
Adherents: GiveWell, “GiveWell is a nonprofit dedicated to helping people in need as much as we can by researching the most cost-effective ways to save and improve lives,” https://www.givewell.org/about. Peter Singer, philosopher and founder of The Life You Can Save, “The why and how of effective altruism,” TED talk (2013), https://www.ted.com/talks/peter_singer_the_why_and_how_of_effective_altruism. Also: Giving What We Can and its 10% Pledge (https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/pledge); William MacAskill, Oxford philosopher and cofounder of Giving What We Can (https://www.williammacaskill.com/); Coefficient Giving, formerly Open Philanthropy.
Case: Every year millions of children die of diseases that cost a few thousand dollars to prevent, and people in rich countries could stop many of those deaths with money they would barely miss. If you would ruin your shoes to save a child drowning in front of you, distance and nationality cannot be what excuses you from saving one far away. And once you decide to help, how you help matters enormously: charities differ in effectiveness not by a little but by factors of ten or a hundred, so the most important choice a donor makes is where to give, not how much. That means taking evidence seriously, comparing causes as well as charities, giving where an extra dollar does the most good rather than where it feels most natural, and giving a meaningful share of what you earn, with 10 percent as a realistic, public commitment. This is not cold calculation replacing compassion; it is compassion taking its object seriously.
Best evidence: GiveWell estimates that its top charities save a life for $3,000 to $5,500, using programs such as bed nets and seasonal malaria medicine backed by randomized trials (GiveWell). Experts in global health and development estimate that the most effective charities helping the global poor are about 100 times as cost-effective as the average one, while lay donors guess 1.5 times (Caviola and others, 2020). Malaria still kills 610,000 people a year, most of them African children under five (WHO, 2025). GiveWell estimates that the $427 million it directed in its 2025 metrics year will save about 86,000 lives.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that charity treats symptoms while leaving the systems that cause poverty untouched, and that the method’s preference for measurable results blinds it to politics and power. Effective altruists answer that the method is not limited to direct aid: it applies to anything whose expected effects can be reasoned about, and its funders have backed policy change such as lead paint elimination and corporate campaigns that moved large egg producers toward cage-free hens. What it insists on is evidence and honest comparison. Most proposals for systemic change are uncertain, contested, and as likely to fail as succeed, and a child who dies of malaria while donors wait for a better political system is not helped by the wait. Saving lives now and supporting good institutions are compatible; refusing to save lives now because it does not fix everything is not a moral position anyone would accept in front of the drowning child.
Variation inside the camp: The movement splits over cause priorities. A global health wing, closest to GiveWell and Singer, concentrates on proven programs for the world’s poorest people. An animal welfare wing applies the same reasoning to the billions of animals in factory farms. A long-term wing, influential among the most engaged members and funders, gives priority to preventing catastrophes from advanced AI and engineered pandemics; in the 2024 community survey, 35 percent rated AI risk a top priority against 30 percent for global health and poverty (Rethink Priorities). Funders such as Coefficient Giving practice “hits-based” giving, accepting that most bets fail in exchange for a few large successes, which brings them close to the systemic change camp in method. On how much is required, Singer’s principle demands giving until further giving would sacrifice something comparably important, while Giving What We Can asks for a fixed 10 percent.

Camp: Give to the communities and causes you care about
Adherents: Philanthropy Roundtable, which describes its mission as to “protect philanthropic freedom and help donors advance liberty, opportunity and personal responsibility,” https://www.philanthropyroundtable.org/about/. William Schambra, Hudson Institute, “The Coming Showdown Between Philanthrolocalism and Effective Altruism” (22 May 2014), https://www.hudson.org/domestic-policy/the-coming-showdown-between-philanthrolocalism-and-effective-altruism. Also: Ken Berger, then president of the charity rater Charity Navigator, and Robert Penna, “The Elitist Philanthropy of So-Called Effective Altruism,” Stanford Social Innovation Review (25 November 2013), https://ssir.org/articles/entry/the_elitist_philanthropy_of_so_called_effective_altruism.
Case: Giving is not a market for outcomes; it is how free people express love, loyalty and faith and take responsibility for the places they live. Most people give to their congregation, their children’s school, the food bank down the road, the hospital that cared for a parent, and the cause that touched their family, and they are right to. They know these organizations, can see the results and hold them to account, and in giving they build the web of mutual obligation that makes a community and a free society work. Donors should give wisely, checking that a charity is honest and well run, but the choice of cause belongs to the donor’s own values, not to a ranking compiled by experts. A philosophy that tells people their local food bank or their church is a morally questionable diversion of money that should go abroad misunderstands both morality and human nature, and if it were followed it would hollow out the civil society that sustains generosity in the first place.
Best evidence: Of the $617.20 billion Americans gave in 2025, religion received $151.58 billion, the largest share, and international affairs $33.02 billion, about 5 percent (Giving USA 2026): donors’ revealed priorities overwhelmingly favor the institutions they belong to. The share of US households that give at all fell from about two-thirds in 2000 to 46.9 percent in 2020 (Lilly Family School of Philanthropy), alongside the decline of congregations and local associations, which this camp reads as evidence that giving depends on community ties. Fewer than 1 percent of US adults have a clear understanding of effective altruism (Rethink Priorities, 2024), a sign of how far its framework is from the way people actually think about generosity.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a dollar given locally in a rich country buys far less good than a dollar given where children die of malaria, so favoring one’s own community means letting strangers die for lesser benefits to neighbors. This camp answers that moral life is not a single ledger. Obligations to family, neighbors and fellow members are real moral facts, not biases, and a person who gives to the parish food bank is not failing a duty to maximize but fulfilling a duty of membership. Local giving is also better informed and better supervised than distant giving, so the comparison of dollars overstates the difference. Many in this camp also give abroad, especially through religious missions and relief agencies; what they reject is the claim that they must, or that their other giving is wrong.
Variation inside the camp: Conservatives and many religious givers emphasize faith, family and local civil society and defend donor intent and the freedom to give without government direction, the Philanthropy Roundtable’s focus. Mainstream evaluators such as Charity Navigator accept evidence and accountability but apply them within whatever cause a donor chooses, rather than across causes. Some former effective altruists, such as Stephanie Wykstra in Stanford Social Innovation Review (2018), argue for evidence-based giving to local causes such as bail funds, on the grounds that deep injustices close to home are also cheap to address.

Camp: Fund movements and policy that change unjust systems
Adherents: Ford Foundation, “From Generosity to Justice,” https://www.fordfoundation.org/news-and-stories/big-ideas/the-future-of-philanthropy/from-generosity-to-justice/, a framing set out by its longtime president Darren Walker, whose 2019 book of that name defines justice as investing money, time and networks “to change the root causes that create the need for charity in the first place” (as quoted by One Village Partners, https://www.onevillagepartners.org/news/good-trouble). Amia Srinivasan, Oxford philosopher, “Stop the Robot Apocalypse,” London Review of Books (2015), https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v37/n18/amia-srinivasan/stop-the-robot-apocalypse. Also: National Committee for Responsive Philanthropy, which urges foundations to devote at least 25 percent of grants to advocacy, organizing and civic engagement (https://ncrp.org/our-mission-history/); Alice Crary, Carol J. Adams and Lori Gruen, editors of The Good It Promises, the Harm It Does: Critical Essays on Effective Altruism (Oxford University Press, 2023).
Case: Poverty, disease and hunger are not natural disasters; they are produced and reproduced by how economies, governments, trade and tax rules, debt, and racial and gender hierarchies are arranged. Charity that delivers bed nets while leaving those arrangements in place treats symptoms and leaves the next generation needing the same help. The great gains against human misery, from the abolition of slavery to labor rights, public health systems and civil rights, came through organized movements and political change. A serious philanthropy funds the people organizing for those changes, especially those most affected, and accepts that its success will not fit in a spreadsheet. As Srinivasan put it, effective altruism “doesn’t try to understand how power works, except to better align itself with it,” which leaves everything as it is and suits those who benefit from the status quo.
Best evidence: The World Bank attributes most of the fall in extreme poverty, from about 2.3 billion people in 1990 to about 831 million in 2025, to broad-based economic growth in East and South Asia, not to charity (World Bank, 2025). Official aid from rich countries, $174.3 billion in 2025 even after a record cut (OECD), is roughly 87 times what the entire effective giving ecosystem moved that year, and the 2025 aid cuts showed that a political decision in one country can undo more than private donors can replace: IHME estimates health aid fell $10.5 billion in a single year. NCRP’s research holds that foundation investments in advocacy, organizing and civic engagement produce “substantial, tangible benefits.”
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that systemic change is uncertain and slow, that most efforts fail, that donors cannot know whether their money made a difference, and that children die while the system is being reformed. This camp answers that uncertainty is not a reason to fund only what is easy to measure; that bias simply guarantees the deepest causes go unaddressed. The choice is not between saving a child now and changing the system later, since movements for health care, labor rights and debt relief have saved far more lives than any single charity. And the claim that politics is too contested for philanthropy is itself a political choice, one that favors the existing order.
Variation inside the camp: Some, like the Ford Foundation, work as large foundations funding justice organizations; others, closer to Srinivasan and the contributors to The Good It Promises, the Harm It Does, are skeptical of large philanthropy itself and favor grassroots movements, mutual aid and solidarity over donor-led strategy, which brings them near the trust-based and taxation camps. Development economists such as Angus Deaton, in a 2015 Boston Review forum on effective altruism, argued from a different direction that people in rich countries do more for the poor by opposing harmful policies at home, such as arms sales and trade barriers, than by funding programs abroad.

Camp: Trust grantees with unrestricted, long-term money
Adherents: Trust-Based Philanthropy Project, which promotes “six grantmaking practices that, when practiced together, contribute to a more just and equitable nonprofit sector,” beginning with multiyear unrestricted funding, https://www.trustbasedphilanthropy.org/practices. MacKenzie Scott, through Yield Giving, whose site defines yield as “to increase” and “to give up control,” https://yieldgiving.com/.
Case: The people closest to a problem understand it best, and the organizations they build know what their communities need far better than a funder reading reports from a distance. Yet traditional philanthropy, and effective altruism even more so, keeps power with the donor: restricted grants, short funding cycles, reporting demands and metrics that the funder chooses. That power imbalance wastes nonprofits’ time, bends their work toward what funders can count, and starves them of the flexible money that makes organizations strong. Good philanthropy gives large, multiyear, unrestricted grants, does its homework before giving rather than demanding paperwork after, listens to grantees, and then gets out of the way. Trust is not a lack of rigor; it is the recognition that the funder is not the expert.
Best evidence: The Center for Effective Philanthropy’s three-year study of MacKenzie Scott’s gifts found that nearly 90 percent of grantee leaders said the money moderately or significantly strengthened their organizations’ finances, fewer than 10 percent expected a lot of difficulty covering ongoing costs afterward, and recipients built financial reserves that comparable organizations did not (CEP, February 2025). Scott has given $26.3 billion since 2019 in this way, $7.1 billion in 2025 alone. Cash transfers, the purest form of letting recipients decide, produced a local economic multiplier of 2.5 in a large randomized study in Kenya (Egger and others, 2022).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that giving without measuring results is giving blind: some grantees will waste money, and a donor who does not compare options will fund less effective work. This camp answers that it does not abandon evidence; it moves the judgment to the people with the most relevant knowledge and collects learning through relationships and feedback rather than imposed metrics. Funder-chosen metrics are not neutral: they measure what is easy to count and reward organizations that are good at reporting rather than good at the work. Unrestricted money given to strong organizations has a strong record, and the costs of funder control, in wasted time and distorted priorities, are real even if no one tallies them.
Variation inside the camp: Some practitioners, like Scott, give mostly to direct-service and community organizations; others in the trust-based and community-centric fundraising movements tie the practice explicitly to racial and economic justice and to shifting power to communities of color, which links them to the systemic change camp. Mainstream foundations increasingly adopt individual practices, such as multiyear general operating support, without accepting the wider critique of funder power.

Camp: Rein in big philanthropy and fund public needs through taxes
Adherents: Institute for Policy Studies, Charity Reform Initiative, Gilded Giving 2026: Philanthropy Under Oligarchy (15 September 2026), https://ips-dc.org/philanthropy-under-oligarchy/. Anand Giridharadas, author of Winners Take All, interview with the New Statesman (23 January 2019): “Even if you’ve made the money in a clean way, and you’re giving it away in the wisest ways, it’s still too much power for one person in a democratic society,” https://www.newstatesman.com/encounter/2019/01/anand-giridharadas-interview-why-elite-philanthro-capitalists-do-more-harm.
Case: Arguing about how billionaires should give away their money accepts the premise that they should have it to give, and that decisions about global health, education and politics should be made by whoever happens to be richest. In a democracy, public needs should be met by public institutions answerable to voters, funded by taxes that the very rich actually pay. Private philanthropy is subsidized by those taxes, since every deductible gift reduces the donor’s bill, yet the public has no say in where it goes; foundations and donor-advised funds can sit on assets for decades; and philanthropy launders the reputations of fortunes made through low wages, monopoly and tax avoidance. Effective altruism, trust-based giving and justice philanthropy are all improvements on vanity giving, but none of them answers the question of power. The better course is to tax great fortunes, raise required payouts, close the loopholes, and let democratic governments do what private wealth cannot legitimately do.
Best evidence: The federal government forgoes about $70.7 billion a year through deductions for charitable gifts (Joint Committee on Taxation, fiscal 2025), so taxpayers co-fund private giving choices. The Institute for Policy Studies reports that the four largest donor-advised fund sponsors held an average of $6.84 in assets for every $1 they paid out in grants in 2025, and that median payout at large foundations was 5.1 percent in 2024, barely above the legal minimum of 5 percent. The 2025 aid cuts showed the limits of private substitution: official aid fell 23.1 percent in one year, to $174.3 billion (OECD), a drop many times larger than everything the effective giving ecosystem moved in 2025, about $2 billion.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that governments are often slow, captured or indifferent to people outside their borders, and that without private philanthropy many good causes, including unpopular ones and the needs of foreigners who cannot vote, would go unfunded. This camp answers that it does not want to abolish charity, only to end the outsized power of a few enormous fortunes and the public subsidy that comes with it; ordinary giving is untouched. Where governments fall short, the remedy is to make them better and more accountable, not to hand the job to billionaires. And the record of private wealth substituting for public aid is poor: the entire effective giving movement moves a small fraction of what a single government’s aid budget does.
Variation inside the camp: Some, like the Institute for Policy Studies, focus on reforming philanthropy itself, with higher payout requirements for foundations and donor-advised funds and more disclosure, alongside higher taxes on great wealth. Others, like Giridharadas, put the emphasis on taxes and on movements that build democratic power. The political philosopher Rob Reich, in Just Giving (2018), accepts a role for foundations in a democracy but argues they must justify their power and their tax privileges, a reforming rather than rejecting position.

## Crux

Should a gift be judged by how much good it does, counted the same for every person wherever they live, or do relationships, membership and democratic choice properly shape where it goes?

Every camp wants giving to help people; they divide over what makes one gift better than another. Effective altruists hold that the right test is the impartial one: compare options by their expected benefit to whoever is helped, and give where that benefit is largest. The others each put something else alongside or ahead of that test: the giver’s ties to a community, the judgment of the people closest to a problem, the long-run work of changing institutions, or the democratic legitimacy of who decides.

What evidence could settle: how large and how reliable the differences in impact between giving options really are, once hard-to-measure effects are counted. That includes whether the best global health programs are in fact tens of times more cost-effective than typical charities, whether estimates like GiveWell’s hold up as evidence accumulates, whether funding advocacy, organizing and policy change produces more lasting benefit per dollar than direct programs, whether unrestricted, trust-based grants improve outcomes for the people served and not only the finances of the organizations, and whether outside aid strengthens or weakens the institutions of the countries that receive it. If the differences turn out to be modest or unknowable, much of effective altruism’s practical case weakens; if they are as large and robust as its proponents believe, the cost of giving by attachment or by trust alone becomes hard to ignore.

What is a value choice no data can close: whether a stranger’s need makes the same claim on us as a neighbor’s, a child’s or a fellow citizen’s; how much of one’s life morality can demand; whether people in need, or the organizations that represent them, should hold the authority to decide how help is used even when an outsider’s evidence points elsewhere; and whether private fortunes should be making these choices at all rather than democratic governments. Two people who agree on every number can still disagree on these, and the camps’ answers to them are where their deepest commitments lie.
