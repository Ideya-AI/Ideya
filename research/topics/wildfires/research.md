# Living With Wildfire — research

Slug: wildfires
Scope: Living with wildfire: fuels, ignition, and hardening communities.
Primary category: food-land (Food, Land & the Living World)
Researched: 2026-09-21

## Overview

Wildfire is the one natural hazard whose severity people largely manufacture. Three human decisions set the size of the damage: how much vegetation is allowed to accumulate before it burns (fuels), how many fires get started and by what (ignition), and how a house, a yard and a street are built where flames and embers arrive (hardening). This topic covers those three levers and the policy fights over them — prescribed and cultural burning, mechanical thinning and logging on public land, power line and human ignition control, building codes and defensible space, where new housing is allowed, who pays for suppression, and who insures the loss. It deliberately excludes the broader question of how fast to decarbonize, which belongs to net-zero-pace and climate-tipping-points; climate enters here only as a driver of fire weather that the fuels-and-hardening debate has to work around. It also excludes wildfire in the tropics as a deforestation tool, which is a land-use topic rather than a living-with-fire one.

The why-now event is the 2026 North American fire season, the largest in more than a decade in the United States. As of 2026-09-21 the National Interagency Fire Center reported 56,651 wildfires and 8,542,800 acres burned nationally for the calendar year, against a 2015-2024 ten-year average of about 7.48 million acres for a full year (National Interagency Fire Center, https://www.nifc.gov/fire-information/statistics). Oregon shattered its all-time record, with 2,533,782 acres burned across 1,667 fires as of 2026-08-26 (Wikipedia, 2026 Oregon wildfires, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Oregon_wildfires) and state suppression costs of $236.2 million as of 2026-08-20, already the highest on record (OPB, 2026-09-01, https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/01/oregon-wildfire-record-costs-2026/). The National Preparedness Level sat at 5, its maximum, through most of August. Running alongside the season is the most consequential hardening rule any US state has written: on 2026-08-19 the California Board of Forestry and Fire Protection adopted emergency regulations creating an ember-resistant zone within five feet of qualifying structures, the long-delayed “Zone 0” required by AB 3074 of 2020 (PublicCEO, 2026-09, https://www.publicceo.com/2026/09/california-board-of-forestry-adopts-emergency-zone-0-regulations-under-ab-3074/).

The debate is live because the losses have outgrown the institutions built to absorb them. The January 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires killed 31 people, destroyed more than 16,000 structures and produced roughly $40 billion in insured losses, the costliest wildfire event ever recorded (Wikipedia, January 2025 Southern California wildfires, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_2025_Southern_California_wildfires). California’s insurer of last resort has grown 157 percent in under four years. The federal government is in the middle of reorganizing its wildland fire agencies into a single US Wildland Fire Service under Executive Order 14308, while a bipartisan Fix Our Forests Act moves through the Senate. The people in it are not lined up as one might expect: fire ecologists who want much more fire on the ground, foresters and timber interests who want faster thinning, building scientists who argue the whole problem is really a housing-construction problem, insurers and land-use planners who want the price of the risk to reach the people taking it, firefighter organizations who want more suppression capacity, and climate scientists who say the fire weather is outrunning all of it. Most of these groups agree on many specifics and split hard on what to do first with limited money.

Source label: Knapp, Valachovic, Quarles and Johnson, Fire Ecology (2021)

Note: In the fire that destroyed most of Paradise, California, 11.5 percent of homes built before 1997 survived, against 44 percent of those built from 2008 on.
Context: This is the strongest single piece of evidence that construction standards work — and also the strongest evidence for their limits, since more than half of the newest, best-built homes still burned. The authors found the difference between homes built just before and just after the 2008 adoption of Chapter 7A (37 percent versus 44 percent) was not statistically significant, meaning the gain came from a long run of incremental code improvements rather than one rule. The dominant predictors of survival were not code year at all: distance to the nearest destroyed structure, the number of structures destroyed within 100 meters, and pre-fire overstory canopy cover. Homes more than 18 meters from a destroyed structure, with less than 53 percent canopy cover 30-100 meters out, survived at a much higher rate — but 73.6 percent of Paradise homes were within 18 meters of another structure. Most damage to surviving homes came from radiant heat off neighboring burning buildings or from near-home combustibles igniting, not from the wildland fire front.

Data year: 2025 (IBHS release, 2025-08-26); ember share from IBHS research
Source label: Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety

Note: Clearing combustible material from the first five feet around a house roughly halves its chance of igniting in a wildfire.
Context: This finding is what California’s Zone 0 regulation, adopted 2026-08-19, is built on — no bark mulch, no woody shrubs, no stored firewood, no combustible fencing attached to the structure, within five feet of the wall. The counterargument is aesthetic and cultural rather than technical, and the enforcement problem is real: a hardened house next to an unhardened one is still exposed to its neighbor’s radiant heat, which is why Zone 0 is written as a rule for everyone in a hazard zone rather than a suggestion.

Source URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S037811272400197X
Note: Treatments that remove surface fuels cut later wildfire severity by about two thirds, and the effect decays by roughly half over ten years.

The study covers conifer-dominated forests of the western US and does not transfer automatically to chaparral, sagebrush or the southeastern pine belt.

The Camp, Thomas, Kincade, Dixie and Eaton fires were all attributed to or are alleged against utility equipment. The response has three tools — undergrounding, which costs on the order of millions of dollars per mile; fast-trip settings and covered conductor; and public safety power shutoffs, which trade fire risk for the risks of cutting power to people on medical equipment. Liability is the other lever: PG&E’s bankruptcy followed the Camp Fire, and Southern California Edison had offered more than $750 million through its Eaton Fire compensation program as of 2026-07-16 (https://newsroom.edison.com/releases/southern-california-edison-exceeds-750-million-in-relief-offered-for-eaton-fire-impacts).

## Questions

Question: With a fixed budget, does the next dollar prevent more loss spent on the house or on the landscape?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Harden the house / Treat the land
Why contested: Both interventions have measured effects, but almost no study puts them on a common scale of dollars per structure saved, and the two work at different distances from the flame. Post-fire forensics from Paradise, Santa Rosa and Altadena keep finding houses lost while nearby trees survived, which points at the structure; landscape modeling keeps finding that treated fuels slow fires and buy suppression time, which points at the land. The agencies with the money — federal land managers — control the landscape and not the houses, and the people who control the houses are millions of private owners, so the institutional path of least resistance does not match either answer cleanly.
Case for harden the house: The evidence that a house’s fate is decided within roughly 30 meters of its walls is unusually direct. Jack Cohen’s experimental and forensic work defines the wildland-urban fire as a structure ignition problem rather than a wildfire control problem, and finds no reliable link between wildfire intensity and whether a community burns. Up to 90 percent of building ignitions trace to embers, which travel kilometers ahead of a front and defeat any fuel break. In Paradise, distance to the nearest destroyed structure and near-home vegetation beat every other variable, and 73.6 percent of homes were within 18 meters of a neighbor — meaning the fire that destroyed the town was largely a building-to-building fire once it arrived. Hardening is also cheap for new construction, at 2 to 3 percent of build cost, and the first five feet cost nothing but effort, halving ignition risk. Landscape treatment, by contrast, must be maintained forever, decays by half in a decade, and has to be in the right place at the right time to matter at all.
Case for treat the land: A hardened house in a landscape primed for megafire is still a house in a burning town without power, water, escape routes, hospital access or an insurable future, and hardening does nothing for watersheds, air quality, carbon or the forest itself. The meta-analytic evidence for fuel treatment is strong and quantitative: 62 to 72 percent reductions in subsequent severity, holding across forest types and across a range of fire weather. Treated landscapes also change what suppression can do, giving crews anchor points and making evacuation feasible; they protect the infrastructure that a hardened house depends on. And the fuel is the part of the system that can actually be changed at scale by public action, whereas retrofitting tens of millions of private homes has no delivery mechanism, no funding stream and no enforcement.

Question: Should people be stopped from building, or rebuilding, in the highest-hazard places?
Tag: value
Poles: Restrict and retreat / Build, but build right
Why contested: Thirty-two percent of US housing is already in the wildland-urban interface, and the fastest growth in fire-prone counties is in the places with the worst housing shortages. Restricting development trades wildfire risk against housing supply, property rights and local tax bases; it also runs into the fact that most of the exposure is already built. After a fire, the question becomes sharper and more personal: whether a community that just burned should be rebuilt where it stood.
Case for restrict and retreat: Every new house in a high-hazard area is a permanent obligation on firefighters who must defend it, on neighbors whose risk it raises through structure-to-structure spread, and on insurance pools that spread the loss to people who never chose it. The exposure, not the fire, is what turned Los Angeles weather into a $65 billion disaster. Land-use tools — zoning overlays, subdivision standards, hazard disclosure, transfer of development rights, refusing to extend infrastructure — are ordinary planning instruments used against floodplains for decades. Post-fire is the one moment when rebuilding somewhere safer is physically possible, and letting it pass locks the hazard in for another century.
Case for build, but build right: Telling Americans where they may not live is politically dead and morally contested, and it lands hardest on people priced out of safer places; wildfire zoning would become one more exclusionary tool in states that already cannot build enough housing. The empirical case for building right is strong: post-1997 homes in Paradise survived at three to four times the rate of older ones, at a marginal cost of 2 to 3 percent. If a house can be built to survive, banning the house is a worse instrument than requiring the standard. Managed retreat also has almost no successful precedent at community scale in the United States, and where it has been attempted it has been slow, expensive and bitterly resented.

Question: Can prescribed and cultural burning actually be scaled to the size of the fire deficit?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Scalable now / A hard ceiling
Why contested: The gap between stated targets and delivered acres is enormous and has not closed. California committed to a million treated acres a year by 2025 and delivered 188,881 acres of beneficial fire in 2024. Forest Service hazardous fuels treatment fell 35 percent in 2025. Whether that gap reflects a fixable shortage of will and staff, or a real physical and legal ceiling, decides whether the fuels strategy is a plan or a slogan.
Case for scalable now: The Southeast already does it. Roughly nine million acres a year are burned deliberately in the United States, the large majority on state and private land in the southeastern pine belt, under looser liability rules and with a culture of routine burning. Nothing physical prevents the West from doing the same: the constraints are burn windows that agencies decline to use, liability exposure that six states have already addressed by adopting gross-negligence standards, air permits that treat planned smoke worse than unplanned smoke, and a workforce hired for suppression. California’s SB 310 and the Karuk Tribe’s cultural burning agreement of 2025-09-11 show the legal barriers can be dismantled. And the safety record is the argument’s floor: 99.84 percent of burns go as planned.
Case for a hard ceiling: The burn window in the interior West is genuinely narrow and getting narrower — the season when fuels are dry enough to carry fire and wet enough not to escape is shrinking on both ends as fire season lengthens. Prescribed fire requires crews who are simultaneously in demand for suppression, and the same weather that makes a burn possible makes fires elsewhere. Smoke from planned burns lands on communities that vote, under air quality rules with no exemption. Liability is asymmetric in a way statistics do not fix: Hermit’s Peak/Calf Canyon cost $5.45 billion in federal compensation and no line officer wants to be the next one. Scaling from 189,000 to a million acres a year in California requires a workforce that does not exist and cannot be recruited quickly.

Question: How much of the rise in destructive fire is climate, how much is fuel accumulation, and how much is exposure?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Climate-driven / Land- and building-driven
Why contested: All three are rising at once and the attribution methods for each are different. Getting the shares wrong sends money to the wrong lever: if it is mostly climate, fuels work is a treadmill; if it is mostly fuels and exposure, emissions cuts will not arrive in time to help.
Case for climate-driven: Anthropogenic warming is estimated to have doubled cumulative western US forest fire area between 1984 and 2015 and to account for more than half of the increase in fuel aridity since the 1970s. The January 2025 Los Angeles fire weather was about 35 percent more likely at today’s warming, and dry Southern California winters are now 2.4 times more likely than pre-industrial. Globally, the most extreme fire events roughly doubled in frequency between 2003 and 2023, concentrated in temperate conifer and boreal forests that have not had a century of suppression in the American sense — Canada’s 2023 season burned six times its long-run average. Fuel-deficit arguments cannot explain boreal Canada or Siberia.
Case for land- and building-driven: Global burned area has fallen about a quarter since the late 1990s even as the climate warmed, because land use, not climate, dominates where fire can carry. The catastrophic losses of the last decade are overwhelmingly in the wildland-urban interface, whose housing grew 47 percent from 1990 to 2020 — the damage function is exposure, not just hazard. The pre-1800 California baseline shows that the amount of fire the modern landscape “should” carry dwarfs even record modern years, which implies most of the western forest fuel problem was created by suppression rather than by warming. And the attribution scientists themselves said, of the Los Angeles fires, that how much had been built where mattered at least as much as the climate signal.

Question: Is aggressive suppression of nearly every fire still the right default?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Put them all out / Let more burn
Why contested: Suppression works — CAL FIRE catches about 95 percent of fires at ten acres or less — and its success is exactly what builds the fuel load that makes the escaped 5 percent catastrophic. Every fire allowed to burn for resource benefit is a fire that might not stay benign, and the decision is made under uncertainty by an officer who bears the downside personally.
Case for put them all out: Fires that escape initial attack are the ones that kill people and destroy towns, and the cheapest fire to stop is a small one. The 2026 season, with national resources fully committed at Preparedness Level 5 for weeks, shows what happens when capacity runs out rather than when policy chooses restraint. Smoke from a managed fire is a real health cost imposed on people who did not consent. With more than 44 million homes in the WUI, the geography of “let it burn” has shrunk: there are fewer and fewer fires far enough from people to manage safely, and a wind event can move a managed fire into a community in hours.
Case for let more burn: A century of suppressing 95 percent of ignitions is precisely how the fuel arrived. The only mechanism that has ever kept western forests at a survivable fuel load is frequent fire, and lightning fires in remote terrain under moderate conditions are free prescribed burns the agency does not have to light, permit or pay for. Managed wildfire is the only tool that operates at the scale of the deficit — it treats hundreds of thousands of acres in a season, which no crew program can match. Suppression also consumes the budget that prevention needs: federal suppression alone averaged $2.52 billion a year over a decade, and UNEP’s recommendation of two thirds on prevention is close to the inverse of what governments do.

Question: Should wildfire insurance be priced at the full modeled risk, even where that makes homes uninsurable?
Tag: value
Poles: Full risk pricing / Shared pooling
Why contested: Price is the most powerful signal available for steering where people build and whether they harden, and it is also the mechanism by which an ordinary family loses its home’s value and its mortgage. California’s FAIR Plan has grown 157 percent in under four years because private carriers left; the question is whether that growth is a market correcting or a market failing.
Case for full risk pricing: Suppressed rates are a subsidy paid by people in safer places to people in riskier ones, and they suppress the single strongest incentive to harden or to build elsewhere. When the FAIR Plan absorbs the risk, assessments can be passed through statewide — socializing a hazard that individual choices created. Accurate prices also make mitigation pay: if hardening and defensible space visibly cut a premium, millions of private decisions move at once, without any regulator having to inspect a yard. A risk pool that cannot charge for its risk eventually cannot pay its claims.
Case for shared pooling: Insurance is a mechanism for spreading exactly the risks individuals cannot bear, and wildfire hazard is heavily inherited — most people in the WUI bought a legally built house under rules that permitted it, often decades ago. Full risk pricing does not relocate anyone; it strands them, destroying equity for people who cannot sell and cannot afford to leave, and concentrating the loss on the old and the poor. Modeled risk is also not observed risk: catastrophe models are proprietary, unaudited and disagree with each other, and letting them set prices hands enormous power to unaccountable vendors. Pooling buys the time in which hardening and land-use change can actually happen.

Question: Does mechanical thinning reduce fire risk on its own, or only when followed by burning?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Thinning works / Surface fuels decide
Why contested: Thinning is the politically easiest treatment — it produces jobs and merchantable timber, needs no burn window, and creates no smoke — and it is also the treatment the evidence supports least on its own, because cutting trees leaves slash on the ground unless it is burned or removed.
Case for thinning works: Thinning changes crown continuity and canopy bulk density, which is what turns a surface fire into a crown fire, and it is the only treatment that can be applied in places where burning will never be permitted: next to towns, under smoke-sensitive airsheds, on steep slopes near infrastructure. It generates revenue and a workforce, which makes it repeatable where prescribed fire depends on annual appropriations. And it is the necessary first step almost everywhere in the dense, fire-excluded West — burning a stand with a century of ingrowth without thinning it first risks killing the overstory.
Case for surface fuels decide: The 2024 meta-analysis of 220 studies is explicit: thinning alone was markedly less effective than thinning plus prescribed or pile burning, and only thinning coupled with prescribed fire still delivered a significant reduction — 49 percent — after ten years. Fire spreads through surface fuel; leaving slash can raise short-term fireline intensity. Treating thinning as a complete answer confuses the measurable output the agency reports (acres entered, board feet sold) with the outcome anyone cares about (severity when fire arrives), and creates an incentive to log where it is profitable rather than where it protects people.

Question: Is smoke from planned burning an acceptable trade for less smoke from wildfire?
Tag: value
Poles: Accept planned smoke / Protect air now
Why contested: Wildfire smoke has already slowed or reversed the decline of fine-particle pollution in 35 states and is associated with roughly 11,400 US deaths a year. Prescribed fire reduces future wildfire smoke but produces smoke now, on a date a public agency chose, over communities that can identify who to blame.
Case for accept planned smoke: Planned burns are lit in conditions selected for dispersion, at lower intensity, in cooler and wetter seasons, and can be scheduled around schools, hospitals and vulnerable neighborhoods; wildfire smoke arrives in August at peak heat with no notice and lasts for weeks. The comparison is not smoke versus clean air, it is a chosen dose now against an unchosen and larger dose later. Regulatory treatment of the two is backwards: wildfire smoke is written off as an exceptional event while prescribed smoke counts against a permit, which penalizes the only action that reduces the total.
Case for protect air now: The health damage from particulate matter is real today and falls hardest on children, the elderly, outdoor workers and people with asthma, who cannot consent to a trade made on their behalf. The claim that today’s burn prevents tomorrow’s wildfire is probabilistic and site-specific — the treated acre may never see a fire, in which case the smoke was pure cost. Air quality standards exist because voluntary trades of this kind were historically made badly and by the people least affected. And prescribed smoke is repeated every few years forever, while the wildfire it is meant to prevent may be decades away or never.

Question: Who should bear the cost when a utility’s equipment starts a fire?
Tag: value
Poles: The utility and its investors / A socialized fund
Why contested: Power line ignitions rose roughly thirteenfold since the early 1990s and account for roughly half of California’s most destructive fires. Full liability can bankrupt a utility, as it did PG&E; capped liability moves the cost onto ratepayers or taxpayers and weakens the incentive to maintain lines.
Case for the utility and its investors: Utilities are monopolies with guaranteed returns, and the fires trace to deferred maintenance decisions their managers made and their shareholders benefited from. Uncapped liability is what produced the fast-trip settings, covered conductor, vegetation programs and undergrounding that followed the Camp Fire; removing it removes the only force that has ever moved these companies quickly. Victims of a corporate failure should be made whole by the corporation, not by a fund the victims themselves pay into through their bills.
Case for a socialized fund: A utility driven into bankruptcy pays victims slowly, in litigation, at a discount, while its capital costs rise and its customers fund the higher interest for decades — the Camp Fire showed all of this. A standing fund, like California’s wildfire fund, pays faster and more predictably, and keeps the utility solvent enough to do the hardening work that prevents the next fire. Utilities also cannot control the weather, the fuels beside their right-of-way, or the decision to build a town at the end of a distribution line; making them the insurer of last resort for other people’s land-use choices misassigns the cost.

## Positions

Camp: Return fire to the land through prescribed and cultural burning
Adherents:
  The Nature Conservancy (institution) — has used prescribed fire for more than 60 years, states that fire is needed on about 80 percent of the US land it works to conserve, and created the Prescribed Fire Training Exchange in 2008 — https://www.nature.org/en-us/what-we-do/our-priorities/protect-water-and-land/land-and-water-stories/why-we-work-with-fire/
  Scott Stephens, professor of fire science, University of California, Berkeley — argues for a large increase in prescribed fire in California forests and co-authored the estimate that about 1.8 million hectares burned annually in pre-1800 California — https://antr.assembly.ca.gov/sites/antr.assembly.ca.gov/files/Scott%20Stephens-UC%20Berkeley%20-%20Prescribed%20Fire%20in%20CA%20Forests%20Presentation.pdf
  Karuk Tribe (institution) — entered California’s first cultural burning agreement under SB 310 on 2025-09-11, allowing cultural burns on ancestral land without state permits — https://www.karuk.us/posts/california-partners-with-karuk-tribe-on-groundbreaking-cultural-burning-agreement
The case in its own voice: Western landscapes are not overgrown by accident. They are the product of a deliberate century-long project to exclude fire from ecosystems that were shaped by burning every few years, much of it set by people who lived there. California burned roughly four and a half million acres in an ordinary year before 1800 and now burns a fraction of that deliberately, so the fuel simply accumulates until weather decides when it goes. The question was never whether these lands will burn; it is whether they burn on a cool morning in March under a crew’s control, or in September at forty miles an hour. Prescribed and cultural fire is the only treatment that works at the scale of the problem, reaches the surface fuels that actually carry fire, and pays for itself in avoided suppression, avoided smoke and a forest that survives. Lifting the legal and cultural barriers — liability, permits, air rules, and the exclusion of Indigenous practitioners — is the fastest available move.
Best evidence: The 2024 Tamm Review meta-analysis of 220 studies found prescribed burning and thinning-plus-burning reduced subsequent wildfire severity by 62 to 72 percent, and that thinning plus prescribed fire was the only treatment still delivering a significant reduction, 49 percent, after ten years. The Forest Service’s own program review found 99.84 percent of prescribed burns go according to plan. The Southeast already burns roughly eight million acres a year on state and private land without catastrophe, demonstrating that the constraint is institutional rather than physical.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is Hermit’s Peak/Calf Canyon — that one escape can cost billions and destroy a community’s trust. The camp’s answer is that the comparison is against the wrong baseline. The relevant counterfactual is not zero fire; it is the wildfire that burns the same ground later under worse conditions, and by that measure the escape rate is a rounding error against the losses avoided. The fix is better burn planning, a trained year-round burn workforce, state claims funds like California’s, and liability standards that do not make a line officer personally ruinable for an outcome inside the prescription — not fewer burns.

Camp: Harden the houses and the ground within five feet of them
Adherents:
  Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (institution) — runs the Wildfire Prepared Home standard and found that a noncombustible five-foot buffer roughly halves a structure’s ignition risk — https://ibhs.org/wildfire/wildfire-prepared-home/
  Jack Cohen, retired research physical scientist, USDA Forest Service Missoula Fire Sciences Laboratory — originated the home ignition zone concept and argues the wildland-urban fire disaster is a structure ignition problem, not a wildfire control problem — https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2024R1/Downloads/PublicTestimonyDocument/111465
  Headwaters Economics (institution) — publishes the construction-cost work showing wildfire-resistant building adds 2 to 3 percent to a new home — https://headwaterseconomics.org/natural-hazards/wildfire/construction-costs-for-wildfire-resistant-homes/
The case in its own voice: Communities do not burn because wildfires are unstoppable; they burn because houses are built to catch embers and then set each other alight. A firebrand that lands in a gutter full of pine needles, under a wooden deck, or in bark mulch against a wall does not care how intense the fire front was two miles away — and the forensic record shows unburned green vegetation standing next to slabs where houses used to be. The fate of a house is decided within about thirty meters of its walls, and above all within the first five feet, which is the cheapest intervention in the entire field and requires no permit, no contractor and no appropriation. Treating this as a forest problem sends billions to landscapes that may never see fire while leaving the actual point of failure untouched. Fix the roof, the vents, the windows, the deck and the first five feet, block structure-to-structure spread, and the wildfire can pass through.
Best evidence: In the Camp Fire, 11.5 percent of homes built before 1997 survived against 44 percent of those built from 2008 on, and the strongest predictors of survival were distance to the nearest destroyed structure and near-home vegetation, not fire behavior in the wildland. Up to 90 percent of building ignitions are attributed to ember exposure. A noncombustible five-foot buffer cuts a structure’s ignition risk roughly in half, and building a new home to a high wildfire-resistant standard costs 2 to 3 percent more.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that a hardened house in a burned-over town is still uninhabitable, with no power, water, schools, insurance or neighbors. The camp accepts this and answers that it is an argument for hardening at community scale rather than for abandoning hardening: parcel-by-parcel work fails precisely because 73.6 percent of Paradise homes were within eighteen meters of a neighbor, so the standard has to be a rule for everyone in the hazard zone, paired with hardened infrastructure and evacuation routes. What it will not concede is the premise that landscape fuel work can substitute; no achievable amount of treatment stops ember cast in a wind event.

Camp: Thin and harvest federal forests faster, with less process
Adherents:
  American Forest Resource Council (institution) — advocates sustained-yield harvest on public timberland to reduce fire risk and names litigation as the greatest risk to the Forest Service’s ability to do management at scale — https://amforest.org/solutions/
  Senator John Curtis, with Senators Padilla, Hickenlooper and Sheehy — advanced the Senate Fix Our Forests Act through committee 18-5 on 2025-10-21, with fireshed designations, categorical exclusions and litigation and injunction reforms — https://www.curtis.senate.gov/press-releases/senate-advances-fix-our-forests-act-marking-key-progress-to-combat-wildfires/
The case in its own voice: Federal forests carry several times the tree density they evolved with, and the binding constraint on fixing that is not science or money but process. The Forest Service is the most-litigated agency in the federal government; a thinning project that would take a season to execute takes five to ten years to clear analysis and appeal, by which time the stand has burned. Prescribed fire is not available at scale in dense, fire-excluded stands anyway — burning a century of ingrowth without first removing it kills the overstory — so mechanical treatment is the necessary first entry across most of the interior West. Harvest also pays for itself, sustains mills and a rural workforce that a grant-funded program cannot, and can be done next to towns, under smoke-sensitive airsheds and on days when no burn window exists. Give the agency categorical exclusions, fireshed-scale authorities and limits on serial litigation, and treated acres will rise.
Best evidence: Forest Service hazardous-fuels treatment fell 35 percent in a single year, from 4.1 million acres in 2024 to 2.6 million in 2025, against a Wildfire Crisis Strategy target of up to 50 million acres over a decade — evidence that delivery, not strategy, is the failure point. The Tamm Review found thinning combined with burning to be the most durable treatment of all, still cutting severity 49 percent at ten years, which makes mechanical entry a prerequisite rather than an alternative. Fix Our Forests advanced with broad bipartisan support, including Democratic senators from California and Colorado.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that thinning alone is weakly supported and that “forest health” is a cover for commercial logging of large, fire-resistant trees. The camp’s answer is that it does not claim thinning alone is sufficient — it claims thinning is the required first step and that the follow-up burn is blocked by the same process problems. On large trees, most mainstream advocates now accept diameter guidance and old-growth protection in exchange for speed; the Senate bill’s supporters point to that trade as the reason conservation-state Democrats signed on. The camp’s deeper answer is that the alternative to imperfect management is not pristine forest but high-severity fire that removes the old trees permanently.

Camp: Build out suppression, detection and aviation capacity
Adherents:
  US Department of the Interior and US Department of Agriculture (institution) — implementing Executive Order 14308 of 2025-06-12 by consolidating federal wildland fire programs into a new US Wildland Fire Service, with aviation modernization and faster response as stated goals — https://www.doi.gov/pressreleases/departments-interior-and-agriculture-announce-wildland-fire-service-plan-modernize
  The White House, Executive Order 14308, “Empowering Commonsense Wildfire Prevention and Response” — directs consolidation of federal firefighting and improved technology, data and response — https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/06/empowering-commonsense-wildfire-prevention-and-response/
The case in its own voice: Almost every fire that matters was small once. The reason most ignitions never become news is an initial-attack system that gets engines, crews and aircraft on scene within minutes, and in California that system keeps about 95 percent of fires to ten acres or less. The catastrophic 5 percent are the ones that outran it — because resources were committed elsewhere, because detection was slow, or because the aircraft were the wrong ones in the wrong place. In 2026 the country ran at maximum national preparedness for weeks with federal resources fully committed and new fires competing for what was left. That is a capacity problem, and it is solvable with money: more crews on permanent rather than seasonal terms, better pay so the workforce stops hemorrhaging, satellite and camera detection that shortens time-to-report to minutes, night-flying helicopters, and a single agency instead of five with different pay scales and radios.
Best evidence: CAL FIRE’s initial-attack standard — aircraft to the most remote parts of the state within 20 minutes, with the goal of holding 95 percent of fires to ten acres or less — describes a system that already prevents the overwhelming majority of potential disasters. The 2026 season’s sustained Preparedness Level 5, with roughly 63 percent of national firefighting resources committed to Oregon and Washington at one point, shows what the shortfall looks like. Federal suppression spending, averaging $2.52 billion a year over a decade, is small against the $65 billion economic loss of a single January in Los Angeles.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is the fire paradox: suppressing everything is what built the fuel load. The camp’s answer is that this is an argument about where and when to suppress, not about whether to have the capacity — the choice to manage a remote lightning fire for resource benefit is only available to an agency that could have put it out, and restraint without capacity is just helplessness. It also argues the paradox is largely a forest story and does not transfer to chaparral, grassland or the WUI, where more fire is not ecologically needed and the only question is how fast it can be stopped.

Camp: Cut the emissions driving the fire weather
Adherents:
  Union of Concerned Scientists (institution) — reports that wildfires burned more than twice as much land per year in 2000-2018 as in 1985-1999, and calls for sharply curtailing global warming emissions alongside better forest management and limits on development in fire-prone areas (page updated 2020-03-11) — https://www.ucs.org/resources/climate-change-and-wildfires
  World Weather Attribution (institution, Imperial College London and partners) — found the weather behind the January 2025 Los Angeles fires about 35 percent more likely and 6 percent more intense at 1.3 degrees Celsius of warming — https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/climate-change-increased-the-likelihood-of-wildfire-disaster-in-highly-exposed-los-angeles-area/
The case in its own voice: Fuels management and home hardening are adaptations to a hazard that is still growing, and adaptation to a moving target eventually loses. Warming has already roughly doubled the area of western US forest that burns, chiefly by drying fuels, and it lengthens the season on both ends — which simultaneously increases the number of days fires can spread and reduces the days on which prescribed burning is safe. Canada’s 2023 season, six times its long-run average, happened in a boreal forest with no century of American-style suppression to blame, and the most extreme fire events on Earth roughly doubled in frequency in twenty years. Every additional tenth of a degree raises the ceiling that the fuels and hardening strategies have to work under, and none of those strategies reduce the hazard itself. Treating wildfire purely as a land-management problem accepts an indefinitely worsening baseline.
Best evidence: Abatzoglou and Williams attributed more than half the increase in western US fuel aridity since the 1970s, and a doubling of cumulative forest fire area from 1984 to 2015, to human-caused warming. Attribution analysis put January 2025 Los Angeles fire weather at about 35 percent more likely, with dry Southern California winters now 2.4 times more likely than pre-industrial. UNEP projects up to 30 percent more extreme fires by 2050 and 50 percent by 2100 on current trajectories.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is timing — emissions cuts affect the 2060s, not this fire season, and global burned area has actually fallen while the climate warmed. The camp answers that the decline in global burned area is in savannas being converted to farmland and says nothing about the forest and boreal fires that produce the damage, and that the point of mitigation was never to be a substitute for this year’s fuel break. It is what determines whether adaptation is a finite project with an end state or a permanent, escalating tax. The camp does not oppose fuels work or hardening; it denies that they can be the whole answer.

Camp: Steer building away from the worst hazard and let price reflect risk
Adherents:
  Resources for the Future (institution) — its report on land use in the wildland-urban interface sets out how state and local governments can use zoning overlays, subdivision regulation and transfer of development rights to reduce exposure — https://www.rff.org/publications/reports/shaping-land-use-patterns-in-the-wildland-urban-interface-the-role-of-state-and-local-governments-in-reducing-exposure-to-wildfire-risks/
  Dave Jones, director of the Climate Risk Initiative at UC Berkeley and California insurance commissioner 2011-2018 — “There is no reason why home insurers cannot take home hardening, defensible space, and forest management into account when writing or renewing home insurance and pricing it. They can and should.” — https://www.insuranceforgood.org/blog/q-and-a-dave-jones
  Headwaters Economics (institution) — finds that insurance alone is not a strong enough market force to stop home development in the fire-prone WUI, and that land-use regulation is needed alongside it — https://headwaterseconomics.org/natural-hazards/wildfire/solutions/insurance-wildfire-home-development/
The case in its own voice: The hazard has been roughly constant in kind for a century; what changed is that 14 million more homes were put in its way. Thirty-two percent of American housing now sits in the wildland-urban interface, and the single reliable predictor of a billion-dollar fire is not fuel load or wind speed but how much had been built in the path. Nothing in the fuels or hardening strategies stops the next subdivision from being approved at the end of a single-egress road in a chaparral canyon. The tools are ordinary planning tools already used for floodplains: hazard mapping with teeth, subdivision standards, second-access requirements, disclosure at sale, refusing to extend water and power, and above all prices that tell people the truth. When the insurer of last resort grows 157 percent in four years and its assessments can be passed to policyholders statewide, the risk is being quietly socialized and the signal that would change behavior is being suppressed.
Best evidence: WUI housing grew 47 percent between 1990 and 2020, with 97 percent of new WUI created by new houses rather than encroaching vegetation. The California FAIR Plan carried 696,562 policies and $768 billion of exposure as of June 2026, up 250 percent in exposure since September 2022. Attribution scientists examining the Los Angeles fires concluded that exposure mattered at least as much as the climate signal in producing the disaster.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that this strands ordinary people — telling families they may not build, or pricing them out of houses they already own, in states that cannot build enough housing anywhere. The camp splits its answer. On new development it holds the line: a house approved today is a ninety-year obligation, and there is no equity case for creating new exposure. On existing homes it does not favor abandonment; it favors mitigation-linked pricing so that hardening visibly lowers a premium, transparent and auditable catastrophe models rather than proprietary black boxes, public funding for retrofits in low-income households, and a FAIR Plan priced to its risk with subsidy made explicit rather than hidden in cross-subsidies. Its core claim is that a risk nobody can see the price of is a risk that keeps growing.

Cross-camp notes:
  Hybrid position that is widely held in practice: “treat the first hundred feet and the first hundred meters” — combine mandatory home hardening and Zone 0 with fuel treatment in a narrow band around communities, and leave the backcountry to managed wildfire. Both the home-hardening camp and the prescribed-fire camp can accept it; it is the operating assumption behind Firewise USA, Community Wildfire Protection Plans and most Community Wildfire Defense Grants.
  Camp that rejects the question as posed: the home-hardening camp does this most explicitly. Jack Cohen’s framing is that “how do we control wildfire” is the wrong question, because community destruction is not determined by wildfire intensity at all; on that view the fuels debate and the suppression debate are both arguments about a variable that does not control the outcome people care about.
  A distinction worth preserving: the prescribed-fire camp and the thinning camp are often lumped together as “active management,” but they disagree on a testable proposition — whether mechanical treatment without follow-up burning reduces later fire severity. They also disagree on who does the work and who profits from it.

## Crux

The crux: when a community burns, was the outcome decided by the fire or by the community?

Every camp on this page is, underneath, answering that one question. The prescribed-fire, thinning, suppression and emissions camps all answer “the fire” — they differ only on which lever changes it, and all four would spend the next dollar making the fire smaller, cooler, later or easier to stop. The hardening and land-use camps answer “the community” — they would spend the same dollar on the roof, the vents, the five feet of ground against the wall, the spacing between houses, the second road out, and the decision about whether that subdivision is approved at all. This is the question that moves money, because the two answers point at different agencies, different budgets and different people with the obligation to act. Settle it and the rest of the argument reorganizes itself; leave it unsettled and every wildfire season produces a round of funding that splits the difference and does neither at scale.

What evidence could settle: A great deal, and much of it has not been collected. The empirical version of the question is a marginal one: per dollar and per year, how much structure loss does a hardening program avert compared with a landscape fuel program in the same place? The pieces exist separately — the Tamm Review’s 62 to 72 percent severity reductions on treated acres, IBHS’s halving of ignition risk from a five-foot buffer, the Camp Fire survival gradient by construction era, the 18-meter structure-separation threshold — but nobody has put them on one axis, because the two literatures use different outcome measures (fire severity versus structure survival) and different units (acres versus houses). Post-fire damage inspections now produce enough standardized data that this is tractable: the January 2025 Los Angeles fires, the 2026 Oregon season and every major event since about 2017 generate parcel-level records of what was built how, what was cleared, what the fire did, and what survived. A well-designed synthesis of those events could in principle establish whether fuel condition at the community edge or building condition at the parcel explains more of the variance in structure loss, and at what cost each unit of protection was bought. Attribution science can also narrow the climate share: how much of the change in fire weather is warming versus variability is a question with a shrinking error bar.

What evidence cannot settle: What the policy is for. If the goal is only to stop houses from burning, the hardening answer is likely to win on cost-effectiveness for a long time. But landscape fire work delivers things hardening cannot buy at any price — forests that persist rather than convert to brush, watersheds that do not fill reservoirs with ash, air that is breathable in August, carbon that stays in trees, habitat, and the continuation of Indigenous practices whose value is not measured in structures at all. Whether those count as benefits of wildfire policy or as separate goods competing for the same money is a judgment, not a finding. So is the allocation of obligation: whether a homeowner who bought a legally permitted house in 1985 owes the retrofit, whether a public land agency owes the treatment, whether a utility owes the undergrounding, or whether a community that keeps approving development in a hazard zone owes its neighbors anything. And so is the deepest one — whether the aim is a landscape where fire is a normal, frequent, survivable process that people live inside, or a landscape where fire is an emergency to be excluded from the places people have chosen. Those are two different visions of what living with wildfire means, and no dataset chooses between them.
