# Gender Identity — research

## Overview

Gender identity is a person’s inner sense of being a man, a woman, both, neither or something else; birth sex is the classification as male or female made at or before birth from the body. For most people the two line up. For transgender people they do not, and that gap has become one of the sharpest disputes in law and public life across the English-speaking world and much of Europe: when the two differ, which one should govern? This topic covers that question in the four arenas where it is fought hardest. Legal recognition: what sex or gender appears on birth certificates, passports and driver’s licenses, whether a person can change it by self-declaration, by medical certification or not at all, and what the words “woman” and “sex” mean in anti-discrimination law. Sports: who may compete in the female category, from school teams to the Olympics. Single-sex spaces: bathrooms, changing rooms, shelters, hospital wards and prisons. Medical care for minors: whether gender-dysphoric children and adolescents should be offered puberty blockers, hormones or surgery, and who decides. That last arena has its own page (youth gender transition), so it appears here only as one place where the identity-versus-sex question is decided, not as a full treatment of the clinical evidence. The topic deliberately leaves to neighboring pages the broader fight over organizing politics around group identity (identity politics versus universalism), the limits of speech law including disputes over pronouns and “misgendering” (free speech and hate speech law), and whether mental-health diagnoses in general are expanding too far (overdiagnosis). The center of gravity is the United States and the United Kingdom, where courts and governments have moved furthest in the past two years, with international comparison where it helps.

Why now: on 30 June 2026 the US Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 in West Virginia v. B.P.J. and Little v. Hecox that states may limit girls’ and women’s school sports teams to biological females. Justice Brett Kavanaugh, writing for the Court, held that “sex” in Title IX “cannot plausibly be interpreted to refer to anything other than biological sex” and that the Equal Protection Clause does not require states to make an exception for transgender girls who have taken puberty blockers or hormones; Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson agreed that the Title IX claim failed but dissented on equal protection, arguing the case should have gone back for fact-finding on whether such girls retain any athletic advantage (Supreme Court of the United States, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-43_2b35.pdf). The ruling came weeks after two other moves the same way. On 26 March 2026 the International Olympic Committee announced that, from the 2028 Los Angeles Games, the female category at its events will be limited to biological females, confirmed by a one-time SRY gene screen (International Olympic Committee, https://www.olympics.com/ioc/news/international-olympic-committee-announces-new-policy-on-the-protection-of-the-female-women-s-category-in-olympic-sport). And on 5 August 2026 a revised statutory Code of Practice from Britain’s Equality and Human Rights Commission came into force, telling service providers that women-only services are provided on the basis of biological sex, following the UK Supreme Court’s unanimous April 2025 ruling in For Women Scotland that “woman” and “sex” in the Equality Act 2010 mean biological woman and biological sex (Fox Williams, https://www.foxwilliams.com/2026/06/30/the-updated-draft-code-of-practice-for-services-public-functions-and-associations-what-will-it-mean-for-single-sex-facilities-and-spaces/; UK Supreme Court, https://supremecourt.uk/cases/press-summary/uksc-2024-0042). More than 100 MPs signed a motion asking Parliament to reject the code before it took effect (PinkNews, https://www.thepinknews.com/2026/06/10/ehrc-single-sex-code-2026/).

The debate is live because the institutions that set the rules have shifted quickly and in different directions, and because the public holds views that do not map neatly onto either side. In the United States the federal government has, since January 2025, defined sex for federal purposes as “an individual’s immutable biological classification as either male or female” and directed agencies to strip gender identity from federal policy (The White House, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/defending-women-from-gender-ideology-extremism-and-restoring-biological-truth-to-the-federal-government/), and 27 states have laws limiting female school sports to biological females; a smaller group of states, mostly Democratic-led, protect gender identity in civil rights law and shield transgender medical care. In Britain the highest court has fixed biological sex as the meaning of sex in equality law, while gender recognition certificates remain available. Much of Europe moved the other way over the past decade, letting adults change their legal gender by self-declaration. The people in the argument include transgender people and the LGBT and civil liberties organizations that represent them; women’s-rights campaigners, many of them on the political left, who hold that sex-based protections for women depend on a clear definition of sex; religious and social conservatives who regard gender identity as an ideology rather than a basis for law; sports federations, medical associations and courts; and a large public that, in surveys from Pew and Gallup, favors birth sex for sports teams and identity documents while also, by majority, supporting protections against discrimination for transgender people.

Data year: review of elite performance records, published 2021
Source label: Emma Hilton & Tommy Lundberg, “Transgender Women in the Female Category of Sport: Perspectives on Testosterone Suppression and Performance Advantage,” Sports Medicine (2021)
Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7846503/
Note: Elite men outperform elite women by about 11 to 13 percent in running, swimming and rowing, about 18 percent in jumping and more than 30 percent in weightlifting and vertical jump.
Context: These gaps are the reason sport has a protected female category at all, and every side of the debate accepts that they exist between males and females who have gone through their own puberty. The dispute is about whether they persist after a transgender woman lowers her testosterone. Hilton, a developmental biologist, and Lundberg, a physiologist, reviewed the studies available in 2020 and concluded that 12 months of suppression to below 10 nanomoles per liter, the then-standard eligibility rule, reduced muscle mass by only about 5 percent, leaving most of the male advantage intact. The gaps open at puberty, under the influence of testosterone, which is why some sports bodies distinguish between athletes who went through male puberty and those who did not; World Aquatics did so in 2022, and the IOC’s 2026 policy instead excludes all athletes whose genes show male sex development. The figures are for elite athletes; gaps at youth and recreational levels vary and are less studied.

The other study most often cited on the inclusive side is a cross-sectional laboratory comparison funded by the IOC: Blair Hamilton, Yannis Pitsiladis and colleagues, writing in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in 2024, tested 23 transgender women, 21 cisgender women, 19 cisgender men and 12 transgender men, and found transgender women had higher absolute handgrip strength than cisgender women but lower jump height and aerobic capacity relative to body mass (University of Brighton, https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/strength-power-and-aerobic-capacity-of-transgender-athletes-a-cro/). The Supreme Court’s June 2026 opinion called the question “the subject of ongoing medical and scientific debate” and held that it would not change the constitutional answer either way.

Context: Prisons are the single-sex space where the stakes are starkest, because residents cannot leave and some have committed violent or sexual offenses. ## Questions

Question: When the law sorts people as men or women, should it go by birth sex or by gender identity?
Tag: value
Poles: Birth sex / Gender identity
Why contested: This is the fork under every other question on the page. Both sides agree that sex and gender identity are different things and that for most people they coincide; they disagree about which one legal words like “woman,” “female” and “sex” should track when they come apart. No study can settle it, because it is a choice about what legal categories are for: recording a bodily fact that matters for some purposes, or recognizing how people live and are seen. Courts in the United States (2026) and the United Kingdom (2025) have recently answered “birth sex” for the statutes before them, while a dozen European countries let adults change their legal gender by declaration.
Case for birth sex: Sex is a fact about bodies, fixed at conception and observable, and many laws exist precisely because of it: separate sports, single-sex shelters and prisons, sex-discrimination law, pregnancy and maternity protections, and data that track women’s pay, health and safety. If “woman” means anyone who identifies as one, those protections lose their object, and the people who bear the cost are women, who are physically more vulnerable to male violence and who fought for sex-based provisions in the first place. Gender identity is inner and unverifiable, so a legal category built on it cannot be applied consistently. Transgender people can and should be protected from discrimination and harassment as transgender people, without the law redefining the categories everyone else relies on. The UK Supreme Court reached this conclusion unanimously, and Pew finds 67 percent of Americans agree that sex at birth determines whether someone is a man or a woman.
Case for gender identity: For a transgender woman who lives as a woman, is seen as one and may have changed her body, being classified as male on documents and in public life is not a neutral record but a daily source of exposure, harassment and exclusion. Gender identity is not a whim; it is persistent, deeply felt and, for those whose identity and body conflict, often the cause of serious distress that recognition relieves. Most social interactions never involve chromosomes or birth certificates, so treating people according to how they live is how society already works. The number of people affected is small, about 1 percent of Americans aged 13 and older, and the countries that have recognized gender identity in law for a decade or more, from Argentina to Denmark and Ireland, have not seen the harms opponents predicted. Where a bodily difference genuinely matters, such as in certain medical settings, it can be addressed on its own terms without denying transgender people their identity everywhere else.

Question: Does a transgender woman who has lowered her testosterone keep a meaningful athletic advantage over other women?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Advantage persists / Advantage largely removed
Why contested: Everyone accepts that male puberty produces large performance advantages, about 10 to 13 percent in running and swimming and far more in strength and power events. The dispute is over what is left after testosterone suppression, and the evidence is thin: few transgender women compete at high levels, most studies are small or measure non-athletes, and results differ by measure and by whether the athlete went through male puberty. It is the most nearly settleable question on the page, though even a settled answer would leave the value question of how much residual advantage is too much.
Case for advantage persists: Testosterone suppression reduces muscle mass only modestly, about 5 percent after a year according to Hilton and Lundberg’s 2021 review, and it does not undo height, limb length, skeletal structure, heart and lung size, or hand and foot size built during male puberty. The longest follow-up, of US Air Force members, found transgender women still running 12 percent faster than other women after two years on hormones. Because elite results are separated by fractions of a percent, even a residual edge is decisive. A sex-based category does not need to prove that every individual transgender woman would win; it exists because the categories differ on average, and the same logic excludes men who are slower than many women.
Case for advantage largely removed: Testosterone is the main driver of male performance advantage, and lowering it reduces muscle, hemoglobin and strength. Larger bodies are not always an advantage after transition: a 2024 IOC-funded laboratory study found transgender women had lower jump height and aerobic capacity relative to body mass than other women. Transgender girls who took puberty blockers never go through male puberty at all, which is exactly the situation of the plaintiffs in the US Supreme Court case. Blanket bans treat a varied group as one, ignore the evidence on individuals and exclude children from school teams where the point is participation, not podiums. Few transgender women have dominated women’s sport, and the NCAA president said in 2024 he knew of fewer than 10 transgender athletes among 510,000.

Question: When including transgender women in women’s spaces conflicts with other women’s privacy, dignity or safety, whose claim should prevail?
Tag: value
Poles: Sex-based spaces / Trans inclusion
Why contested: Bathrooms, changing rooms, shelters, hospital wards and prisons are separated by sex for reasons of privacy, dignity and safety, and both sides invoke those same reasons. Most of the conflict is not about whether transgender women are dangerous as a group, but about who carries the cost of uncertainty: women who object to sharing intimate spaces with anyone male-bodied, or transgender women who are excluded from spaces they have used for years and may be unsafe in the men’s alternative. The UK’s 2025 ruling and 2026 code put the default on biological sex; many US states and most LGBT organizations put it on gender identity.
Case for sex-based spaces: Women’s spaces exist because men, as a class, commit the overwhelming majority of sexual and violent crime, and women undressing, sleeping, giving birth or recovering from abuse are entitled to be free of male bodies without having to judge each person’s intentions. A rule based on self-declared identity cannot keep out the minority of men who would exploit it, and it puts the burden on women to object. In January 2026 an employment tribunal found that an NHS trust in Darlington, England, had harassed eight nurses by requiring them to share a changing room with a transgender colleague without a suitable alternative and by not taking their concerns seriously (Nursing Times, https://www.nursingtimes.net/policies-and-guidance/darlington-nurses-were-unlawfully-harassed-over-trust-trans-rules-17-01-2026/). In prisons the stakes are highest: a 2023 Scottish case in which a person convicted of two rapes was initially sent to a women’s prison led the government to change its policy within weeks (CBS News, https://www.cbsnews.com/news/transgender-woman-rape-scotland-mens-prison-nicola-sturgeon-uk). Third spaces and single-occupancy facilities can protect transgender people’s privacy too.
Case for trans inclusion: Transgender women have used women’s facilities for decades in many places, usually unnoticed, and there is little evidence that inclusive policies have increased assaults in bathrooms or changing rooms. Forcing a transgender woman into men’s facilities exposes her to harassment and violence and, in practice, excludes her from public life, because a person who cannot use a bathroom cannot work, study or travel. Policing spaces by biology also means policing appearance, and women who are tall, muscular or masculine-looking, whether transgender or not, get challenged. Exceptional cases such as violent offenders can be handled individually, as prison services already do, without excluding everyone.

Question: Do the benefits of puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones for gender-dysphoric adolescents outweigh the risks?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Benefits outweigh / Risks outweigh
Why contested: The question turns on long-term outcomes that existing studies were not designed to measure: whether treatment reduces distress and suicide risk over years, how many young people would have come to accept their bodies without it, and the costs to fertility, bone density and sexual function. The University of York’s reviews for England’s Cass Review rated almost all of the evidence moderate or low quality, which both sides accept in part and read differently. Medical bodies have split: US pediatric and endocrine associations support access, while health authorities in England, Sweden and Finland restricted it and the American Society of Plastic Surgeons in 2026 recommended delaying surgery until 19. This page treats it as one arena of the larger question; the clinical debate is on the youth gender transition page.
Case for benefits outweigh: Gender dysphoria in adolescence can be severe, with very high rates of depression and suicidality: in the CDC’s 2023 survey a quarter of transgender high school students had attempted suicide in the past year. Puberty blockers pause an irreversible process and buy time; going through the wrong puberty is itself irreversible, and for those who persist it leaves bodily changes that later treatment cannot undo. Observational studies, including long-running Dutch clinic data, report improved mental health and very low regret. Low-quality ratings reflect the near-impossibility of randomized trials in this field, not evidence of harm, and much accepted pediatric care rests on similar evidence. Decisions should be made by families and clinicians case by case, not by legislatures.
Case for risks outweigh: The treatments are powerful and partly irreversible, the patients are minors, and the evidence that they improve mental health over the long term is weak by the account of independent systematic reviews in England, Sweden and Finland. Before puberty blockers, most young children with gender dysphoria came to accept their bodies by adulthood, many growing up gay or lesbian, and nearly all children who start blockers go on to hormones, which suggests blockers lock in a path rather than buy time. The patient population changed sharply after 2010 toward adolescent girls with other mental-health conditions, a group the original Dutch studies did not include. When benefits are unproven and harms to fertility, bones and sexual function are known, caution is the ordinary medical standard; psychological support should come first.

Question: Is the rise in young people identifying as transgender mainly the result of greater acceptance, or partly of social influence?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Greater acceptance / Social influence
Why contested: In the United States, 3.3 percent of 13- to 17-year-olds identify as transgender, against 0.3 percent of those 65 and older, and gender clinics in several countries saw referrals rise many times over in the 2010s, with a shift toward adolescent girls. The two explanations are not exclusive, but they lead to different policies: if the rise mostly reflects people who were always there now feeling safe to say so, recognition is the response; if part of it reflects peer and online influence on distressed adolescents, caution is. Good data to separate the two barely exist, and research on the second hypothesis is itself disputed.
Case for greater acceptance: Every group that faced stigma, from left-handed people to gay men, grew in visible numbers when stigma lifted, and the age pattern among transgender people looks like that history: older generations grew up when identifying as transgender meant losing family, jobs and safety. Much of the youth increase is among nonbinary young people who do not seek medical treatment at all, so it is a change in how young people describe themselves, not a wave of medical transition. The “social contagion” idea rests mostly on surveys of parents recruited from websites skeptical of transition, and it echoes past claims that gay identity spreads by influence.
Case for social influence: The scale and speed of the change, and its concentration among adolescent girls, many with autism, anxiety, depression or trauma, are hard to explain by acceptance alone; clinicians in England, Finland and Sweden reported the shift before the politics hardened. Adolescence is a time when identity is fluid and peer influence strong, and other forms of distress, such as eating disorders, have shown similar social patterns. Some young people who transitioned now say they were influenced by online communities and that their underlying problems went untreated. Taking this seriously does not deny that transgender people exist; it asks for careful assessment of each young person before steps that cannot be reversed.

Question: Does letting adults change their legal gender by self-declaration put women at risk in practice?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real risk / Negligible risk
Why contested: Self-declaration has been law in Argentina since 2012, in Denmark since 2014, in Ireland since 2015 and in Germany since 2024, so there is experience to study, but the risk at issue is about rare events, such as a predatory man using a legal change to enter women’s spaces, which are hard to count and easy to dispute. Opponents point to individual cases; supporters point to the absence of a measured pattern. The answer also depends on what legal gender is used for in a given country, which varies widely.
Case for real risk: A system with no gatekeeping cannot distinguish a transgender woman from a man who wants access to women, and it only takes a few such men to cause serious harm in shelters, prisons and changing rooms. Once a person is legally female, staff who exclude them risk discrimination claims, so the practical effect is to disarm the people responsible for women’s safety. Cases in prisons in Scotland and elsewhere show the problem is not hypothetical, and the burden of proof should lie with those changing a protection women already had.
Case for negligible risk: In the countries with the longest experience, there is no documented surge in men using legal gender changes to harm women; the number of people changing legal gender is small, and most are transgender people seeking documents that match how they live. Laws against assault, voyeurism and harassment apply regardless of legal gender, and facilities and prisons keep the power to make individual risk assessments. Medical gatekeeping, as in Britain’s 2004 Act, imposes delay, cost and intrusive scrutiny on a vulnerable group without stopping anyone determined to abuse the system.

Question: Should these rules be set uniformly by legislatures and courts, or left to sports bodies, schools, doctors and families?
Tag: value
Poles: Uniform law / Local discretion
Why contested: Much of the recent change has come from legislatures and executives setting one rule for all: 27 US state laws on school sports, 26 on youth medical care, a federal executive order on sex, and UK equality law as interpreted by its Supreme Court. Some argue that only clear law protects women and children from institutions captured by one view; others argue that general laws cannot fit the range of sports, ages and medical situations involved. The split does not track left and right neatly: conservatives favor uniform rules on sports and care but local discretion on schools, and progressives the reverse.
Case for uniform law: When the stakes are fairness for girls, safety in intimate spaces and irreversible treatment of children, the rules should not depend on which school board, sports body or clinic a family happens to deal with. Professional bodies and institutions were slow to recognize problems, as England’s Cass Review found of its national gender clinic, and legislatures are accountable to the public in a way that associations are not. Clear statutory definitions also protect institutions from litigation on both sides.
Case for local discretion: A single rule cannot fit a middle-school cross-country team and an Olympic final, or a 12-year-old and a 17-year-old with years of distress. Sports bodies know their sports, doctors know their patients and parents know their children; politicians writing broad bans, often for electoral advantage, override them all. The Center for American Progress argued in 2026 that eligibility rules belong “with sports’ governing bodies or schools themselves rather than with politicians,” even if some restrictions result, and the NCAA and IOC have shown that governing bodies can and do draw lines on their own.

Question: Does legal and social recognition of gender identity improve transgender people’s well-being?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Recognition helps / Little measured effect
Why contested: Transgender people report far worse mental health than others, and both sides want it to improve. Supporters of recognition cite studies linking identity documents that match a person’s gender, use of chosen names and family acceptance to lower distress; critics note that these studies are mostly cross-sectional surveys of self-selected participants that cannot separate cause from effect, and that recognition policies have spread for a decade without clear population-level improvement. Causal evidence is scarce on both sides.
Case for recognition helps: Living as oneself without being forced to disclose a transgender history every time a document is checked removes a constant source of stress and danger. Studies in several countries associate gender-concordant identity documents, use of chosen names and supportive families with less depression and suicidality, and the minority-stress model, well established for gay and lesbian people, predicts exactly this. Policies that deny recognition send a public message of rejection that young people in particular absorb.
Case for little measured effect: Much of the distress transgender people report coexists with other conditions that recognition does not treat, and correlational studies cannot show that recognition caused the better outcomes rather than reflecting families and circumstances that were better to begin with. Registers in Finland and Sweden find poor outcomes persisting after transition, which points to the need for broad mental-health care rather than legal and social changes presented as treatment. Telling young people that recognition is a matter of life and death can itself raise the stakes and the distress.

## Positions

Order note: the first two camps are the poles of the argument on its own terms, and both carry broad weight. The first is held by LGBT and civil liberties organizations, most major US medical associations, Democratic-led states and the governments of a dozen European countries with self-declared legal gender. The second is held by women’s-rights campaigners, many of them on the left, is close to the combination most common in US and international polls (birth sex for sports and documents, protection from discrimination for transgender people), and is now the reading of UK equality law. The third holds the most governmental power in the United States today, through the federal executive and Republican-led states, and differs from the second on whether gender identity should be recognized or protected in law at all. The fourth is the hybrid: it accepts the first camp’s view in most domains but accepts sex-based limits in competitive sport, and it has gained ground among Democratic politicians and center-left institutions since 2024. The fifth rejects the question as posed, holding that the law should stop assigning people a sex or gender at all; it is mainly an academic and activist position with little current political weight, but it answers the question differently from every other camp.

Camp: Recognize gender identity in law and shared life
Adherents: American Civil Liberties Union, https://www.aclu.org/press-releases/attorneys-for-transgender-student-athletes-respond-to-supreme-court-ruling-in-bpj-and-hecox (30 June 2026, responding to the Supreme Court’s sports ruling; Joshua Block, senior counsel in its LGBTQ & HIV Project, called it “a heartbreaking ruling for our clients and transgender girls like them who’ve asked for nothing more than the same opportunities afforded to their peers”); Chase Strangio, co-director of the ACLU’s LGBTQ & HIV Project and counsel in United States v. Skrmetti, https://www.democracynow.org/2026/7/1/supreme_court_trans_student_athletes (1 July 2026: “There is an effort, that we’re seeing escalate, to push trans people out of public life, out of our schools, out of our workplaces”); American Academy of Pediatrics, https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/33988/AAP-Proposed-restrictions-to-gender-affirming-care (calling proposed restrictions on gender-affirming care “a baseless intrusion into the patient-physician relationship”)
Case: Transgender people are who they say they are. A transgender woman is a woman, not a man claiming a privilege, and the law should treat her as one: on her documents, in the spaces she uses, on the teams she plays for and in the care she and her doctors choose. Gender identity is a deep and persistent part of a person, not a preference, and for those whose identity and body conflict, recognition is what makes ordinary life possible. The restrictions now spreading across the United States and Britain do not address real problems. Transgender people are about 1 percent of the population; the NCAA knew of fewer than 10 transgender athletes among 510,000; assaults by transgender women in bathrooms are vanishingly rare. What the restrictions do is make it harder for transgender people to work, study, travel, play sports and get medical care, and they send a message that transgender people do not belong in public life. Women’s rights and transgender rights are not in competition: the same sexism that polices how women look and behave targets transgender people, and the same rules used to check transgender women end up checking any woman who looks masculine. Civil rights law has always protected people from being treated worse for who they are; it should protect transgender people too.
Best evidence: In the CDC’s 2023 national survey, 25.9 percent of transgender high school students had attempted suicide in the past year, against 11.0 percent of cisgender girls and 5.3 percent of cisgender boys, and transgender students reported far more bullying and violence, which the camp attributes to stigma and exclusion (CDC, MMWR, 2024). Countries that have allowed legal gender change by self-declaration for a decade or more, including Argentina (2012), Denmark (2014) and Ireland (2015), are the camp’s evidence that recognition does not produce the harms opponents predict. Large majorities still support protecting transgender people from discrimination: 73 percent across 26 countries in Ipsos’s 2026 survey and 56 percent of Americans in Pew’s 2025 survey. A 2024 IOC-funded laboratory study found transgender women had lower jump height and aerobic capacity relative to body mass than other women (Hamilton and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that recognizing gender identity in sports and single-sex spaces takes something real from women: fair competition and privacy from male bodies. The camp’s answer is that these concerns are better met with targeted rules than blanket exclusion. Sports bodies can set eligibility by sport and level based on evidence about individuals, as many did before 2022, and children’s sports are about participation, not records. In spaces, behavior is what threatens women, and laws against assault, voyeurism and harassment apply to everyone; individual risk assessments already handle the rare dangerous case, as in prisons. Excluding all transgender women to guard against a hypothetical few treats a whole group as suspects, which civil rights law forbids in every other context.
Variation inside the camp: Most advocates favor legal gender change by self-declaration, as in Ireland or Germany; some accept a simple administrative process with minimal gatekeeping. On sports, a wing accepts eligibility criteria such as testosterone limits at elite levels while opposing categorical bans, especially for children, while others oppose any restriction. On medical care for minors, the camp holds that decisions belong to families and clinicians following guidelines such as those of WPATH and the Endocrine Society; some within it accept more cautious assessment of adolescents than US clinics practiced in the 2010s. Some favor adding a third, nonbinary legal marker; others focus on male and female.

Camp: Use biological sex where sex matters, and protect transgender people from discrimination
Adherents: Sex Matters, UK human-rights charity, https://sex-matters.org/about-us/ (its statement of belief: “I do not believe it is incompatible to recognise that human beings cannot change sex whilst also protecting the human rights of people who identify as transgender”); For Women Scotland, the campaign group that won the April 2025 UK Supreme Court case, https://forwomen.scot/20/04/2025/uk-supreme-court-the-judgment/; J.K. Rowling, author, https://www.jkrowling.com/opinions/j-k-rowling-writes-about-her-reasons-for-speaking-out-on-sex-and-gender-issues/ (10 June 2020: “So I want trans women to be safe. At the same time, I do not want to make natal girls and women less safe.”)
Case: Sex is real, binary and immutable, and it matters. Women’s rights were won on the basis of sex, because it is female bodies that are discriminated against, that get pregnant, that are on average smaller and weaker, and that are the overwhelming majority of victims of male sexual violence. Separate sports, changing rooms, shelters, hospital wards and prisons exist for those reasons, and they only work if “woman” means adult human female. Gender identity is a sincere feeling for those who have it, but it is not a physical fact and cannot be verified; a law that lets it override sex leaves women unable to say no to any male who identifies into their spaces and makes them bigots for trying. None of this requires denying transgender people dignity. They should be free to live as they wish, dress and present as they choose, and be protected from discrimination, harassment and violence as transgender people, as the UK’s Equality Act already does through the protected characteristic of gender reassignment. The argument is about where sex-based rules apply, not about whether transgender people deserve respect. Many in this camp are lesbians, feminists and people on the left who regard it as the continuation of feminism, not a departure from it.
Best evidence: The UK Supreme Court ruled unanimously in April 2025 that “woman” and “sex” in the Equality Act 2010 mean biological woman and biological sex, while stressing that transgender people remain protected against discrimination (UK Supreme Court, For Women Scotland v The Scottish Ministers). Male performance advantages of 11 to 13 percent in running and swimming and more than 30 percent in weightlifting are largely retained after a year of testosterone suppression, which reduces muscle mass by about 5 percent (Hilton and Lundberg, Sports Medicine, 2021); transgender women in the US Air Force still ran 12 percent faster than other women after two years of hormones (Roberts and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2021). In January 2026 an employment tribunal found an NHS trust had harassed eight nurses by requiring them to share a changing room with a transgender colleague without a suitable alternative. The University of York found one high-quality study out of 50 on puberty blockers (Archives of Disease in Childhood, 2024), the basis for England’s more cautious approach to youth gender medicine.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that in practice sex-based rules exclude transgender people from ordinary life, because a transgender woman barred from the women’s toilets and unsafe in the men’s has nowhere to go. The camp’s answer is that the solution is more provision, not redefinition: single-occupancy toilets and changing rooms, third spaces and open sports categories protect transgender people’s privacy and safety without removing women’s. The UK code of practice that took effect in August 2026 requires providers to consider the needs of everyone affected. The camp also rejects the charge that it seeks to erase transgender people: it defends their right to transition as adults and to be free from discrimination, which separates it from those who regard gender identity as an ideology to be rolled back.
Variation inside the camp: Members differ on legal gender recognition itself. Some accept a gender recognition certificate as a legal fiction for limited purposes such as marriage and pensions; others want legal sex fixed at birth. On minors, the camp generally favors the Cass Review’s cautious, psychology-first approach and opposes social transition in schools without parental involvement, but not all support criminal bans on medical care. Most accept transgender people in sex-neutral settings and in their own facilities; some would allow transgender women who have had genital surgery into some women’s spaces. The camp includes feminists on the left and many people on the center-right who are not religious conservatives, and its members often stress their distance from the next camp.

Camp: Make biological sex the only category the law recognizes
Adherents: The White House, Executive Order 14168, “Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government” (20 January 2025), https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/defending-women-from-gender-ideology-extremism-and-restoring-biological-truth-to-the-federal-government/ (defining sex as “an individual’s immutable biological classification as either male or female,” ordering that “federal funds shall not be used to promote gender ideology,” and requiring identity documents to reflect sex); The Heritage Foundation, Solutions 2026, https://static.heritage.org/solutions/2026/00_solutions_2026.pdf (calling to “oppose any federal or state law or regulation that would enshrine gender ideology in law” and stating that “feelings of gender confusion do not constitute a protected ‘identity’”)
Case: There are two sexes, male and female, fixed at conception, and the law should say so plainly and consistently. “Gender identity” is not a fact about anyone’s body but an ideology that asks everyone else to treat a feeling as more real than biology, and in the past decade it has been written into school curricula, medical practice, federal regulations and civil rights law with little public debate. The costs fall on women, who lose fair sports and safe spaces; on children, who are taught that they may be born in the wrong body and steered toward drugs and surgery that cannot be undone; on parents, who are kept in the dark by schools that socially transition their children; and on anyone whose conscience or faith tells them the truth about sex and who is punished for saying it. Compassion for people who experience gender distress is real, and they deserve care, often from counselors and families rather than surgeons. But compassion does not require the law to adopt a false premise. Government should record sex, protect sex-based spaces and opportunities, end public funding for gender transition, and protect the freedom of Americans to speak, work and raise their children according to their beliefs about sex.
Best evidence: Two-thirds of Americans say sex at birth determines whether someone is a man or a woman (67 percent, Pew, November 2025), up from 54 percent in 2017, and 69 percent say transgender athletes should play on teams matching their birth sex (Gallup, 2025). The Supreme Court upheld state bans on medical transition for minors in United States v. Skrmetti (June 2025) and state laws reserving female sports for biological females in West Virginia v. B.P.J. (June 2026), each by 6 to 3. Health authorities in England, Sweden and Finland restricted puberty blockers after systematic reviews found weak evidence, and the US Department of Health and Human Services reached similar conclusions in 2025. The camp points to the NCAA’s 2025 and the IOC’s 2026 moves to sex-based eligibility in women’s sport as evidence that institutions are correcting a mistake.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that removing legal recognition and anti-discrimination protection leaves a small, vulnerable group exposed to mistreatment for who they are. The camp’s answer is that nobody should be harassed or assaulted, and laws against those acts protect everyone equally, transgender people included; what it rejects is a special legal category built on identity that then overrides other people’s rights, speech and consciences. It argues that the deeper harm to people with gender distress comes from telling them that changing their bodies is the answer, and that honest care, including mental-health treatment, serves them better than affirmation enforced by law.
Variation inside the camp: Religious conservatives ground the position in a view of the human person as created male and female; secular conservatives ground it in biology and the protection of children and women. Members differ on adults: most focus on minors, sports, spaces and public funding and leave adults free to transition privately, while some favor restricting adult care in public programs such as prisons, the military and Medicaid. Some would keep narrow protections against outright harassment; others oppose any gender-identity category in civil rights law, including compromise bills such as the Fairness for All Act. The Trump administration has also barred transgender people from military service, which some in the camp support and others regard as a separate question.

Camp: Recognize gender identity, with sex-based limits in competitive sport
Adherents: Center for American Progress, “Ending the Backlash on LGBTQI+ Issues” (July 2026), https://www.americanprogress.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/07/LGBTQIagenda-report.pdf (“While local bodies and associations should minimize unnecessary exclusion, restrictions may be appropriate under some circumstances,” alongside support for the Equality Act and for medical decisions made by parents and physicians); Gavin Newsom, governor of California, https://calmatters.org/politics/2025/03/newsom-transgender-athletes/ (6 March 2025, on transgender athletes in girls’ and women’s sports: “It is an issue of fairness. It’s deeply unfair,” while defending transgender people against “deplorable anti-LGBTQ rhetoric”)
Case: Transgender people deserve the same dignity, documents, jobs, housing, health care and freedom from discrimination as anyone else, and most of the rules now being passed against them are cruel and unnecessary. But competitive sport is the one place where the body, not identity, is the point: the female category exists to protect women from male physical advantage, and that advantage does not fully disappear with hormones. Saying so is not bigotry, and treating it as bigotry has cost transgender people allies on everything else. The fair settlement is to recognize gender identity in ordinary life while letting sports bodies draw eligibility lines by sport, age and level, with as much inclusion as fairness allows: open participation in children’s recreational play, sex-based categories where competition is serious. Defending transgender people on the broadly popular ground of non-discrimination, rather than on the unpopular ground of sports eligibility, is both right on the merits and the way to stop the backlash.
Best evidence: Public opinion matches this settlement more closely than either pole: 56 percent of Americans support protecting transgender people from discrimination while 66 percent favor birth-sex sports teams (Pew, 2025), and across 26 countries 73 percent support anti-discrimination protection while 22 percent support gender-identity-based sports eligibility (Ipsos, 2026). Even among Democrats, 45 percent favored the birth-sex sports requirement in 2025, up from 37 percent in 2022 (Pew). The NCAA, after a long period of hormone-based eligibility, limited women’s competition to athletes assigned female at birth in February 2025, and the camp sees in this a governing body making a sport-specific judgment rather than a political one.
Answer to the strongest objection: Critics on the inclusive side say this camp concedes the principle that transgender women are not really women and invites further restrictions; critics on the sex-based side say it is incoherent to accept sex in sports but not in changing rooms. The camp’s answer to both is that different domains raise different questions. In sport the issue is physical performance, which follows sex; in documents, jobs and most public spaces the issue is dignity and safety, which follow how a person lives. Drawing lines domain by domain is how law handles every other hard case, and it is the only approach that can command a durable majority.
Variation inside the camp: Members differ on who should draw the sports line: some favor leaving it to sports bodies and schools, others accept state laws limited to competitive levels. Some extend sex-based rules to a few other settings, such as prisons for those with violent records or rape crisis services; others limit them to elite sport. On youth medical care the camp leans toward the first camp’s view that parents and doctors should decide, though some members accept England’s more cautious approach.

Camp: Stop classifying people by sex in law
Adherents: Future of Legal Gender project, King’s College London, led by Professor Davina Cooper, https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/future-of-legal-gender-project-publishes-final-report (10 May 2022, final report finding that abolishing legal sex status would “dismantle a legal structure that entrenches and legitimates gender-based differences” and “remove legal burdens from those who seek to change their sex or gender”); Gender-Free ID Coalition, Canada, http://gender-freeidcoalition.ca/ (a coalition of lawyers and advocates seeking removal of the sex or gender designation from all state-issued documents, including birth certificates, passports and driver’s licenses)
Case: The fight over whether the state should record a person’s birth sex or gender identity assumes that the state should record either. It need not. Race, religion and sexual orientation are not assigned at birth and printed on passports, yet the law still protects people from discrimination on those grounds and collects data about them where needed. Assigning everyone a legal sex at birth and making it hard to change creates the very conflicts this debate is about, forces transgender and nonbinary people into categories that do not fit, and entrenches the idea that men and women are fundamentally different kinds of citizen. Where a body really matters, such as in medicine, pregnancy or particular sports, rules can refer directly to the relevant physical characteristic or to how a person lives, rather than to a status on a certificate. An “X” marker is not enough, because it marks out the people who choose it.
Best evidence: The camp points to how little practical work legal sex does in many settings: Germany’s national identity card, for example, carries no sex field, and the Future of Legal Gender project spent four years mapping where British law relies on sex or gender, from marriage to housing, and drafted legislative principles for removing legal sex status. The UK census episode, in which 2021 gender identity estimates for England and Wales lost their accredited status because respondents misread the question, is to the camp evidence that one-box classifications serve no one well. Its members note that both sides of the current debate fight over the same document, the birth certificate, whose sex field is the source of the conflict.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that removing legal sex would leave women unable to define or defend sex-based rights such as single-sex services, pregnancy protections and sex-discrimination claims, and would destroy data on sex. The camp’s answer is that those rights can be written in terms of the characteristic that matters (pregnancy, female physiology, being treated as a woman) rather than a birth status, that sex-discrimination law already protects people on the basis of how they are perceived, and that data can still be collected on sex where relevant, as it is on many characteristics that carry no legal status. Its own researchers acknowledge these concerns as the main risks of reform.
Variation inside the camp: Some seek full decertification, removing sex from birth registration and all documents; others seek only to remove it from documents where it serves no purpose, such as identity cards and driver’s licenses, while keeping it in birth and medical records. Some hold this position as a long-term goal while supporting self-declared gender recognition in the meantime.

## Crux

The question under the others: when a person’s gender identity and birth sex differ, in which settings do differences between male and female bodies matter enough that sex should govern, and where they do, who should bear the cost of the line: the transgender people it excludes, or the women and girls it is meant to protect?

What evidence could settle: How much physical advantage remains after male puberty and testosterone suppression, sport by sport and at each age and level of competition, which longitudinal studies of athletes could measure far better than the small and cross-sectional studies available now. Whether inclusive or sex-based rules for bathrooms, changing rooms, shelters and prisons change rates of assault, harassment and exclusion, for women and for transgender people, which incident records from places with different rules could show. What happens over decades to gender-dysphoric adolescents who receive puberty blockers and hormones and to comparable adolescents who do not, in mental health, regret, fertility and bone health, which long-term cohort studies and trials such as England’s planned puberty-blocker study are designed to answer. Whether legal recognition and acceptance measurably improve transgender people’s mental health, and how much of the rise in young people identifying as transgender reflects acceptance and how much social influence. Evidence on each of these would move people in the middle, especially on sports and youth medicine, where many hold their view provisionally.

What no data can close: Whether legal words like “woman” should name a bodily category or a social identity, which is a choice about what law is for rather than a finding about the world. How to weigh the inclusion and dignity of a small group against the fairness, privacy and sense of safety of a much larger one, when both claims are sincere and neither can be fully met without some cost to the other. Whether, under real uncertainty about children, the default should be caution about irreversible steps or respect for the judgment of families, clinicians and young people themselves. And whether the state should sort people by sex at all. Someone who holds that sex-based protections are the foundation of women’s equality will keep sex-based rules even if the measured harms of inclusion turn out small; someone who holds that being treated as one’s gender is a basic condition of a dignified life will oppose them even if some residual unfairness is proven. Most people sit between these, accepting sex in some domains and identity in others, and the political fight is largely over where those lines fall.
