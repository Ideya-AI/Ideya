# Shareholders vs. Stakeholders — research

## Overview

This topic is about whom a business corporation exists to serve. One answer, usually called shareholder primacy, holds that a company’s directors and managers work for its owners and should run it to maximize the long-run value of their shares, within the law; obligations to workers, customers, suppliers, communities and the environment are set by contracts, competition and regulation, not by the board’s own sense of what society needs. The other answer, usually called stakeholder capitalism or stakeholder governance, holds that a large company serves a wider set of people whose lives depend on it, and that its leaders should weigh their interests alongside, and sometimes against, those of shareholders. Between and around those poles sit the live disputes: whether serving stakeholders is just smart long-term business or a real trade-off; whether US corporate law actually requires profit maximization; whether workers should hold seats on corporate boards, as in Germany; whether asset managers should use their votes to press companies on climate, diversity and other social questions; and whether the “environmental, social and governance” (ESG) movement of the 2010s and early 2020s was sound risk management or politics carried into the boardroom. The page is centered on the United States, where the fight over corporate purpose and ESG has been sharpest, with comparisons to the United Kingdom, Germany and the European Union, which write different answers into law. It deliberately leaves to their own pages the broader gap between rich and poor (economic inequality), unions and collective bargaining outside the boardroom (labor power), the market power of very large firms (antitrust and market power), and carbon pricing and climate policy as such, which appear here only as examples of what corporations might be asked to do.

Why now: on 16 September 2026 the US Securities and Exchange Commission proposed rescinding Rule 14a-8, the shareholder proposal rule adopted in 1942 that requires public companies to put qualifying shareholder resolutions on the ballot they send to investors, which would leave the question to state law and companies’ own governing documents (SEC fact sheet, https://www.sec.gov/files/34-106383-fact-sheet.pdf; adoption date per Orrick, https://www.orrick.com/zh-CN/Insights/2026/09/SEC-Proposes-to-End-Federal-Shareholder-Proposal-Rule-and-Modernize-Proxy-Solicitation). The commission argued that the rule exceeds its statutory authority and has become a mechanism for influencing companies in ways inconsistent with its original purpose. Rule 14a-8 is the channel through which climate, diversity, political-spending and labor resolutions have reached corporate ballots for decades, so investor coalitions such as the Interfaith Center on Corporate Responsibility filed comments opposing it; its chief executive, Josh Zinner, said rescission “would create a massive void impacting both investors and companies” (ICCR, 23 November 2026, https://www.iccr.org/in-comment-letter-to-the-sec-iccr-defends-shareholder-proposal-rule-as-a-critical-bulwark-of-corporate-accountability/). The proposal capped a year in which the US pulled away from stakeholder-oriented governance on several fronts: an executive order of 11 December 2025 directed regulators to scrutinize the proxy advisers ISS and Glass Lewis, accusing them of advancing politically motivated agendas on diversity and ESG (Federal Register, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-16/pdf/2025-23093.pdf); SEC staff stopped substantively reviewing most companies’ requests to exclude shareholder proposals; ExxonMobil shareholders voted in May 2026 to move the company’s legal home to Texas, whose revised corporate law lets companies raise the ownership needed to file a proposal; and the European Union, in Directive (EU) 2026/470 published on 26 February 2026, narrowed its new corporate due-diligence law to companies with more than 5,000 employees and €1.5 billion in turnover (DLA Piper, https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/blogs/environment-health-safety-and-product-compliance/2026/eu-omnibus-i-directive-amending-csrd-and-csddd-will-enter-into-force-on-18-march-2026).

The debate is live because the formal commitments and the practice have moved apart. In August 2019, 181 chief executives in the Business Roundtable signed a statement committing to “deliver value to all” of their stakeholders, retiring the group’s earlier language that corporations exist principally to serve shareholders. Since then, record share buybacks, a backlash against ESG in Republican-led states and in Washington, withdrawals by large asset managers from climate alliances, and falling investor support for environmental and social resolutions have led some to declare stakeholder capitalism over, while others argue that the underlying idea (that companies which treat workers, customers and the planet well create more value over time) never depended on the label. A third group says the voluntary version always lacked teeth and that only changes in corporate law, such as worker-elected directors or new legal duties for directors, will shift corporate behavior. The participants include corporate executives and their associations, such as the Business Roundtable; asset managers such as BlackRock, Vanguard and newer anti-ESG funds such as Strive; shareholder activists on both sides; proxy advisers; state treasurers and attorneys general; the SEC and Delaware’s courts and legislature; legislators such as Senator Elizabeth Warren; labor unions; think tanks on the left and the right; and academics in law, finance and economics, from Lucian Bebchuk and Alex Edmans to Colin Mayer and Luigi Zingales.

Operating earnings exclude one-time items and are higher than reported (GAAP) earnings, so the payout share would be higher against reported earnings. The ratio is the heart of a measurement dispute. Lazonick (Harvard Business Review, 2014) found that 449 companies in the S&P 500 over 2003 to 2012 spent 54 percent of earnings on buybacks and 37 percent on dividends. Fried and Wang (Harvard Business Review, 2018, summarized at https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/03/19/are-buybacks-really-shortchanging-investment/) agree that gross payouts were 96 percent of net income over 2007 to 2016 but subtract the roughly $3.3 trillion companies raised by issuing shares and add back research and development spending, and put net payouts at 41.5 percent of R&D-adjusted net income. Which measure is right depends on whether one asks how much cash leaves individual companies or how much leaves the corporate sector as a whole.

Context: The Business Roundtable is an association of chief executives of major US companies. Its principles of corporate governance had stated since 1997 that corporations exist principally to serve their shareholders; the 2019 statement replaced that language with five commitments: delivering value to customers, investing in employees, dealing fairly and ethically with suppliers, supporting communities and “generating long-term value for shareholders.” Its text says the signers “share a fundamental commitment to all of our stakeholders” and “commit to deliver value to all of them” (https://opportunity.businessroundtable.org/ourcommitment/). Unit: percent of supervisory board seats elected by employees (companies with more than 2,000 employees)
Viz: ranked

Data year: Current law (One-Third Participation Act 2004; Codetermination Act 1976)
Source label: Simon Jäger and Benjamin Schoefer, “Worker Voice and Firm Governance,” NBER Reporter 2026, No. 1 (April 2026)
Source URL: https://www.nber.org/reporter/2026number1/worker-voice-and-firm-governance
Note: German companies with 500 to 2,000 employees must give workers one-third of the seats on their supervisory boards, and companies with more than 2,000 employees must give them half, with shareholders keeping a tie-breaking vote.
Context: Germany’s “codetermination” (Mitbestimmung) is the main real-world model for putting workers inside corporate governance, and US proposals such as Senator Elizabeth Warren’s Accountable Capitalism Act, which would have employees elect at least 40 percent of the directors of companies with more than $1 billion in revenue, draw on it. Under German law the chair, chosen by shareholder representatives, casts the deciding vote in a tie, which is why critics call the half-and-half boards “quasi-parity.” German firms also have elected works councils at the workplace level. The strongest evidence on effects comes from Jäger, Schoefer and Jörg Heining (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2021), who used a 1994 reform that abolished the one-third mandate for newly incorporated companies; they found “no detectable effect on wages or the wage structure” and could rule out wage effects larger than about 4 percent, while codetermined firms had, if anything, slightly higher capital intensity. Studies of Finland find small positive effects on wages and larger positive effects on productivity. Advocates and opponents both cite these results: advocates as proof that worker voice does not harm firms, opponents as proof that it does little for workers.

## Questions

Question: Should companies pursue social goals beyond what their owners’ long-run profit requires?
Tag: value
Poles: Leave it to government / Business shares the duty
Why contested: Almost everyone agrees that companies should obey the law and that treating workers and customers well is often good business. The dispute is over the remainder: the cases where doing more for workers, communities or the climate would cost shareholders money, and over who should decide when that trade is worth making.
Best case, leave it to government: A corporation is a tool owners use to pool capital, and its managers spend other people’s money. When an executive chooses to sacrifice profit for a social cause, he is in effect taxing shareholders, customers or employees and spending the proceeds on goals no one elected him to choose, as Milton Friedman argued in 1970. Elected governments can weigh competing social aims, tax and regulate everyone alike, and be voted out; corporate boards cannot. Pollution, low wages and unsafe products are real problems, but the remedy is law that binds every firm, not the private conscience of managers, which competitors without such scruples will undercut. Asking firms to be guardians of the public good also blurs whom managers answer to, and leaders who answer to everyone answer to no one.
Best case, business shares the duty: The modern corporation is a creature of law, granted limited liability and perpetual life by the public, and its largest examples have more economic power than many governments. Law is always incomplete and slow, and companies themselves lobby to shape it, so “leave it to regulation” often means leaving it to rules the regulated wrote. Workers who invest years in firm-specific skills, communities that build roads and schools around a plant, and suppliers who commit to one buyer all bear risks much like shareholders do and have no vote. A company that treats these people as costs to be minimized will often be acting within the law and still doing harm. Much of what is asked is modest: not sacrificing returns for causes, but declining to profit from harms the law has not yet caught up with.

Question: Does a focus on stakeholders raise or lower long-run returns to shareholders?
Tag: empirical
Poles: It pays off / It costs owners
Why contested: Hundreds of studies link ESG scores, employee satisfaction or customer satisfaction to financial performance, but they disagree on direction and size, ESG ratings themselves disagree widely, and it is hard to tell whether good practices cause good performance or profitable firms can simply afford them.
Best case, it pays off: Companies are built by people and depend on reputations, and the evidence that investing in them pays is broad. Alex Edmans of London Business School found that companies with high employee satisfaction outperformed their peers by 2.3 to 3.8 percent a year over 28 years. Reviews of more than 1,000 studies find positive or neutral links between sustainability and corporate performance far more often than negative ones. Firms that cut corners on safety, the environment or their workforce run risks that markets underprice until they explode, as in BP’s Deepwater Horizon spill or Boeing’s 737 MAX crisis. The long-run shareholder and the stakeholder want mostly the same thing.
Best case, it costs owners: The cases where serving stakeholders also serves shareholders need no special doctrine; ordinary profit-seeking already covers them. The interesting cases are the trade-offs, and there the evidence is weaker than it looks. ESG ratings correlate only 0.38 to 0.71 with one another, many studies cannot separate cause from effect, and sustainable funds have charged higher fees while lagging in years when energy stocks rallied. Danone, which adopted France’s “company with a mission” status in 2020, saw its chief executive ousted in 2021 after years of underperformance. When managers insist that every stakeholder program pays, they make their own choices impossible to judge.

Question: Would stakeholder governance make corporate leaders more accountable or less?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Less accountable / More accountable
Why contested: Telling managers to serve many groups gives them more discretion, and the dispute is whether they would use it on behalf of those groups or of themselves. The answer depends on how leaders actually behave when given the freedom, and on whether stakeholders get any means of enforcing their claims.
Best case, less accountable: A single measurable goal, shareholder value, lets owners judge managers by results. A manager told to balance five constituencies can justify almost any decision by pointing to one of them, which is why corporate leaders welcome the idea. Lucian Bebchuk and Roberto Tallarita of Harvard found that of 48 Business Roundtable signatories that answered their inquiry, only one had board approval to sign, and in more than 100 private-equity buyouts in states whose laws let directors weigh stakeholders, leaders negotiated large premiums for shareholders and payouts for themselves while rarely securing any protection for employees. Stakeholderism, they conclude, increases leaders’ insulation from shareholders and would fail the stakeholders it promises to serve.
Best case, more accountable: The weakness Bebchuk describes is real, but it is an argument against voluntary stakeholderism without enforcement, not against stakeholder governance. Give workers elected seats on the board, as Germany and 13 other European countries do in some form, or require directors to report how they weighed each group, as the UK’s Companies Act does, and stakeholders gain voices of their own. Shareholder accountability has its own failure: managers paid mainly in stock answer to share prices that can be pumped by buybacks and cost cuts, which is accountability to the wrong thing. Broadening who can call managers to account adds checks; it does not remove them.

Question: Has the pressure to deliver shareholder value made companies short-termist?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Short-termism is real / Markets value the long run
Why contested: Record share buybacks, quarterly earnings guidance and stock-based pay coexist with high R&D spending and stock prices that reward firms, such as Amazon or Tesla, that invested for years without profit. Each side can point to data, and the measures they use (gross or net payouts, company or economy level) give different answers.
Best case, short-termism is real: In a well-known 2005 survey of financial executives by John Graham, Campbell Harvey and Shiva Rajgopal, a majority said they would forgo a value-creating investment rather than miss a quarterly earnings target. S&P 500 companies returned the equivalent of about 80 percent of their operating earnings to shareholders in 2024, and buybacks can lift earnings per share and executives’ stock awards without creating anything. William Lazonick argues that a corporate habit of downsizing and distributing cash to shareholders replaced the postwar habit of retaining and reinvesting it, starving wages and productive investment. Patient strategies, from training workers to cutting emissions, pay off over horizons longer than most executives’ tenure.
Best case, markets value the long run: Stock prices are the market’s estimate of all future cash flows, so a firm that shortchanges its future is punished now; companies with distant payoffs often trade at very high valuations. Jesse Fried and Charles Wang show that once share issuance is netted out, S&P 500 net payouts were about 41.5 percent of R&D-adjusted net income from 2007 to 2016, and that investment and R&D were near record levels. Cash returned to shareholders does not vanish; it is reinvested in younger firms with better opportunities. The short-termism complaint often reflects a preference for keeping capital in incumbent firms that no longer have good uses for it.

Question: Does US corporate law require directors to maximize shareholder value?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Law requires it / Law leaves discretion
Why contested: Courts have said both that a for-profit corporation must be run for its stockholders and that directors are protected when they make good-faith business judgments, including generous ones toward employees or communities. What the law requires in practice depends on which situations one looks at.
Best case, law requires it: In Dodge v. Ford Motor Co. (1919) Michigan’s Supreme Court held that a business corporation is organized primarily for the profit of its stockholders, and Delaware, home to two-thirds of the Fortune 500, has repeatedly said the same. In eBay Domestic Holdings v. Newmark (2010) the Court of Chancery struck down craigslist’s defense against eBay because its founders openly put community values ahead of stockholder value, and under the Revlon doctrine directors selling a company must seek the best price for shareholders. Stakeholder considerations are permitted only as means to shareholder value; a board that openly sacrificed shareholders would lose in court.
Best case, law leaves discretion: Outside a sale of the company, the business judgment rule shields almost any decision a board can plausibly connect to the company’s long-term interest, which is nearly every decision. The late Lynn Stout of Cornell argued that no statute requires profit maximization and that shareholder primacy is a managerial and market norm, not a legal command. A majority of states have constituency statutes that let directors weigh employees and communities, more than 40 states allow benefit corporations with explicit stakeholder duties, and the UK’s Companies Act requires directors to have regard to employees, suppliers, communities and the environment. The law permits a stakeholder-minded board; markets and pay packages are what push the other way.

Question: Should workers elect some of the directors of large companies?
Tag: value
Poles: Give workers seats / Keep boards to owners
Why contested: Germany has required worker directors for decades, and Senator Elizabeth Warren and some conservative thinkers have proposed versions for the United States. The evidence suggests modest effects either way, so the choice turns on whether worker voice is valued for itself and on what it would do in the US system.
Best case, give workers seats: Workers have more at stake in most companies than any single shareholder: their income, skills and often their town are tied to the firm, and unlike shareholders they cannot diversify. Worker directors bring knowledge of operations that outside directors lack and a voice for the long term, since workers stay when activist investors leave. The best evidence from Germany, by Simon Jäger, Benjamin Schoefer and Jörg Heining, finds that worker representation does not harm firms and if anything slightly raises capital investment; Finnish evidence shows productivity gains. American Compass, a conservative think tank, and Senator Warren’s Accountable Capitalism Act, which would give employees 40 percent of board seats at companies with more than $1 billion in revenue, both see board voice as a way to rebuild worker power where unions have faded.
Best case, keep boards to owners: Boards work when they share one goal; splitting them between owners and workers invites deadlock, slows decisions and imports collective bargaining into the boardroom. The same German evidence found no detectable effect on wages, which suggests board seats do little for workers that unions and labor law do not do better. Germany’s system grew from a postwar settlement, with sector-wide bargaining, works councils and bank-led finance, that the United States does not have. Investors who supply capital need assurance that it will be managed for a return; diluting their control raises the cost of capital, which ultimately falls on the jobs that capital creates.

Question: Should large asset managers use shareholder votes to press companies on climate and social issues?
Tag: value
Poles: Use the votes / Stick to returns
Why contested: BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street together vote about a quarter of the shares at S&P 500 companies on behalf of millions of savers who hold a wide range of political views. Whether those votes should press for climate targets, diversity or political-spending disclosure has become a partisan fight, with Republican states passing laws to restrict it.
Best case, use the votes: Climate change, workforce practices and political risk can affect the value of every company in a diversified portfolio, so a universal owner that ignores them is neglecting its clients’ money. Many savers also care about what their capital does: Oliver Hart and Luigi Zingales argue that companies and funds should maximize their investors’ welfare, including their social preferences, not just market value. Shareholder votes are the least coercive way to raise these issues: they are advisory, transparent and decided by the owners themselves.
Best case, stick to returns: Index fund managers are fiduciaries for savers of every political view and have no mandate to impose one side’s priorities on corporate America. Their votes concentrate power in a handful of firms that answer to no voter, which the State Financial Officers Foundation, Strive and Republican attorneys general describe as political activism with other people’s money. If savers want their values expressed, they can choose funds that do so or vote their own shares through pass-through programs; the default should be to vote for long-term value alone.

Question: Should shareholders keep a federal right to put proposals on company ballots?
Tag: value
Poles: Keep the federal right / Let states decide
Why contested: Rule 14a-8 has let small shareholders place resolutions on corporate ballots since 1942. The SEC proposed in September 2026 to rescind it, leaving the matter to state law and company bylaws, and Texas already lets companies require large holdings before a proposal can be filed.
Best case, keep the federal right: Shareholder proposals are cheap, advisory and often prescient: many governance practices now standard, such as annual election of directors and majority voting, began as shareholder proposals that management opposed. They give owners a way to raise risks, from climate to political spending, that boards prefer not to discuss. Without a federal floor, companies will pick the state rules that shield them most, and the channel through which stakeholder concerns reach the boardroom will close for all but the largest investors.
Best case, let states decide: Corporate governance is a matter of state law, and the SEC itself now argues that Rule 14a-8 exceeds its statutory authority. The rule has been used by a small number of repeat filers to push advocacy agendas that most shareholders reject, at real cost to companies that must answer them and to an agency that had to referee them. States and companies can write their own rules, and investors can choose where to put their money; Delaware’s bar has said it will consider how state law should respond.

## Positions

Camp: Serve stakeholders to build long-term value
Adherents: BlackRock, the world’s largest asset manager, in Larry Fink’s 2022 letter to chief executives: “Stakeholder capitalism is not about politics,” and “Stakeholder capitalism is all about delivering long-term, durable returns for shareholders” (https://www.blackrock.com/corporate/investor-relations/2022-larry-fink-ceo-letter). Business Roundtable, whose 2019 statement signed by 181 chief executives commits members to “deliver value to all” stakeholders, including “generating long-term value for shareholders” (https://www.businessroundtable.org/business-roundtable-redefines-the-purpose-of-a-corporation-to-promote-an-economy-that-serves-all-americans). Alex Edmans, London Business School, author of Grow the Pie: “By investing in stakeholders, a company doesn’t reduce investors’ slice of the pie... it grows the pie, ultimately benefiting investors” (https://www.london.edu/think/how-great-companies-deliver-both-purpose-and-profit). Also associated: the World Economic Forum’s Davos Manifesto 2020, the former Unilever chief executive Paul Polman, the law firm Wachtell, Lipton’s “New Paradigm” for corporate governance, and the UK’s “enlightened shareholder value” duty in section 172 of the Companies Act 2006.
Case: The supposed choice between shareholders and stakeholders is mostly false. A company creates value by serving customers, employing and developing people, working with suppliers and earning the trust of the communities that host it; profit is the result of doing those things well over many years, not a rival to them. Shareholders who own a company for the long run, which in an age of index funds and pensions means most shareholders, want exactly what its other stakeholders want: a durable, well-run business that does not court disaster by cutting corners. The job of leadership is to grow the pie rather than fight over its slices, and a clear purpose beyond profit is the best guide to doing that, because it tells employees and partners what the firm will and will not do when the rules are silent. Real trade-offs exist, and managers must make them with judgment, but they are rarer than the slogans on either side suggest. This camp wants no new laws: corporate law already lets boards take the long view, and the change needed is in how leaders and investors think, measure and pay.
Evidence: Edmans’s finding that companies with high employee satisfaction outperformed peers by 2.3 to 3.8 percent a year over 28 years; reviews of more than 1,000 studies (NYU Stern and Rockefeller Asset Management, 2021) and of more than 2,000 (Friede, Busch and Bassen, 2015) finding positive or neutral links between ESG and corporate performance far more often than negative ones; the cost to shareholders of stakeholder failures such as BP’s Deepwater Horizon spill, Volkswagen’s emissions scandal and Boeing’s 737 MAX crashes; and the UK experience, where directors have had a statutory duty since 2006 to have regard to employees, suppliers, communities and the environment while promoting the company’s success for its members.
Answer to objections: To shareholder-primacy critics who say this is either empty or a license for managers to do as they please, the camp answers that purpose is not a substitute for accountability but a way to make the long-term case measurable: companies should report on the stakeholder outcomes that drive their value and tie pay to them, and investors should judge managers over years, not quarters. To those who say voluntary commitments failed, citing the lack of board approval for the Business Roundtable statement and the retreat from ESG language since 2023, it replies that labels change but practices persist, that BlackRock and others dropped climate-alliance memberships under legal and political pressure rather than because the economics changed, and that mandating stakeholder seats or duties would import rigidities without fixing the underlying problem of short horizons.

Camp: Keep shareholder value as the corporate goal
Adherents: Strive Asset Management, an Ohio-based fund manager founded to counter ESG investing, in its 2025 Stewardship Report: “Shareholders are the owners of the corporation. Boards and executives are stewards of shareholder capital,” and companies that respect “shareholder primacy” are better positioned to compete (https://strive.com/documents/Strive_2025_Stewardship_Report.pdf). Lucian Bebchuk and Roberto Tallarita, Harvard Law School, “The Illusory Promise of Stakeholder Governance” (2020): “Stakeholderism would increase the insulation of corporate leaders from shareholders” and “would therefore be contrary to the interests of the stakeholders it purports to serve” (https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2020/03/02/the-illusory-promise-of-stakeholder-governance/). The classic statement is Milton Friedman’s 1970 New York Times Magazine essay, which calls a corporate executive “an employe of the owners of the business” whose responsibility is to make as much money as possible “while conforming to the basic rules of the society” (copy at https://business.pitt.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/25Right-Readings-Friedman-1.pdf). Also associated: the State Financial Officers Foundation and Republican state treasurers, and the Wall Street Journal editorial page.
Case: A corporation exists to make money for the people who put their capital at risk, and that clarity is what makes it useful to everyone else. Shareholders are paid last, after workers, suppliers, lenders and the tax authorities have received what they were promised, so maximizing the value of their claim tends to maximize the value the firm creates overall. Other groups are protected by contracts, by competition for their labor and custom, and by laws that apply to every firm alike; when those protections fall short, the answer is better law made by elected governments, not boardroom discretion. Telling managers to serve everyone frees them to serve themselves, since any decision can be justified by appeal to some stakeholder, and it invites them to impose their own politics on the owners’ money. The ESG era showed this: firms took public stances on contested questions, diverted shareholder resources to causes their customers and investors had never approved, and drew political backlash that hurt owners. The camp’s wings differ on mechanics: Bebchuk and other “shareholder empowerment” scholars want shareholders to have strong voting rights and see Rule 14a-8 as useful, while “director primacy” scholars such as Stephen Bainbridge, and the drafters of Texas’s and Nevada’s recent corporate laws would give boards more insulation from activist shareholders while keeping profit as the goal, a direction the SEC’s 2026 proposal to rescind the federal shareholder proposal rule also takes.
Evidence: Bebchuk and Tallarita’s finding that one of 48 responding Business Roundtable signatories had board approval to sign, and their study with Kobi Kastiel of more than 100 private-equity sales in states with constituency statutes, where leaders secured premiums for shareholders and payouts for themselves while rarely negotiating any protection for employees or other stakeholders; Raghunandan and Rajgopal’s finding (2024) that signatories had more environmental and labor violations than peers and did not change after signing; the low agreement among ESG ratings (correlations of 0.38 to 0.71); the ouster of Danone’s chief executive in 2021 after its turn to “mission” status; and the falling support for environmental and social proposals since 2021, which this camp reads as owners rejecting the agenda.
Answer to objections: To the charge that shareholder primacy breeds short-termism, the camp answers that a stock price is the market’s estimate of all future cash flows, that firms with long payoffs often command the highest valuations, and that net of share issuance the corporate sector’s payouts are about half of what gross figures suggest (Fried and Wang). To the charge that law is too slow and captured to protect stakeholders, it answers that managers are no more democratic or disinterested than legislators, and that a weak law is fixed by changing the law, not by giving unelected executives a roving mandate. To the charge that “shareholders” means the rich, it answers that the beneficiaries of returns include the pension funds and retirement accounts of the 62 percent of Americans who own stock.

Camp: Give stakeholders a legal share of corporate control
Adherents: Senator Elizabeth Warren, whose Accountable Capitalism Act, reintroduced in December 2024, would require companies with more than $1 billion in revenue to obtain a federal charter, have employees select at least 40 percent of the board, and oblige directors to consider the interests of all corporate stakeholders (https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warren-renews-fight-for-economic-growth-that-benefits-workers). The Roosevelt Institute, whose economist Lenore Palladino said “Stakeholder corporate governance is necessary for the kind of economic future we all want—and need” and which calls for multiple stakeholders to be represented on corporate boards (https://rooseveltinstitute.org/press-releases/the-call-to-replace-shareholder-primacy-reshaping-corporate-law-to-promote-workers-interests-a-better-economy/). B Lab UK’s Better Business Act campaign, backed by more than 3,000 organizations, which asks the UK to amend section 172 so that directors are legally required to balance shareholders’ interests with those of employees and other stakeholders (https://bcorporation.uk/news-stories-and-events/news/better-business-act-marks-20-years-of-companies-act-with-call-for-urgent-reform/). From the right, American Compass proposes letting workers elect board representatives through works councils or unions (https://americancompass.org/a-better-bargain-worker-voice-and-representation/). Also associated: Leo Strine, former chief justice of Delaware, Colin Mayer of Oxford and the British Academy’s Future of the Corporation program, the AFL-CIO, and advocates of employee and “steward” ownership such as Patagonia’s 2022 transfer of its voting shares to a trust.
Case: Shareholder primacy is not a law of nature but a choice written into corporate law and reinforced by pay packages and capital markets, and it can be rewritten. The voluntary version of stakeholder capitalism has been tested and found wanting: chief executives signed pledges without asking their boards, kept paying record buybacks, and dropped the language at the first political pressure. That is not because executives are hypocrites but because nothing in their legal duties, their pay or their electorate changed. Stakeholders who bear real risk in a firm, above all workers who cannot diversify their jobs as investors diversify their portfolios, should have real power in it: elected seats on the board, legal duties owed to them, or ownership itself. Europe shows this is workable: 14 of 30 European countries put workers on some company boards, and German firms with half their supervisory board elected by employees remain among the world’s most competitive exporters. The camp’s variants differ in instrument (worker-elected directors, new directors’ duties, federal charters, benefit-corporation status as the default, employee or foundation ownership) and in degree, from Warren’s 40 percent of seats to American Compass’s optional worker representatives.
Evidence: The Jäger, Schoefer and Heining study of German codetermination (2021), finding no harm to firms and slightly higher capital investment; Finnish evidence of productivity gains from worker board seats; the long record of German, Nordic and Dutch codetermination alongside high productivity; the failure of voluntary pledges documented by Bebchuk and Tallarita and by Raghunandan and Rajgopal; the concentration of stock ownership, with the top 1 percent of US households holding about half of household-held equities, which means shareholder primacy channels gains to a narrow group; and the decline in workers’ share of national income since 2000 in the United States.
Answer to objections: To the charge that divided boards would deadlock and raise the cost of capital, the camp points to decades of German and Nordic practice in which shareholders keep the tie-breaking vote and firms raise capital without difficulty. To the charge that the German evidence shows little wage gain, it answers that the purpose of voice is not only higher pay but a say over layoffs, plant closings, training and long-term investment, and that the evidence at least disproves the warning that worker voice would wreck firms. To the charge that stakeholder duties without a single metric free managers, it agrees, and says that is why stakeholders need enforceable seats and rights rather than vague pledges.

Camp: Let shareholders steer companies’ social choices
Adherents: The Interfaith Center on Corporate Responsibility, a coalition of more than 300 institutional investors representing over $4 trillion, which files shareholder resolutions on climate, human rights and labor and opposes rescinding Rule 14a-8; its senior policy adviser Tim Smith said “Proposals are potent corporate accountability tools that can flag risks” (https://www.iccr.org/in-comment-letter-to-the-sec-iccr-defends-shareholder-proposal-rule-as-a-critical-bulwark-of-corporate-accountability/). With As You Sow, ICCR sued the SEC over its November 2025 policy of no longer reviewing companies’ grounds for excluding shareholder proposals, arguing that “the right to present and vote on shareholder proposals is essential for investors” (https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/04/01/complaint-challenging-restrictions-on-shareholder-proposal-rights/). Oliver Hart of Harvard and Luigi Zingales of the University of Chicago, in “Companies Should Maximize Shareholder Welfare Not Market Value” (2017), argue that companies and asset managers should pursue policies consistent with the preferences of their investors, including their social preferences (https://hart.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/companies-should-maximize-shareholder-welfare-not-market-value). Also associated: Ceres, pension funds such as the New York City and California public employee systems, and pass-through voting programs such as BlackRock Voting Choice and Vanguard Investor Choice.
Case: Shareholders are not only claims on cash flow; they are people and institutions with values, and most of them, through pension funds and index funds, own a slice of the whole economy. A diversified owner loses when one company profits by imposing costs, such as emissions, on the others it owns, so “shareholder value” measured company by company can work against shareholders themselves. Friedman was right that managers should not spend owners’ money on causes of their own choosing, but the logical conclusion is to ask the owners, not to assume they care only about each firm’s share price. The proper channel is shareholder democracy: owners file resolutions, vote on them and hold directors to the results. That keeps the decision with the people whose money is at stake, avoids handing a roving mandate to managers, and avoids waiting for governments that may never act. The camp therefore defends the shareholder proposal rule, proxy advisers and investors’ freedom to consider ESG, and supports giving individual savers a say in how their funds vote.
Evidence: The history of shareholder proposals that began as minority demands and became standard practice, including majority voting for directors, board declassification and disclosure of political spending and greenhouse gas emissions; the majority votes for climate resolutions around 2021, including the election of three dissident directors to ExxonMobil’s board by the small fund Engine No. 1 that year; Hart and Zingales’s formal argument that when social harms cannot be separated from production, maximizing market value does not maximize shareholder welfare; and continued demand for sustainable funds in Europe, which took in $9.4 billion in the third quarter of 2026.
Answer to objections: To the shareholder-value camp’s charge that this is politics with other people’s money, it answers that resolutions are decided by the votes of the owners, that most are advisory, and that the decline in support since 2021 shows the system working, not failing: weak proposals lose. To the charge that a few repeat filers impose costs on companies, it answers that the cost of putting a proposal on a ballot is small relative to the risks they flag, and that removing the federal rule would let managers silence owners. To the stakeholder-law camp’s objection that shareholders’ values are no substitute for workers’ own voice, it replies that shareholder engagement is the channel available now under existing law, and that it can and does press companies on labor rights and supply chains.

## Crux

Crux: When what is best for a company’s shareholders and what is best for the people it affects pull apart, who should make the call: the market and the law from outside the firm, or the people who govern it from inside?

Every camp agrees that most of the time the interests line up, and that a well-run company treats its workers, customers and neighbors decently because that is how it lasts. The fight is over the remainder, the cases where honoring a stakeholder costs owners money. The shareholder-value camp says those cases belong to elected governments, which can bind every firm alike, and that handing them to boards frees managers from any clear test. The long-term-value camp says such cases are rarer than either side claims and that good leadership resolves most of them by taking the long view. The stakeholder-law camp says the people affected need their own power inside the firm because outside law is slow and shaped by the firms it governs. The shareholder-voice camp says the owners themselves should decide, by voting on what they want their companies to do.

What evidence could settle: how often genuine trade-offs between shareholders and stakeholders arise, and how large they are, as opposed to investments that pay off over a longer horizon; whether managers given formal latitude to serve stakeholders actually use it for them or for themselves, as studied in buyouts, benefit corporations and companies that signed purpose pledges; whether worker directors and stakeholder duties change layoffs, investment, wages and safety in ways that can be measured, as the German and Finnish evidence begins to show; whether external regulation in practice keeps up with corporate harms, or lags them because firms shape it; and whether shareholder votes on social and environmental questions lead to changes that later prove to have added or destroyed value.

What no data can close: whether a large corporation is at bottom its owners’ private property, run for them within the rules, or a social institution chartered by the public that owes duties to everyone who depends on it; how much weight to give democratic legitimacy, which favors leaving trade-offs to elected governments, against the speed and reach of private action when governments do not act; whether workers who invest their working lives in a firm have a claim to a say in it comparable to the claim of those who invest money; and whether it is acceptable for large investors to use savers’ votes to press companies toward goals some of those savers do not share.
