# Money in Politics — research

## Overview

This topic is about how much money people, companies, unions and organized groups may spend to win elections and influence government, and whether that spending is a form of free speech the state must protect or a source of corruption and unequal political power the state may limit. It covers contributions to candidates and parties; independent spending by super PACs and other outside groups; “dark money” from groups that do not disclose their donors; contribution limits, spending limits and disclosure rules; public financing, from matching funds and vouchers to tax checkoffs; the constitutional doctrine the US Supreme Court has built from Buckley v. Valeo (1976) through Citizens United v. FEC (2010), McCutcheon v. FEC (2014) and NRSC v. FEC (2026); enforcement by the Federal Election Commission; lobbying as a second channel of paid influence; and how other democracies, most of which cap spending or fund parties publicly, handle the same problem. Most of the evidence and the sharpest legal conflict are in the United States, so the page centers on it while drawing comparisons abroad. It deliberately excludes the design of electoral systems, districting and primaries (covered in the electoral system design topic), speech rules on social media platforms (covered in the content moderation topic), corporate market power in general (covered in the antitrust topic), and outright bribery, vote buying and foreign donations, which every camp agrees should be illegal.

Why now: on 30 June 2026 the US Supreme Court, in NRSC v. FEC, struck down by 6 to 3 the federal limits on how much a political party may spend in coordination with its own candidates, overruling its 2001 decision upholding them (Colorado II). Justice Kavanaugh wrote for the majority that the limits were not “proportionate, necessary, and narrowly tailored” given less restrictive tools such as earmarking and disclosure laws; Justice Kagan, dissenting for three justices, wrote that a donor “will be able to give a party as much as half a million dollars (as compared to the $7,000 he can give directly to the candidate) to cover the candidate’s bills,” and that the Court “ushers back in the same opportunities for quid pro quo corruption that the contribution limits were meant to check” (Supreme Court opinion, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-621_h315.pdf). The ruling lands in the middle of a midterm campaign that is already heavily financed by outside groups. At the end of 2025 the pro-Trump super PAC MAGA Inc. held about $304 million, the crypto industry’s Fairshake nearly $194 million and the AI industry’s new Leading the Future $39 million in cash (NBC News, 31 January 2026, https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/ai-crypto-trump-super-pacs-stash-millions-spend-midterms-rcna256622), and the ad-tracking firm AdImpact projected $10.8 billion in political ad spending for the 2026 cycle, which would be the most for any midterm (https://adimpact.com/political-projections-26). The agency that enforces federal campaign finance law has been unable to act on most of it: the Federal Election Commission has lacked the four commissioners it needs for a quorum since 1 May 2025 and had 195 pending enforcement matters by March 2026 (NOTUS, https://www.notus.org/money/federal-election-commission-fec-shutdown-case-backlog). In the states, Montana voters will decide on 3 November 2026 whether to bar corporations, unions and other “artificial persons” from spending on elections (Initiative 194; Montana Public Radio, https://www.mtpr.org/montana-news/2026-09-30/montana-plan-ballot-initiative-aims-to-end-dark-money-spending-in-montana), and a federal appeals court heard argument on 29 July 2026 over Maine’s voter-approved $5,000 cap on contributions to super PACs, which a lower court struck down in 2025 (WABI, https://www.wabi.tv/2026/07/29/federal-appeals-court-hears-arguments-over-voter-approved-limit-maine-super-pacs/).

The debate is live because public opinion and constitutional law point in different directions. In a 2023 Pew Research Center survey, 72 percent of Americans, including 71 percent of Republicans and 76 percent of Democrats, said there should be limits on how much individuals and organizations can spend on political campaigns (https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/10/23/7-facts-about-americans-views-of-money-in-politics/), while the Supreme Court has held since 1976 that spending money to communicate is protected speech and since 2010 that independent spending, including by corporations, does not give rise to corruption. Those who want to curb political money, including the Campaign Legal Center, the Brennan Center for Justice, American Promise and most Democratic lawmakers, joined in June 2026 by a Republican congressman, Tom Barrett of Michigan, argue that big donors buy access and distort policy and that the Constitution should be amended if necessary. Those who defend political spending as speech, including the Institute for Free Speech, the Cato Institute, Senator Mitch McConnell and the Republican Party’s campaign committees, argue that limits silence citizens, protect incumbents and invite government control of political debate. Between them, the American Civil Liberties Union opposes spending limits but wants public financing to give more people a voice, and a group of election law scholars, including Richard Pildes, Samuel Issacharoff and Bob Bauer of NYU’s Democracy Project, argue that the real problem is not how much money there is but that it flows to unaccountable outside groups instead of political parties.

Context: Outside spending is money spent to support or oppose candidates by groups that are legally independent of them, chiefly super PACs, as well as nonprofits, unions and corporations. It grew after two 2010 rulings: Citizens United v. FEC, which let corporations and unions spend unlimited sums on independent political advocacy, and SpeechNow.org v. FEC, a federal appeals court decision that led to super PACs, committees that may accept unlimited contributions as long as they do not coordinate with candidates. The FEC’s own count of independent expenditures in 2023–2024, which includes those by party committees, is $4.4 billion (FEC statistical summary, 23 April 2025, https://www.fec.gov/updates/statistical-summary-of-24-month-campaign-activity-of-the-2023-2024-election-cycle/). Those who want limits see the post-2010 jump as the direct result of the Court’s deregulation. Defenders of the rulings answer that outside spending also rose because candidates and parties remained capped, so money moved to the channel the law left open, and that outside groups often help challengers and political newcomers compete with incumbents.

Note: An individual may give a federal candidate at most $3,500 per election, or $7,000 across a primary and a general election, but may give a super PAC an unlimited amount.
Context: Limits on giving directly to candidates were set at $1,000 per election in 1974 and are now indexed for inflation in odd-numbered years. Until 30 June 2026 a party’s spending in coordination with its own nominee was also capped, at $65,300 for most House nominees in 2025 (Skadden, https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/07/nrsc-v-fec); the Supreme Court removed that cap in NRSC v. FEC. A donor can give each of a party’s national committees and their special accounts in a single year, which is why Justice Kagan’s dissent estimated a donor could now route “as much as half a million dollars” through a party to cover a candidate’s bills (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-621_h315.pdf). The majority answered that earmarking rules still treat money given to a party for a particular candidate as a contribution to that candidate.

## Questions

Question: Does campaign money change what governments do, or does it mostly follow candidates and policies voters already favor?
Type: empirical
Poles: Money buys policy / Money follows support
Why contested: Donors give to candidates who already agree with them and who are likely to win, so the correlation between money and outcomes cannot by itself show that money caused anything. Studies that try to isolate the effect use different methods, cover different offices and periods, and reach different conclusions, and the most direct form of influence, a quiet change in a bill’s wording or a regulator’s priorities, is the hardest to measure.
Case for “Money buys policy”: When the Supreme Court’s Citizens United ruling forced about half the states to drop their bans on corporate election spending, those states went on to adopt more corporate-friendly policies on issues with large effects on corporations’ welfare, and not on unrelated issues, which is what a causal effect would look like (Gilens, Patterson & Haines, American Political Science Review, 2021, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/campaign-finance-regulations-and-public-policy/B739B3AB64C90AE652CEE2FF6911883F). A randomized experiment found that congressional offices made senior policymakers available considerably more often when told a constituent meeting would include donors (Kalla & Broockman, American Journal of Political Science, 2016, https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8wm3k5g4), and access is where agendas are shaped. A study of 1,779 policy issues concluded that “economic elites and organized groups representing business interests have substantial independent impacts on U.S. government policy, while average citizens and mass-based interest groups have little or no independent influence” (Gilens & Page, Perspectives on Politics, 2014, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/testing-theories-of-american-politics-elites-interest-groups-and-average-citizens/62327F513959D0A304D4893B382B992B). Industries that spend heavily, from pharmaceuticals to crypto, would not keep doing so if it bought nothing.
Case for “Money follows support”: Most money goes to candidates who were going to win anyway, chiefly incumbents, and the best evidence on persuasion finds that “the best estimate of the effects of campaign contact and advertising on Americans’ candidates choices in general elections is zero” (Kalla & Broockman, American Political Science Review, 2018, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/minimal-persuasive-effects-of-campaign-contact-in-general-elections-evidence-from-49-field-experiments/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433). Economists Stephen Ansolabehere, John de Figueiredo and James Snyder found that legislators’ votes are explained mainly by party, ideology and constituency, with little left for contributions, and that most giving looks like consumption by ideologically motivated individuals rather than investment by firms (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2003, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F089533003321164976). Critics of the Gilens and Page study, such as Peter Enns (Perspectives on Politics, 2015), show that rich and middle-income Americans want the same thing on most issues, so the middle usually gets its way. Big spenders lose often: the candidate backed by Elon Musk in the 2025 Wisconsin Supreme Court race lost, as did Jeb Bush in the 2016 Republican primaries despite the heavily funded super PAC behind him.

Question: Is spending money to spread a political message a form of speech the government must leave alone?
Type: value
Poles: Spending is speech / Money is property
Why contested: Almost every way of reaching voters, from printing leaflets to buying airtime, costs money, so a limit on spending is in practice a limit on how much one can say. But the same money can buy gratitude and access from officeholders, and a rule that treats dollars as speech gives people with more dollars more speech. The Supreme Court has held since Buckley v. Valeo (1976) that spending on political communication is protected, and most of the debate since has been over what follows from that.
Case for “Spending is speech”: “A restriction on the amount of money a person or group can spend on political communication during a campaign necessarily reduces the quantity of expression by restricting the number of issues discussed, the depth of their exploration, and the size of the audience reached,” as the Court said in Buckley and as Senator Mitch McConnell’s 2025 brief in NRSC v. FEC quoted (https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/24/24-621/371978/20250828091516676_24-621%20Amicus%20Brief.pdf). Newspapers, books and documentaries all cost money; Citizens United itself began when the government said a nonprofit could not distribute a film criticizing Hillary Clinton close to an election. If government can cap political spending, those in power decide how much criticism of themselves is allowed, and the people who write the caps are incumbents with every reason to keep challengers quiet. The remedy for speech one dislikes is more speech, not enforced silence.
Case for “Money is property”: Money enables speech but is not itself speech, any more than a gun is self-defense. Justice John Paul Stevens, dissenting in Citizens United, wrote that “a democracy cannot function effectively when its constituent members believe laws are being bought and sold” (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/08-205.ZX.html). Most democracies, including Canada, the United Kingdom and France, cap campaign spending or contributions without anyone regarding them as unfree, and the UK bans paid political advertising on television altogether. Treating unlimited spending as protected speech converts economic inequality directly into political inequality, letting a single donor such as Elon Musk, who gave more than $290 million in 2024, outspeak millions of citizens combined. Reasonable limits that apply to everyone equally leave every voice audible.

Question: Should the only legitimate reason to regulate political money be preventing quid pro quo corruption, or may the law also aim at political equality?
Type: value
Poles: Only quid pro quo / Equal voice too
Why contested: The Supreme Court has held that the only interest strong enough to justify campaign finance limits is preventing “quid pro quo” corruption, an explicit exchange of money for official acts, or its appearance, and has rejected “leveling the playing field” as a goal (McCutcheon v. FEC, 2014; NRSC v. FEC, 2026). Reformers want a broader idea of corruption, or an outright equality interest, and the constitutional amendments they propose would write one in.
Case for “Only quid pro quo”: A government that may limit speech in order to equalize influence must decide whose voice is too loud, and that power will be used by the people who hold office. In Buckley the Court called the idea that government may restrict the speech of some to enhance the relative voice of others “wholly foreign to the First Amendment” (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/424/1). Influence that comes from persuading voters, organizing allies or funding ads is how democratic politics works; corruption is the exchange of official acts for money, and bribery laws and contribution limits already target it. Equality-based limits would logically reach newspapers, celebrities, unions and activists, all of whom have more influence than ordinary citizens.
Case for “Equal voice too”: Corruption in the founders’ sense, as Lawrence Lessig and Zephyr Teachout have argued, is not only the bribe but the dependence of representatives on a small class of funders rather than on the people; members of Congress spend large parts of their week raising money from the roughly 1 percent of Americans who give more than $200. A legislature that answers to donors before voters is corrupt even if no single exchange can be proved. Political equality, one person one vote, is a founding democratic value, and a system that lets money multiply some citizens’ voices by millions violates it as plainly as a poll tax would.

Question: Did Citizens United and the rise of super PACs change who wins elections?
Type: empirical
Poles: Changed outcomes / Mostly moved money
Why contested: Outside spending grew more than twelvefold between 2008 and 2024 (OpenSecrets), but total spending, polarization and online fundraising grew at the same time, and both parties have benefited from outside money in different years, which makes the ruling’s own effect hard to separate.
Case for “Changed outcomes”: States that had banned corporate and union independent spending before 2010, and were forced by Citizens United to allow it, saw Republicans’ probability of winning state house races rise by “approximately 4 percentage points overall and 10 or more percentage points in several states” (Klumpp, Mialon & Williams, Journal of Law and Economics, 2016, https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/jle/vol59/iss1/1/). Policy in those states also shifted toward business preferences (Gilens, Patterson & Haines, 2021). Super PACs let a handful of donors keep a candidate alive who would otherwise have dropped out, as single-donor super PACs did for several 2012 Republican presidential candidates, and the threat of a super PAC spending against a legislator shapes votes even when the money is never spent.
Case for “Mostly moved money”: Most of what super PACs do, chiefly television ads, has small and fast-fading effects on votes, and super PAC money is spread across both parties: in 2024 the largest super PACs included Democratic as well as Republican groups, and dark money favored Democrats by nearly two to one (Brennan Center). The candidate who spent less won the presidency in 2016, and well-funded campaigns lose regularly. Much of the money that now flows through super PACs would otherwise have flowed through party “soft money” accounts, 527 groups and issue ads, as it did before 2010; Citizens United changed the channel more than the total.

Question: Do contribution and spending limits protect incumbents and push money toward less accountable groups?
Type: empirical
Poles: Limits backfire / Limits work
Why contested: Limits are written by sitting legislators, and every major tightening of the rules has been followed by money finding a new route, from soft money in the 1990s to 527 groups in 2004 and super PACs after 2010. Reformers reply that the leaks come from court decisions and weak enforcement, not from limits as such.
Case for “Limits backfire”: Challengers need to spend more than incumbents to become known, so caps on spending or fundraising help the people already in office, a finding political scientists such as Gary Jacobson documented in the 1970s and 1980s. When the law capped what parties could spend with their candidates, donors did not stop giving; they gave to super PACs and nonprofits instead, which are less accountable and more ideological. As Samuel Issacharoff wrote after NRSC v. FEC, “money under pressure does not disappear, it finds the path of least resistance” (Democracy Project, 1 July 2026, https://democracyproject.org/posts/a-welcome-correction-nrsc-v-fec-and-the-need-to-empower-parties). Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner argue, from state-level evidence, that rules which restrict party fundraising push money toward ideological donors and produce more polarized legislatures (Campaign Finance and Political Polarization, University of Michigan Press, 2015, https://press.umich.edu/Books/C/Campaign-Finance-and-Political-Polarization2).
Case for “Limits work”: Contribution limits on candidates, upheld since 1976, have kept direct giving to candidates small and transparent, and the places where money pours in unchecked, super PACs and dark money nonprofits, are exactly the ones courts and the FEC left unlimited. Ohio’s largest corruption case shows what unlimited, undisclosed money can buy: FirstEnergy funneled about $60 million through a 501(c)(4) nonprofit to elect allies of House Speaker Larry Householder and pass a $1 billion bailout, and Householder was sentenced to 20 years in prison in 2023 (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/former-gop-ohio-house-speaker-sentenced-to-20-years-for-role-in-60m-bribery-scheme). Countries such as Canada and the United Kingdom cap spending and still see incumbents lose regularly. The leaks show the need to close loopholes, not to give up on limits.

Question: Should the donors behind political spending be publicly disclosed?
Type: value
Poles: Disclose all donors / Protect donor privacy
Why contested: Eight of nine justices in Citizens United endorsed disclosure as the alternative to limits, and disclosure is the one tool most reformers and many deregulation advocates have accepted. But the Court has also protected the anonymity of members of controversial groups since NAACP v. Alabama (1958), and in Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta (2021) struck down California’s demand for charities’ donor lists. Groups that do not disclose donors spent an estimated $1.9 billion on the 2024 federal elections (Brennan Center).
Case for “Disclose all donors”: Voters judge a message partly by who is paying for it, and an ad from “Citizens for a Better Tomorrow” means something different when the money comes from one industry. Justice Kennedy’s majority in Citizens United relied on disclosure to let “citizens and shareholders” hold corporations and elected officials accountable, and Justice Scalia wrote in Doe v. Reed (2010) that “requiring people to stand up in public for their political acts fosters civic courage, without which democracy is doomed” (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/09-559.ZC4.html). Without disclosure, contribution limits and foreign money bans cannot be enforced, and the Ohio FirstEnergy scandal ran through an undisclosed nonprofit. Thresholds can protect small donors while exposing the large ones who matter.
Case for “Protect donor privacy”: Disclosure exposes donors to boycotts, harassment and job loss for supporting unpopular causes, which is why the Court protected the NAACP’s Alabama membership list during the civil rights movement. The Institute for Free Speech and Americans for Prosperity argue that in a polarized era, a public list of donors to any contested cause becomes a target list, and the chilling falls hardest on ordinary people who cannot afford retaliation. The anonymous Federalist Papers were political speech too. Voters can judge an argument on its merits, and the government does not need a database of citizens’ political associations.

Question: Does public financing make politics more representative, or does it spend taxpayers’ money without changing who participates?
Type: empirical
Poles: Broadens participation / Changes little
Why contested: Fifteen states, Washington, D.C., and cities such as New York and Seattle run public financing programs (NCSL), with designs that differ in ways that matter: grants, multiple matching of small gifts, vouchers. Evaluations of the same program can disagree, depending on whether they compare voucher users with past cash donors or with the electorate as a whole.
Case for “Broadens participation”: In Seattle’s first voucher election in 2017, 20,727 residents used their democracy vouchers, more than double the number who gave cash to local campaigns, and voucher users were “significantly more economically diverse than cash contributors”: households earning over $100,000 made up nearly a quarter of cash donors but 16 percent of voucher users, though the lowest-income residents remained underrepresented, according to researchers Brian McCabe and Jennifer Heerwig (Brennan Center summary, https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/democracy-vouchers-broadened-seattles-2017-donor-base). A Brennan Center and Campaign Finance Institute study found that small donors to New York City Council candidates, whose gifts the city matched, came from a much broader range of neighborhoods than small donors to the same city’s State Assembly candidates, who had no matching funds (Donor Diversity Through Public Matching Funds, https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/donor-diversity-through-public-matching-funds). Matching makes a $50 donor worth courting, which changes whom candidates spend their time with, and lets candidates without wealthy networks run.
Case for “Changes little”: A 2024 study of Seattle using individual voter records found that “those most mobilized by the availability of vouchers belong to groups already overrepresented within the donor pool,” across race, income, age, past participation and partisanship (Yorgason, American Political Science Review, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/campaign-finance-vouchers-do-not-expand-the-diversity-of-donors-evidence-from-seattle/BD8E21A4B646DE4EA56BF8787DF0FF81). The federal presidential program has withered: only about 3 percent of taxpayers now check the box to fund it, and no major-party nominee has taken its general-election grant since 2008 (FEC). Public financing compels taxpayers to fund candidates they may oppose, and outside spending, which public money cannot cap, still dominates competitive races.

Question: Should political parties be freed to raise and spend more than outside groups?
Type: empirical
Poles: Strengthen the parties / Parties as conduits
Why contested: The Supreme Court’s NRSC v. FEC ruling on 30 June 2026 removed the caps on what parties may spend in coordination with their candidates. Some reform-minded scholars welcomed it as a way to pull money back from super PACs into accountable, coalition-building parties; other reformers, and the three dissenting justices, see parties as a pipe through which large donors can now pay candidates’ bills.
Case for “Strengthen the parties”: Parties must win majorities across many districts, so they favor electable, relatively moderate candidates and answer to voters for a whole ticket; super PACs and single-issue groups answer to a few donors. Capping party coordination while leaving outside groups unlimited “systematically disadvantaged parties relative to every other player in the campaign finance ecosystem,” Samuel Issacharoff wrote, producing a system “increasingly dominated by single-issue and candidate-specific vehicles with no stake in governing coalitions” (https://democracyproject.org/posts/a-welcome-correction-nrsc-v-fec-and-the-need-to-empower-parties). Raymond La Raja and Jonathan Rauch argue that “laws can’t do much to reduce the amount of money in politics; what they can change is where the money goes” (Brookings, 2016, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/want-to-reduce-the-influence-of-super-pacs-strengthen-state-parties/). A majority of states largely let parties coordinate with their nominees, and the Institute for Free Speech says “there is no evidence of the corruption the federal government fears” (https://www.ifs.org/news/institute-for-free-speech-applauds-supreme-court-ruling-in-campaign-finance-case/).
Case for “Parties as conduits”: A candidate can now ask a donor to give a party far more than the $7,000 the donor could give the candidate directly, knowing it will pay the candidate’s bills; Justice Kagan put the figure at “as much as half a million dollars” and said the Court “ushers back in the same opportunities for quid pro quo corruption that the contribution limits were meant to check” (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-621_h315.pdf). Earmarking rules are hard to enforce, especially with an FEC that has lacked a quorum since May 2025. Parties today are themselves dominated by large donors and joint fundraising committees, so routing more money through them strengthens the donors, not the voters.

Question: Should corporations and unions have the same right to spend on elections as individual citizens?
Type: value
Poles: Same rights / Citizens only
Why contested: Citizens United held that the First Amendment does not allow the government to restrict independent political spending based on the speaker’s corporate identity. Montana’s Initiative 194, on the ballot on 3 November 2026, would instead deny corporations, unions and other “artificial persons” the power to spend on elections at all, by redefining what state-chartered entities may do, and proposed constitutional amendments, including one introduced in June 2026 by Republican Tom Barrett, would let legislatures distinguish people from “artificial entities.”
Case for “Same rights”: Corporations are associations of people, shareholders, workers and members, and so are unions, advocacy nonprofits like the ACLU and the NRA, and newspapers, most of which are corporations. A rule that lets the government silence corporate speakers would let it silence the Sierra Club and the New York Times as well as Exxon. The First Amendment protects speech, not speakers, and voters are capable of weighing who is talking. Montana’s Republican Party opposes the initiative on the ground that it would silence small Montana businesses with a stake in the outcome of state elections (Montana Public Radio, https://www.mtpr.org/montana-news/2026-09-30/montana-plan-ballot-initiative-aims-to-end-dark-money-spending-in-montana).
Case for “Citizens only”: Corporations are created by state law for economic purposes and given privileges, limited liability, perpetual life, favorable treatment of assets, that let them amass wealth no individual could, and using that wealth to elect the officials who regulate them is a distortion, not an exercise of citizenship. Shareholders and workers did not consent to their company’s political spending and have their own votes and voices. Montana itself banned corporate election spending from 1912, after copper companies bought legislators, until the Supreme Court struck its law down in 2012 on the authority of Citizens United, and that history is the model for today’s initiative.

## Positions

Camp: Limit political money and overturn Citizens United
Adherents: Senator Adam Schiff of California, who on 17 September 2025 introduced the Citizens Over Corporations Amendment with Representatives Joe Neguse, Jim McGovern and Summer Lee, saying “the Supreme Court’s decision in Citizens United opened the door for corporations to pour billions of dollars of dark money into elections” and that the amendment “will keep big money out of politics and restore power where it should belong — with the American people” (https://www.schiff.senate.gov/news/press-releases/news-sen-schiff-reps-neguse-mcgovern-and-lee-introduce-constitutional-amendment-to-overturn-citizens-united/). American Promise, a cross-partisan group campaigning for the For Our Freedom Amendment, which says “the problem of money in politics is constitutional” and that 25 states have called on Congress to propose an amendment (https://americanpromise.net/); it backed the near-identical Constitutional Campaign Finance Reform Amendment introduced on 4 June 2026 by Republican Representative Tom Barrett of Michigan, who said “Americans are tired of watching billions of dollars flood into our elections while their own voices feel increasingly ignored” (https://barrett.house.gov/media/press-releases/barrett-launches-fifth-pillar-blueprint-better-america-reform-campaign-finance). The Campaign Legal Center defended the coordinated-spending limits in NRSC v. FEC (https://campaignlegal.org/cases-actions/defending-limits-coordinated-spending-political-parties), and the Brennan Center for Justice filed in support of Maine’s super PAC limit (https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/dinner-table-action-v-schneider).
Case: Democracy rests on political equality: one person, one vote, and a government that answers to the people rather than to whoever can pay. Fifty years of Supreme Court decisions have taken that principle apart. The Court decided in 1976 that spending money is speech, in 2010 that corporations may spend without limit and that independent spending cannot corrupt, and in 2026 that parties may serve as a pass-through for donors who have already given candidates the legal maximum. The result is what any voter can see: about 1 percent of Americans fund campaigns, single billionaires spend hundreds of millions of dollars, industries form super PACs to choose the lawmakers who will regulate them, and nonprofits that hide their donors spent almost $2 billion in one election. Officeholders spend their days raising money from that donor class, and they listen to it. Every other established democracy accepts reasonable limits on political money without losing its freedom; the United States can too. Because the Court has closed the door to ordinary legislation, the Constitution should be amended to let Congress and the states set reasonable limits on contributions and spending and to distinguish people from corporations, while the states test the limits of current law through measures like Maine’s cap on super PAC donations and Montana’s ban on corporate election spending.
Evidence: 72 percent of Americans, including 71 percent of Republicans, say there should be limits on how much individuals and organizations can spend on campaigns, and 80 percent say campaign donors have too much influence on Congress (Pew Research Center, 2023, https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/10/23/7-facts-about-americans-views-of-money-in-politics/). After Citizens United forced states to allow corporate independent spending, those states adopted more corporate-friendly policy on issues that affect corporate profits (Gilens, Patterson & Haines, American Political Science Review, 2021, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/campaign-finance-regulations-and-public-policy/B739B3AB64C90AE652CEE2FF6911883F). Outside spending rose from $338 million in 2008 to $4.2 billion in 2024 (OpenSecrets, https://www.opensecrets.org/outside-spending/by_cycle). Maine voters approved a $5,000 cap on contributions to super PACs with about 75 percent of the vote in 2024 (Brennan Center, https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/dinner-table-action-v-schneider). In Ohio, about $60 million routed through an undisclosed nonprofit bought a $1 billion utility bailout and a 20-year prison sentence for the House speaker who delivered it (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/former-gop-ohio-house-speaker-sentenced-to-20-years-for-role-in-60m-bribery-scheme).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that limits on spending are limits on speech, the camp answers that no one proposes to stop anyone from speaking, only to cap how much money one person or corporation can pour into amplifying a message, as contribution limits already do and as courts have upheld since 1976; a level of political spending that drowns out other citizens is not more speech but less democracy. To the objection that limits protect incumbents, it answers that today’s unlimited system protects them far more, since incumbents attract most of the money, and that well-designed limits paired with public financing help challengers. To the objection that money simply flows to new channels, it answers that the channels exist because courts opened them, which is why a constitutional amendment rather than another statute is needed.
Variation: The camp agrees on the goal and divides on instruments and reach. Some push a constitutional amendment, either broad (the For Our Freedom Amendment, the Democracy for All Amendment backed by most Democrats) or aimed mainly at corporations (Schiff’s Citizens Over Corporations Amendment); others test current law through state measures, from Maine’s cap on super PAC contributions to Montana’s Initiative 194, which would bar corporations, unions and nonprofits from election spending by redefining their state-granted powers rather than limiting speech; others focus on disclosure, such as the federal DISCLOSE Act. Most adherents also support public financing as a complement to limits, and some, such as Lawrence Lessig’s Equal Citizens, see candidates’ dependence on funders, rather than any single ruling, as the core problem.

Camp: Protect political spending as free speech
Adherents: The Institute for Free Speech, founded by former FEC chairman Bradley Smith, which called NRSC v. FEC “a landmark victory for the First Amendment and for the millions of Americans who exercise their political rights through the parties they support” (https://www.ifs.org/news/institute-for-free-speech-applauds-supreme-court-ruling-in-campaign-finance-case/). Senator Mitch McConnell of Kentucky, lead plaintiff against the 2002 McCain-Feingold law, whose 2025 brief in NRSC v. FEC argued that “money is essential to extend a political campaign beyond the soapbox” and that the federal limits form a “sprawling regulatory edifice” whose “load-bearing pillars” have been “toppled by the First Amendment” (https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/24/24-621/371978/20250828091516676_24-621%20Amicus%20Brief.pdf). The Cato Institute, whose brief in the same case argued that the limits “do not prevent corruption; instead, they prevent parties and candidates from exercising their constitutional right to speak and work together” (https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/24/24-621/373002/20250828111459411_NRSC%20v%20FEC_Final.pdf). The Republican Party’s Senate and House campaign committees, which brought the case.
Case: The First Amendment was written to protect political speech above all, and in the modern world political speech costs money: printing, postage, airtime, staff, websites. A law that caps what a citizen, a union or an advocacy group may spend to reach voters is a law that caps how much they may say, and it is written by the incumbents whose records that speech would criticize. The Supreme Court has rightly held that the only valid reason to regulate political money is to prevent the exchange of money for official acts, and bribery laws, contribution limits on candidates and earmarking rules already do that. Trying to equalize influence is both impossible and dangerous, because it gives the government power to decide which voices are too loud, a power that will be used against unpopular causes, outsiders and the opposition. The “money in politics” problem is mostly a symptom of how much power government holds over the economy; the way to reduce the incentive to spend on politics is to limit what politicians can sell, not what citizens can say. Voters, not regulators, are the right judges of whether a message is persuasive or a donor’s influence is too great.
Evidence: The candidate who spends the most usually wins because donors back likely winners: experimental evidence finds that the average persuasive effect of campaign contact and advertising in general elections is zero (Kalla & Broockman, American Political Science Review, 2018, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/minimal-persuasive-effects-of-campaign-contact-in-general-elections-evidence-from-49-field-experiments/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433), and Elon Musk’s heavily funded candidate lost the 2025 Wisconsin Supreme Court race. Legislators’ votes are explained mainly by party, ideology and constituency rather than contributions (Ansolabehere, de Figueiredo & Snyder, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2003, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F089533003321164976). A majority of states “largely give parties free rein to make coordinated expenditures on behalf of their state-level nominees,” and the Supreme Court in NRSC v. FEC treated the absence of evidence of resulting corruption as “a strong sign” the federal concern was too speculative (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-621_h315.pdf). Every major tightening of federal law, from the 1974 amendments to McCain-Feingold in 2002, was followed by money moving into less accountable channels such as soft money, 527 groups and super PACs. In 2024 dark money favored Democrats by nearly two to one (Brennan Center), evidence that unlimited spending is a tool of both sides rather than of one class or party.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that unlimited spending lets a wealthy few drown out everyone else, the camp answers that spending does not silence anyone, that no one is forced to listen, and that large spenders on both sides cancel out and frequently lose; the cure for speech one dislikes is more speech, which the internet has made cheaper than ever, as the record sums raised in small online donations show. To the polls showing most Americans want limits, it answers that the First Amendment exists to protect speech from majorities, and that support for limits in the abstract does not mean support for silencing a particular union, church or advocacy group. To the Ohio bribery case, it answers that bribery was already a crime and was prosecuted, which shows the existing law works against actual corruption.
Variation: The camp divides on disclosure. The Institute for Free Speech and Americans for Prosperity oppose compelled disclosure of donors to advocacy groups as a threat to privacy and association, citing NAACP v. Alabama (1958) and the Court’s 2021 ruling in Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta; others in the camp, following Justice Kennedy’s opinion in Citizens United, accept disclosure as the right alternative to limits. It also divides on how far to go: some, like Justice Thomas, would subject contribution limits too to strict scrutiny and strike most of them, while others accept the base limits on giving to candidates, which the NRSC majority left in place. Most of the camp opposes taxpayer-funded campaigns.

Camp: Expand public financing without capping spending
Adherents: The American Civil Liberties Union, which opposes campaign finance rules “premised on the notion that the answer to money in politics is to ban political speech” and “will firmly oppose any constitutional amendment that would limit the free speech clause of the First Amendment,” while holding that “the answer to concerns over the escalating cost of political campaigns is to expand, not limit, the resources available for political advocacy” and supporting “a comprehensive and meaningful system of public financing” (https://www.aclu.org/issues/free-speech/campaign-finance-reform; https://www.aclu.org/documents/aclu-and-citizens-united). Michael Malbin, a University at Albany political scientist and former executive director of the Campaign Finance Institute, whose 2021 article “steps away from the speech-and-corruption debates dominating campaign finance since Buckley v. Valeo” and argues for public financing targeted at small, in-district donors, as adopted in New York State (University of Pennsylvania Journal of Constitutional Law, https://con.law/print-articles/a-neo-madisonian-perspective-on-campaign-finance-reform-institutions-pluralism-and-small-donors).
Case: The trouble with American campaign finance is not that some people speak too much but that most people are not heard at all. About 1 percent of adults give more than $200 in a federal cycle, so candidates spend their time with that 1 percent. Capping spending will not fix this: the courts will strike the caps down, and even where caps survive, money finds another route, while the caps themselves restrict real political speech, including that of unpopular and minority causes the ACLU has spent a century defending. The better answer is to raise the floor rather than lower the ceiling. Multiply small contributions with public matching funds, give every voter a voucher or a tax credit to give to the candidates they choose, and a candidate who courts thousands of ordinary donors can compete with one who courts a few rich ones. That approach adds voices instead of subtracting them, is clearly constitutional, and changes whom candidates spend their days talking to.
Evidence: Fifteen states and Washington, D.C., offer some form of public financing (NCSL, https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/public-financing-of-campaigns-overview). In Seattle’s first voucher election in 2017, 20,727 residents used democracy vouchers, more than double the number who gave cash, and users were significantly more economically diverse than cash donors, according to researchers Brian McCabe and Jennifer Heerwig (Brennan Center, https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/democracy-vouchers-broadened-seattles-2017-donor-base). A Brennan Center and Campaign Finance Institute study found that New York City’s matched small donors came from a much broader range of neighborhoods than small donors to unmatched State Assembly races (https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/donor-diversity-through-public-matching-funds). The Supreme Court has upheld voluntary public financing since Buckley (1976), striking down only the “trigger” provisions that boosted a candidate’s public funds in response to an opponent’s spending (Arizona Free Enterprise Club v. Bennett, 2011).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that public money cannot compete with super PACs and billionaires, the camp answers that it does not need to outspend them, only to give every serious candidate enough to be heard and a reason to court ordinary voters, and that a candidate free of dependence on large donors governs differently even when outside groups spend against them. To evidence such as a 2024 study finding Seattle’s vouchers mobilized mainly groups already overrepresented among donors (Yorgason, American Political Science Review, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/campaign-finance-vouchers-do-not-expand-the-diversity-of-donors-evidence-from-seattle/BD8E21A4B646DE4EA56BF8787DF0FF81), it answers that design and outreach matter and that even a donor pool similar in composition is far larger and less dependent on a few. To the objection that taxpayers should not fund candidates they oppose, it answers that democracy is a public good like courts and elections themselves, and that the cost is a small fraction of what large donors receive in policy.
Variation: Adherents differ on whether public financing should come with spending or contribution limits for participating candidates, as most state programs require, and on design: full grants (Arizona, Maine, Connecticut), multiple matching of small donations (New York City and New York State), vouchers (Seattle) or tax credits and rebates for small donors, which some conservative reformers favor. Many supporters of limits in the first camp also back public financing; what distinguishes this camp is that it rejects caps on independent spending and a constitutional amendment to allow them.

Camp: Route political money through the parties
Adherents: The Democracy Project, an NYU-based program co-directed by Samuel Issacharoff, Richard Pildes and Bob Bauer, former White House counsel to President Obama; Issacharoff wrote on 1 July 2026 that NRSC v. FEC is “a welcome and overdue correction” and that capping party coordination while leaving outside spenders free produced “not less money in politics but the same money flowing through different channels — channels far less accountable and far less programmatic than parties themselves” (https://democracyproject.org/posts/a-welcome-correction-nrsc-v-fec-and-the-need-to-empower-parties); the Court’s opinion quoted Bauer’s observation that “even many in the political reform community support an end to the limits” (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-621_h315.pdf). Political scientist Raymond La Raja of the University of Massachusetts Amherst and Jonathan Rauch of the Brookings Institution, who argue that “laws can’t do much to reduce the amount of money in politics; what they can change is where the money goes,” and that strengthening state parties “can help offset the clout of super PACs” (Brookings, 2016, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/want-to-reduce-the-influence-of-super-pacs-strengthen-state-parties/).
Case: The usual debate asks how much money there should be in politics; it is the wrong question. Money, like water, will flow into the system whatever the law says, as every round of reform has shown. What law can decide is where it goes. Parties are the one political organization that must build majorities across many districts, answer to voters for a whole ticket, and govern afterward; they favor candidates who can win general elections, which pushes them toward the center. Super PACs, single-candidate committees and dark money nonprofits answer to a handful of donors and to ideological activists, and they have grown because the law capped parties while leaving them free. The result has been weaker parties, more extreme candidates, more polarization and less accountability. The fix is to let parties raise and spend more, and coordinate with their own candidates, so that money moves back into organizations that voters can hold responsible, while keeping limits and disclosure aimed at genuine quid pro quo deals.
Evidence: Rules that restrict party fundraising push money toward ideological individual donors and groups and are associated with more polarized state legislatures, according to Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner (La Raja & Schaffner, Campaign Finance and Political Polarization, University of Michigan Press, 2015, https://press.umich.edu/Books/C/Campaign-Finance-and-Political-Polarization2). Super PACs spent about $2.7 billion on independent expenditures in 2024, roughly as much as all party committees disbursed (FEC, https://www.fec.gov/updates/statistical-summary-of-24-month-campaign-activity-of-the-2023-2024-election-cycle/). A majority of states largely let parties coordinate with their own nominees, without evidence of resulting quid pro quo corruption, as the Supreme Court noted in NRSC v. FEC. Other democracies channel most campaign money through parties, often with public funding attached.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection, pressed by Justice Kagan’s dissent, that parties will become conduits for donors to pay candidates’ bills with sums far above the candidate limits, the camp answers that earmarking rules still forbid directing party donations to a particular candidate, that party money is pooled across many races and donors, which dilutes any single donor’s leverage, and that a party is a more accountable broker than a super PAC funded by one billionaire. To the objection that parties are themselves captured by large donors, it answers that they must still answer to primary and general-election voters across the country in a way a single-issue group never does, and that public matching funds for small donors can be directed to parties as well as candidates.
Variation: The camp includes scholars who otherwise favor regulation, such as Bauer, a Democratic election lawyer, alongside Republicans who wanted the coordination limits struck for free-speech reasons. Some would pair stronger parties with lower limits on super PACs if the courts allowed it; others, like Malbin in the previous camp, accept the diagnosis of fragmentation but warn against enhancing national party leaders and favor empowering small donors instead.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others is whether concentrated political money distorts representation enough to justify limiting how much people and organizations may spend to be heard. Every camp opposes bribery, wants voters informed and wants officials to answer to the public; they divide over whether large donations and outside spending are the system working, as citizens supporting the causes they believe in, or the system failing, as wealth buying a louder voice and a closer ear than votes can.

The part evidence could settle: how much money changes election results, legislators’ votes and agendas, and public policy, once one accounts for donors giving to candidates and causes that were already likely to win; whether contribution and spending limits reduce the influence of large donors or mainly push money into super PACs, dark money groups and other less accountable channels; whether routing more money through parties moderates politics; and whether matching funds and vouchers change who gives and whom candidates listen to. Natural experiments such as the states forced to change their rules by Citizens United, field experiments on access and persuasion, and city and state public financing programs have begun to answer these questions, with mixed results.

The part no data can close: whether political equality, each citizen having a roughly equal chance to be heard, is a goal important enough to justify restricting political speech, or whether freedom to speak and spend on politics must come first because any authority that rations political speech can be turned against its critics; whether spending money to amplify a message is itself speech or only a means to it; whether corporations, unions and other associations should share individuals’ political rights; and whether these questions belong to elected legislatures and voters or to courts enforcing the Constitution against them.
