# Policing Reform — research

## Overview

This topic is about what police should do and how they are held to account. It covers the size and funding of police forces; which problems police should answer and which should go to other responders, such as mental-health crisis teams; how officers are trained, supervised and disciplined; the legal tools for holding them answerable when they cause harm, including criminal prosecution, civil lawsuits and the qualified immunity doctrine, decertification, civilian oversight and federal consent decrees; the role of police unions; and the proposals at the far ends of the debate, from expanding proactive enforcement to abolishing police departments. The debate is overwhelmingly American, because the United States has about 17,500 separate state and local police agencies and far more killings by police than other rich democracies, but it draws on comparisons with countries such as England and Wales, where most officers do not carry guns. It deliberately excludes the purposes of punishment, sentencing, prisons and the death penalty (covered in the crime and punishment reform topic), firearm regulation (covered in the gun policy topic), the legal status of drugs (covered in the drug policy topic), and immigration enforcement as a policy question in its own right, though the conduct and accountability of federal immigration agents enters where it raises the same use-of-force questions as local policing.

Why now: on 14 August 2026 the FBI released its full 2025 crime figures, confirming that violent crime fell more in 2025 than in any year since its estimates began in 1936 and that murder fell an estimated 18.1 percent, to a rate of 4.1 per 100,000 people, tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest on record (FBI, https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/fbi-releases-2025-reported-crimes-in-the-nation-statistics). The Council on Criminal Justice reported in July 2026 that homicides in large cities fell another 18 percent in the first half of 2026 (https://counciloncj.org/crime-trends-in-u-s-cities-mid-year-2026-update/). Police killings, by contrast, remain near their record: Campaign Zero’s Mapping Police Violence counted at least 1,314 people killed by US law enforcement in 2025, down 5 percent from a record 1,383 in 2024 (https://campaignzero.org/wp-content/uploads/campaign-zero-mapping-police-violence-2025-report.pdf). Accountability has moved to the center again because of federal agents: on 7 January and 24 January 2026, federal immigration officers shot and killed two US citizens, Renee Good and Alex Pretti, during an enforcement surge in Minneapolis, the city where George Floyd was killed in 2020. Minnesota and Hennepin County sued the federal government in March 2026, saying it was withholding the evidence state investigators needed (NPR, https://www.npr.org/2026/04/10/nx-s1-5775847/alex-pretti-renee-good-ice-shootings-federal-investigations), and on 1 October 2026 Good’s family filed civil suits against the United States and the agent (MPR News, https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/10/01/renee-good-lawsuit-minneapolis-ice-shooting-operation-metro-surge).

The debate is live because both sides can point to the same recent years. Those who want more and more assertive policing note that murder rose by nearly 30 percent in 2020, the worst one-year increase on record, beginning in the weeks after George Floyd’s killing and the protests against police that followed, and that the decline since has come as cities rehired officers and solved more murders (Murder Accountability Project, https://www.murderdata.org/2026/08/heres-reason-for-americas-good-news.html). Those who want police held to stricter account note that killings by police rose to record levels even as crime fell, and that officers are charged with a crime in only a few percent of them. The federal government has changed sides on the question: the Trump administration signed an executive order on 28 April 2025 to “unleash high-impact local police forces” and expand legal protection for officers (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/04/strengthening-and-unleashing-americas-law-enforcement-to-pursue-criminals-and-protect-innocent-citizens/), and on 21 May 2025 its Justice Department dropped the consent decrees the previous administration had sought with Minneapolis and Louisville and retracted findings of unconstitutional policing in six other departments (https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/us-department-justices-civil-rights-division-dismisses-biden-era-police-investigations-and). On 11 August 2025 the president took temporary control of the District of Columbia’s police department, invoking the Home Rule Act for the first time (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/08/declaring-a-crime-emergency-in-the-district-of-columbia/). Meanwhile cities and states have kept their own reforms going: Minneapolis’s mayor signed an executive order on 10 June 2025 requiring the city to carry out the abandoned federal decree anyway (https://www.minneapolismn.gov/resident-services/public-safety/police-public-safety/investigations-settlement-agreement/consent-decree/), Hawaii restricted vehicle pursuits and shooting at moving vehicles in 2025 (https://policeviolencereport.org/), and New York City’s mayor, Zohran Mamdani, who had declined to call for expanding the police department, signed an executive order in March 2026 creating an Office of Community Safety to reshape the city’s response to mental-health crises, launching with two staff members (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/nyc-mayor-mamdani-launches-community-safety-office). In the debate are police unions and chiefs, civil rights organizations, the Black Lives Matter movement and the abolitionist organizers within it, think tanks on the right and left, criminologists and economists who study police effects on crime, and the federal, state and city governments now pulling in different directions. Public opinion holds two things at once: confidence in police is near its lowest in three decades of Gallup polling and sharply split by party and race, yet most Americans of every race have told pollsters they want police to spend the same amount of time or more in their neighborhoods.

Context: The report counts about 300 Black people killed out of a population of about 40 million, 234 Hispanic people out of 63 million, 560 White people out of 193 million and 16 Asian Americans out of 19 million; Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander residents, a very small group, had the highest rate of all. White Americans are the largest group of people killed in absolute numbers. Rates fell for every group from 2024 to 2025, most for Black and Pacific Islander victims. The disparity in raw population rates is not disputed; what it means is. Those who see bias in it point to the share of Black victims who were unarmed or not threatening anyone, and to studies of stops and searches. Those who doubt that population is the right benchmark argue that rates should be compared with rates of contact with police or of involvement in violent crime, which also differ by race; the economist Roland Fryer’s study of Houston, for example, found no racial difference in police shootings once encounters were accounted for, while finding Black and Hispanic civilians more likely to face lesser force (Journal of Political Economy, 2019, https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/701423). Other researchers argue that benchmarking against police encounters hides bias in who gets stopped in the first place (Knox, Lowe & Mummolo, American Political Science Review, 2020, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/administrative-records-mask-racially-biased-policing/66BC0F9998543868BB20F241796B79B8).

Note: The US murder rate fell an estimated 18.1 percent in 2025, to 4.1 per 100,000, tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest the FBI has recorded.
Context: The FBI reports that violent crime fell 9.3 percent from 2024 to 2025, the largest one-year decline since its estimates began in 1936, with robbery down 18.5 percent, rape down 7.6 percent and aggravated assault down 7.2 percent. The fall follows the sharpest one-year rise in murder on record, nearly 30 percent in 2020 (Murder Accountability Project, https://www.murderdata.org/2026/08/heres-reason-for-americas-good-news.html), and has continued: the Council on Criminal Justice found homicides in the large cities it tracks 18 percent lower in the first half of 2026 than a year earlier, 51 percent below their early-2022 peak and 31 percent below the first half of 2019 (https://counciloncj.org/crime-trends-in-u-s-cities-mid-year-2026-update/). Every camp in the policing debate claims some of the credit or rejects the others’ claim to it. Those who favor stronger policing point to rehiring, higher clearance rates and federal enforcement; those who favor accountability and alternatives point to community violence intervention, the end of pandemic disruption and the fact that crime fell in cities that adopted reforms as well as in those that did not. The FBI’s national figures are estimates built from agencies that report, and it switched reporting systems in 2021, so year-to-year comparisons around that change carry extra uncertainty.

Context: Stinson’s count covers on-duty shootings in which an officer killed someone; The Washington Post’s database records about 10,430 people shot and killed by on-duty police from 2015 to 2024 (our count from https://github.com/washingtonpost/data-police-shootings). The figures are read in opposite ways. Advocates of accountability see a system that almost never punishes officers who kill; police organizations and many prosecutors reply that most killings by police involve armed people and are lawful under the Supreme Court’s standard of objective reasonableness, so a low charge rate is what the law predicts, and that the rising number of prosecutions shows the system responds.

Note: US police cleared an estimated 68.8 percent of murders in 2025, up from an all-time low of 52.3 percent in 2022 and the largest one-year improvement on record.
Context: A murder is “cleared” when police arrest and charge a suspect, or close the case for reasons such as the suspect’s death. The Murder Accountability Project says unsolved murders became steadily more common over six decades before the trend reversed in 2023, and that 2025 had the best clearance rate in a quarter century. Its chairman, Thomas Hargrove, attributes the improvement mainly to resources: cities hired detectives and forensic staff during the 2020–21 surge and kept them as murders fell. Clearance rates for other violent crimes are much lower; the FBI estimated that 43.8 percent of all violent crimes were cleared in 2024 (https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/UCR%20Summary%20of%20Reported%20Crimes%20in%20the%20Nation%202024.pdf). Clearance rates matter to every camp. Those who want more police investment argue that solving crimes deters them and gives victims’ families justice; those who want resources moved elsewhere argue that police spend most of their time on calls unrelated to serious crime, and that freeing officers from those calls would let more go to investigations.

Note: In June 2026, 45 percent of Americans said they had a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in the police, two points above the record low of 43 percent in 2023.
Context: Gallup has asked the question every year since 1993. Confidence averaged 55 percent from 1993 and peaked at 64 percent in 2004, has been below that average since 2018 and fell to 48 percent in 2020, the year of George Floyd’s killing (Gallup 2025, https://news.gallup.com/poll/692633/democrats-confidence-institutions-sinks-new-low.aspx). Those who favor reform read the racial gap as evidence that policing does not serve all communities equally; those who favor stronger policing read the overall level, still higher than for Congress, the courts or the news media, as evidence of broad public support.

Note: In a study of 242 large US cities, each additional officer prevented an estimated 0.06 to 0.1 homicides a year, about one life saved for every 10 to 17 officers hired.
Context: The authors estimate that the effect per person is about twice as large for Black victims as for White victims, so that, in their words, Black communities are “simultaneously over and under-policed.” Each additional officer also made an estimated 7.3 more arrests a year for low-level “quality of life” offenses, a burden that falls disproportionately on Black residents per capita, and arrests for serious offenses fell. Other studies using different sources of variation, such as federal hiring grants and terror-alert deployments, also find that more police reduce crime, and the Manhattan Institute and other advocates of police funding cite this body of work as the strongest evidence in the debate (https://manhattan.institute/article/de-policing-and-what-to-do-about-it). Note: In nine cities’ 911 data, no more than 7 percent of calls for police service involved violent crime, and an average of 19 percent could have been answered by unarmed crisis responders, by Vera’s classification.
Context: Vera analyzed 15.6 million calls from January 2019 to November 2021 from police departments in nine cities, including Baltimore, Detroit, New Orleans and Seattle; in most of them fewer than 3 percent of calls involved violent crime, and an average of 62.6 percent involved situations Vera classified as noncriminal (https://www.vera.org/news/most-911-calls-have-nothing-to-do-with-crime-why-are-we-still-sending-police). Vera, which favors shifting calls to civilians, reads these figures as evidence that police are sent to many problems that do not need armed officers. Police organizations and some analysts reply that call types are coded by dispatchers before anyone arrives, that calls described as non-criminal can turn violent, and that the time police spend on calls, rather than their number, is what matters for staffing; serious crime calls take far longer per call than minor ones. The Dee and Pyne study of Denver cites estimates that a quarter to two-thirds of calls involving disorder, mental health, medical and non-criminal problems could be handled by others (https://tom-dee.github.io/files/sciadv.abm2106.pdf).

## Questions

Question: Do more police officers reduce serious violence by enough to outweigh the stops, arrests and force that come with them?
Type: empirical
Poles: Benefits outweigh costs / Costs outweigh benefits
Why contested: Few in the debate now deny that police presence reduces some crime; the dispute is over the size of the effect, which activities produce it, and how to count the burdens that more officers bring. The best estimates come from natural experiments in a limited number of cities and periods, and the costs, from low-level arrests to killings by police, fall on different people from the benefits.
Case for “Benefits outweigh costs”: Study after study using federal hiring grants, terror-alert deployments and randomized patrols finds that more police mean less crime, and the effect on homicide is large: about one life saved for every 10 to 17 officers hired, with the benefit per person roughly twice as large for Black residents, who are most often the victims of violence (Chalfin and colleagues, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28202/w28202.pdf). The National Academies’ 2018 review found strong evidence that concentrating police at crime hot spots reduces crime without simply displacing it. Cities that lost officers after 2020 saw murders rise; the recent fall in murder came as departments rebuilt staffing and solved more cases. Each prevented killing also prevents the retaliation that would follow. If policing has costs, the answer is to direct it better, not to have less of it.
Case for “Costs outweigh benefits”: The same study that finds lives saved finds each added officer making about seven more low-level arrests a year, with the burden falling disproportionately on Black residents, and cities with the largest Black populations getting the smallest homicide benefits. Arrests and stops carry real harms, from lost jobs to lasting distrust that makes residents less willing to report crimes or cooperate with investigations. Killings by police rose to records even as police budgets recovered. Money spent on officers is money not spent on interventions that also reduce violence, such as street outreach, summer jobs, cleaning vacant lots and better lighting, several of which have randomized evidence behind them and none of which risk a death at the hands of the state. The right comparison is not police against nothing but police against the next best use of the money.

Question: How much of the racial gap in police stops and force reflects bias, rather than differences in where police are sent and what they encounter?
Type: empirical
Poles: Mostly bias / Mostly circumstances
Why contested: Black Americans are killed by police at about 2.6 times the rate of White Americans and report threats or use of force at more than three times the rate in police-initiated contacts. Researchers agree on the disparities; they disagree on the benchmark. Comparing with population, with rates of violent crime, or with police encounters gives different answers, and each benchmark can itself be shaped by policing.
Case for “Mostly bias”: The disparities appear at every stage, in who is stopped, who is searched, who is handcuffed and who is shot. The Stanford Open Policing Project, analyzing nearly 100 million traffic stops, found Black drivers generally stopped at higher rates than White drivers and searched more often once stopped, and its threshold test, which infers how much suspicion officers require before searching, found that police require less suspicion to search Black and Hispanic drivers than White drivers (https://openpolicing.stanford.edu/findings/). Studies that compare outcomes only among people police chose to stop understate bias, because the decision to stop is where much of it enters (Knox, Lowe and Mummolo, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/administrative-records-mask-racially-biased-policing/66BC0F9998543868BB20F241796B79B8). Black people killed by police are more likely to have been unarmed and less likely to have been threatening anyone. A disparity this persistent, across thousands of agencies, is evidence of a system that treats people differently by race.
Case for “Mostly circumstances”: Police are deployed where calls for service and violent crime are concentrated, and in many cities those neighborhoods are disproportionately Black, as are the victims of violent crime. Measured against involvement in violent encounters rather than against population, the disparity in police shootings shrinks or disappears in several studies; Roland Fryer’s analysis of Houston found no racial difference in officer-involved shootings once encounters were accounted for, even as it found Black and Hispanic civilians more likely to face lesser force (https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/701423). In the Stanford project’s simpler outcome test, searches of Black drivers turned up contraband at rates generally in line with searches of White drivers, which that test reads as an absence of discrimination in search decisions, though the project itself prefers its threshold test, which points the other way. Disparities driven by the location of crime call for reducing crime, not for treating every disparity as proof of bias.

Question: Which calls should police stop answering, and can civilian responders take them over safely at scale?
Type: empirical
Poles: Shift calls out / Keep police responding
Why contested: Only a small share of 911 calls involve violent crime, and pilots in Denver, Eugene and New York show civilians can handle some crisis and disorder calls. But the programs are small, the calls they take are screened, and no city has yet moved a large share of police work to other agencies.
Case for “Shift calls out”: In nine cities, no more than 7 percent of calls for police involved violent crime, and Vera estimated that about 19 percent could go to unarmed crisis responders (https://www.vera.org/publications/911-analysis). About one in five people killed by police in 2025 showed signs of mental illness, and most of those deaths followed a call for help. When Denver sent a clinician and a paramedic instead of officers, reports of the targeted low-level crimes fell by a third, serious crime did not rise, and the cost per offense averted was a fraction of a police and court response (https://tom-dee.github.io/files/sciadv.abm2106.pdf). Moving those calls also frees officers to investigate the shootings and murders that most often go unsolved. Armed officers are the wrong first response to a person in crisis, and every unneeded encounter is a chance for one to go wrong.
Case for “Keep police responding”: Dispatchers classify calls on thin information, and calls that sound routine, a disturbance, a man acting strangely, a welfare check, can involve weapons or violence. Civilian programs work because they take carefully screened calls, operate limited hours and rely on police backup; Denver’s team answered 748 calls in its six-month pilot, and Eugene’s police department estimates that CAHOOTS, the oldest such program, diverts only about 5 to 8 percent of police calls for service (https://www.eugene-or.gov/DocumentCenter/View/56717/CAHOOTS-Program-Analysis). New York City’s B-HEARD program and Mamdani’s new Office of Community Safety have started small for the same reason. People in crisis also commit crimes and sometimes threaten others, and families often call police precisely because no one else will come at 3 a.m. Building civilian capacity is worth doing, but at the margin, not as a replacement for officers who can respond to anything.

Question: Did the 2020 protests and the pullback in proactive policing contribute to that year’s surge in killings?
Type: empirical
Poles: Pullback raised killings / Other causes dominated
Why contested: Murder rose nearly 30 percent in 2020, the largest one-year increase on record, beginning in the weeks after George Floyd’s killing, during a pandemic that also closed schools and services and coincided with record gun sales. The timing fits several explanations at once, and the answer shapes what each side thinks the lesson of 2020 is.
Case for “Pullback raised killings”: The surge began the week of Floyd’s killing, not when the pandemic began in March, and was concentrated in cities with large protests and sharp drops in stops and arrests. Officers in surveys reported pulling back from discretionary enforcement for fear of becoming the next viral video. An earlier pattern supports the mechanism: Devi and Fryer found that federal pattern-or-practice investigations launched after viral police killings were followed by large increases in homicide, almost 900 excess homicides across the cities studied, apparently because police activity fell sharply, while investigations not preceded by viral incidents reduced crime (https://www.nber.org/papers/w27324). Homicide clearance rates collapsed to an all-time low in 2022. The recovery followed rehiring and renewed enforcement.
Case for “Other causes dominated”: Murder rose almost everywhere in 2020, in suburbs and rural counties as well as protest cities, in places with and without reforms, and in places where police budgets rose. The pandemic closed schools, jobs and the violence-intervention programs that work with people at highest risk, while Americans bought guns at record rates. No major city abolished its police department, so “defunding” cannot explain a national surge. The decline since 2022 has also been nearly universal, including in cities that kept reforms and invested in community violence intervention, which suggests that broad forces, not police posture alone, drove both the rise and the fall.

Question: Should officers who violate someone’s constitutional rights be shielded from civil suits by qualified immunity?
Type: value
Poles: End the shield / Keep the shield
Why contested: Qualified immunity is a doctrine the Supreme Court created, not a statute; it bars damages suits unless an officer violated “clearly established” law. Congress has repeatedly failed to pass changes, and the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act, which would curtail it, was reintroduced in September 2025. Three states and New York City have created state or local claims that limit it.
Case for “End the shield”: A person whose rights were plainly violated should be able to recover in court, and the doctrine often requires a prior case with nearly identical facts before an officer can be held liable, so new forms of misconduct go unremedied. Officers rarely pay anyway: governments paid about 99.98 percent of what plaintiffs recovered in Joanna Schwartz’s national study (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2297534), so ending the defense would mainly move costs onto the cities that hire, train and supervise officers, giving them a reason to do it better. Colorado ended the defense for state claims in 2020 and its police departments continue to function.
Case for “Keep the shield”: Officers make split-second decisions under threat, and the doctrine protects those who act reasonably in situations the law has not clearly addressed, while still allowing suits against those who break clearly established rules. Removing it would expose officers to years of litigation, discovery and personal anxiety even when they ultimately win, and would deter recruitment at a time of staffing shortages. Police organizations warn that it would make officers hesitate in exactly the moments when hesitation is dangerous. If the doctrine needs adjusting, it is for courts to refine, not for Congress to abolish.

Question: Who should hold local police to account when they violate rights: federal courts and monitors, state agencies, or local voters and officials?
Type: value
Poles: Federal oversight / Local control
Why contested: Federal consent decrees, negotiated by the Justice Department and enforced by judges and monitors, were the main national tool for reforming troubled departments from the 1990s to 2024. The current administration has dropped them as federal overreach, while some states, such as Minnesota, have pursued their own agreements.
Case for “Federal oversight”: Local officials depend politically on police unions and often fail to act until a crisis, and constitutional rights should not depend on the city one lives in. In Seattle, the court-appointed monitor’s 2017 assessment found that moderate and higher-level force by officers fell 60 percent between July 2014 and October 2016 under the city’s consent decree, as summarized by city council members (Seattle City Council, https://council.seattle.gov/2017/04/10/councilmembers-burgess-and-gonzalezs-statements-on-the-seattle-police-monitors-assessment-of-use-of-force/), and decrees impose outside data, monitoring and deadlines that local reformers cannot. The Justice Department’s dismissal of the Minneapolis and Louisville cases in May 2025, and its retraction of findings in six other cities, removes protection from communities where federal investigators found patterns of unconstitutional conduct.
Case for “Local control”: Policing is a local function accountable to local voters, and federal decrees hand control to unelected monitors and judges for a decade or more at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars, as the Justice Department argued in May 2025 (https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/us-department-justices-civil-rights-division-dismisses-biden-era-police-investigations-and). Research on investigations launched after viral incidents found they were followed by spikes in homicide as police pulled back (https://www.nber.org/papers/w27324). Cities that want reform can adopt it themselves, as Minneapolis did by executive order, and states can set training, decertification and use-of-force standards for every agency, which reaches far more departments than a handful of federal decrees ever did.

Question: Does proactive enforcement, such as pedestrian stops, pretextual traffic stops and arrests for minor offenses, prevent enough serious crime to justify its intrusions?
Type: empirical
Poles: Prevents serious crime / Mostly causes harm
Why contested: Proactive tactics are distinct from police numbers: the National Academies found good evidence for some focused strategies and weak or mixed evidence for others, and the harms, including the encounters that turn fatal, are concentrated in a few neighborhoods. New York’s stop-and-frisk era, its decline and the city’s continued fall in crime are cited by both sides.
Case for “Prevents serious crime”: Crime is intensely concentrated; in New York about half of reported violent crime occurs on 4 percent of street segments, according to research Rafael Mangual cited to Congress in November 2025 (https://docs.house.gov/meetings/JU/JU13/20251119/118671/HHRG-119-JU13-Wstate-MangualR-20251119.pdf). Police who work those places actively, stopping people with illegal guns, enforcing warrants and addressing disorder, prevent shootings that response alone cannot. Most homicide suspects have long arrest records, so contact with repeat offenders is the point, not a side effect. Hot-spots policing and focused deterrence have among the strongest evidence of any crime-control strategy.
Case for “Mostly causes harm”: Most stops find nothing, and New York’s recorded stops fell from a record 685,724 in 2011 to 25,386 in 2024, a drop of more than 96 percent, without a return to the violence of the early 1990s (NYCLU, https://www.nyclu.org/data/stop-and-frisk-data). Traffic stops are among the routine encounters that end in deaths, about one in eleven police killings in 2025 (https://policeviolencereport.org/). Each stop that finds nothing still teaches residents that police see them as suspects, which corrodes the cooperation investigations depend on. Several cities and states, including Philadelphia and Virginia, have restricted stops for minor equipment violations, and the evidence that focused strategies work is evidence for precision, not for broad stops.

Question: Can policing be made just through reform, or is the institution itself the problem?
Type: value
Poles: Reform can work / Replace the institution
Why contested: This is the question beneath the slogans of 2020. Reformers point to measurable gains from training, policies and oversight; abolitionists argue that decades of reform have not changed what police are for and that safety should be built by other means. Most Americans reject abolition, but a third of adults under 35 told Gallup in 2020 that they supported it.
Case for “Reform can work”: Policing has changed before: deadly force rules have narrowed since the 1985 Supreme Court case Tennessee v. Garner, departments that adopted de-escalation training have measurably reduced force, and states have created decertification systems and duty-to-intervene laws. Every society needs someone who can respond with force to violence in progress, and the communities most affected by crime want police to stay. The task is to make policing accountable, professional and focused, not to abolish the only institution that answers a 911 call at any hour.
Case for “Replace the institution”: Each wave of reform, from the 1960s riot commissions to body cameras, has left the basic role unchanged: armed agents of the state sent to manage poverty, addiction, mental illness and disorder, with predictable violence against the people they police. Accountability measures are absorbed or resisted, and police budgets grow regardless of results. Abolitionists argue that safety comes from housing, health care, jobs and community-based ways of responding to harm, and that each reform that expands police resources or legitimacy delays building those alternatives. They support reducing police step by step, by shrinking budgets and functions, toward a society that no longer needs them.

## Positions

Camp: Fund police and bind them to enforceable standards
Adherents: The Biden White House, whose Safer America Plan of August 2022 proposed funding 100,000 additional officers “recruited, trained, hired, and supervised consistent with the standards” of the president’s 2022 executive order on accountable policing, and quoted the president: “We should all agree the answer is not to defund the police. It’s to fund the police. Fund them with the resources and training they need to protect our communities” (https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2022/08/01/fact-sheet-president-bidens-safer-america-plan-2/); the National Organization of Black Law Enforcement Executives (NOBLE), whose members lead police agencies in many large cities and which wrote to House leaders that “Law enforcement agencies and their leaders have a responsibility to ensure that justice is administered fairly in all communities” and endorsed the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act (https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-judiciary.house.gov/files/migrated/UploadedFiles/NOBLE_Congresssional_Letter_-_Justice_In_Policing_Act.pdf); Representative Glenn Ivey of Maryland, a former prosecutor, who reintroduced that act on 15 September 2025, saying it would ensure “that law enforcement is both effective and accountable” (https://ivey.house.gov/media/press-releases/congressman-glenn-ivey-announces-re-introduction-george-floyd-justice-policing). Most Democratic officeholders, many police chiefs and the Police Executive Research Forum hold versions of this view.
Case: Communities need police, and the communities that suffer most from violence want them to stay. They also need police they can trust, and trust depends on officers being held to clear rules and real consequences when they break them. These are not competing goals: a department that uses force sparingly, explains itself and removes bad officers gets more cooperation from witnesses and solves more crimes. The way forward is to fund policing properly and attach conditions to that money: national standards on use of force, a duty to intervene, bans on chokeholds and most no-knock warrants, body cameras, independent investigation of deaths, a national registry so officers fired for misconduct cannot be quietly rehired elsewhere, reform of qualified immunity so victims can recover when their rights are violated, and better training in de-escalation and crisis response. Some calls, such as many mental-health crises, are better handled by trained civilians working alongside police, which frees officers for serious crime. Done this way, reform and public safety reinforce each other.
Evidence: Chalfin and colleagues estimate that each 10 to 17 additional officers prevents about one homicide a year, with larger benefits per capita for Black residents (https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28202/w28202.pdf); a randomized rollout of de-escalation training in Louisville was followed by 28 percent fewer use-of-force incidents and 36 percent fewer officer injuries (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1745-9133.12574); Seattle’s consent decree was followed by a 60 percent fall in moderate and higher-level force (https://council.seattle.gov/2017/04/10/councilmembers-burgess-and-gonzalezs-statements-on-the-seattle-police-monitors-assessment-of-use-of-force/); homicide clearance rates rose to 68.8 percent in 2025 as cities invested in investigators (https://www.murderdata.org/2026/08/heres-reason-for-americas-good-news.html); in 2020, 81 percent of Black Americans wanted police to spend the same or more time in their area while 88 percent said policing needed major changes (Gallup, https://news.gallup.com/poll/316571/black-americans-police-retain-local-presence.aspx, https://news.gallup.com/poll/315962/americans-say-policing-needs-major-changes.aspx).
Answer to the strongest objection: To those who want police unleashed, the camp answers that accountability is what makes effective policing possible: departments with credible discipline and transparent use-of-force rules earn the cooperation that solves crimes, and the de-escalation evidence shows officers are safer, not more endangered, under well-designed rules. It notes that the Louisville training reduced officer injuries along with civilian ones. To those who want police shrunk or abolished, it answers that when Minneapolis voters were asked in November 2021 whether to replace their police department with a new Department of Public Safety, 56.2 percent said no (Ballotpedia, https://ballotpedia.org/Minneapolis,_Minnesota,_Question_2,_Replace_Police_Department_with_Department_of_Public_Safety_Initiative_(November_2021)), that most Black Americans want police presence maintained, and that the evidence on police and homicide is too strong to bet lives on its absence; it supports civilian responders as a complement, not a substitute. To the charge that decades of reform have changed little, it answers that the reforms that work have rarely been adopted fully or nationally, which is the point of federal standards.
Variation: Adherents differ on emphasis and instrument. Civil rights organizations such as the NAACP Legal Defense Fund and Campaign Zero concentrate on accountability, including ending qualified immunity and independent prosecution of police killings, and say less about police funding; elected Democrats and police executives tend to pair accountability with hiring money. Some favor federal mandates, others state laws on training, decertification and use of force. New York City’s mayor, Zohran Mamdani, declined to call for expanding the police department but has not proposed cutting it, and has moved to shift mental-health calls to a new civilian office, a position that overlaps with the camp that would move duties from police to other responders.

Camp: Expand police and free them to enforce the law
Adherents: The Trump White House, whose Executive Order 14288 of 28 April 2025 states that “Safe communities rely on the backbone and heroism of a tough and well-equipped police force” and that “When local leaders demonize law enforcement and impose legal and political handcuffs that make aggressively enforcing the law impossible, crime thrives and innocent citizens and small business owners suffer” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/04/strengthening-and-unleashing-americas-law-enforcement-to-pursue-criminals-and-protect-innocent-citizens/); the Justice Department under that administration, whose Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights, Harmeet Dhillon, said on dropping the Minneapolis and Louisville consent decrees that “Overbroad police consent decrees divest local control of policing from communities where it belongs, turning that power over to unelected and unaccountable bureaucrats, often with an anti-police agenda” (https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/us-department-justices-civil-rights-division-dismisses-biden-era-police-investigations-and); the Fraternal Order of Police, the largest police labor organization, whose national president Patrick Yoes told Congress on 3 December 2025 that “Now is the time for our elected officials at every level of government to answer our call and support law enforcement” (https://www.congress.gov/119/meeting/house/118672/witnesses/HHRG-119-HM00-Wstate-YoesP-20251203.pdf); the Manhattan Institute, whose analyst Robert VerBruggen wrote that “the research is convincing that police presence reduces crime, both in general and when it comes to many types of proactive efforts” (https://manhattan.institute/article/de-policing-and-what-to-do-about-it). Most Republican officeholders hold this view.
Case: The first duty of government is to protect people from violence, and police are the institution that does it. The evidence that more officers and active policing reduce crime is among the strongest in social science, and the people who benefit most are residents of the poor, mostly minority neighborhoods where violence concentrates. After 2020, politicians and activists demonized officers, cut budgets, stripped away legal protections and opened investigations after every viral video; officers pulled back, resignations rose by more than half between 2019 and 2022 in the Police Executive Research Forum’s survey of its member agencies (https://www.policeforum.org/trending23may26), and murder rose by nearly 30 percent in a single year, the worst increase ever recorded. The recovery since has come as cities rehired, police returned to proactive work and the federal government stood behind them. The right policy is to fund and staff departments fully, support officers who act lawfully under pressure, keep qualified immunity, end federal micromanagement through consent decrees, target the small number of repeat violent offenders who commit most serious crime, and hold individual officers who break the law accountable through the ordinary criminal process, not by treating the whole profession as suspect.
Evidence: The FBI reports that murder fell 18.1 percent in 2025 to a rate of 4.1 per 100,000, tied for the lowest on record (https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/fbi-releases-2025-reported-crimes-in-the-nation-statistics), after the 2020 increase of nearly 30 percent (https://www.murderdata.org/2026/08/heres-reason-for-americas-good-news.html); Devi and Fryer estimate that federal investigations launched after viral police killings were followed by almost 900 excess homicides as police activity fell (https://www.nber.org/papers/w27324); Chalfin and colleagues find each 10 to 17 officers prevents a homicide, with larger benefits for Black residents (https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28202/w28202.pdf); the FBI reports assaults on officers at 13.8 per 100 officers in 2025, the highest in a decade, and 258 officers feloniously killed from 2021 to 2024, more than in any other four-year period in its data (https://cde.ucr.cjis.gov/LATEST/resources/reports/Officers%20Killed%20and%20Assaulted%20in%20the%20Line%20of%20Duty,%202025.pdf); the United States has about 241 sworn officers per 100,000 residents, fewer than the European Union average (https://bjs.ojp.gov/media/67846/download).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that killings by police reached records and that Black Americans bear them disproportionately, the camp answers that about three-quarters of the people killed by police in 2025 were reported to be armed, half with a gun (https://campaignzero.org/wp-content/uploads/campaign-zero-mapping-police-violence-2025-report.pdf), that a population benchmark ignores where violent crime and police encounters occur, and that far more Black lives are lost to the violence that under-policing allows than to police. It supports prosecuting officers who commit crimes, as Derek Chauvin was, but not lowering the legal standards for force or exposing officers to suits for split-second judgments. To the claim that civilian responders can take over much police work, it answers that it has no objection to crisis teams for screened calls, but that dispatchers cannot reliably predict which calls turn violent and that the pilots are too small to replace officers.
Variation: Adherents differ on how far federal power should reach. The administration has used it aggressively, taking temporary control of the District of Columbia’s police in August 2025 and deploying federal agents and the National Guard to cities, a step the Supreme Court limited in Illinois in December 2025 (NPR, https://npr.org/2025/12/23/nx-s1-5641959/supreme-court-chicago-national-guard), while many conservatives who support the police prefer to leave policing to states and localities on federalist grounds. Some, such as Rafael Mangual of the Manhattan Institute, put more weight on incapacitating repeat offenders through prosecution and pretrial detention than on police tactics. Police unions defend officers’ due-process protections in discipline, which some in the camp’s law-and-order wing and some reformers alike regard as obstacles to removing bad officers.

Camp: Move money and duties from police to other responders
Adherents: The Movement for Black Lives, a coalition of Black-led organizations whose “Defund the Police” campaign calls on decision-makers “to defund the police after decades of inaction and failed reforms, consent decrees, investigations, and oversight” and whose proposed BREATHE Act would divest federal money from policing and incarceration and invest it in communities (https://m4bl.org/defund-the-police/); the sociologist Alex Vitale of Brooklyn College, author of The End of Policing, who said in 2020 that “we need to directly challenge the scope and power of policing. We need to take their budgets away, take their toys away, take their authority away in as many dimensions as we can” (The Nation, https://www.thenation.com/article/society/alex-vitale-defund-police-interview/). Many local organizers, some city council members and the Vera Institute of Justice, which argues that most 911 calls do not need an armed response, support parts of this program.
Case: The United States asks police to handle almost every social problem, from homelessness and addiction to mental illness, school discipline and traffic, and then is surprised when armed officers trained mainly in force produce violent outcomes. Most calls to police have nothing to do with violent crime, and many of the people killed by police were in a mental-health crisis, stopped for a traffic violation or suspected of a minor offense. Decades of reform, training, body cameras, consent decrees and review boards have not changed the basic pattern, because they leave police in charge of the same work with the same tools. The answer is to shrink what police do and what they spend, and to build what actually prevents harm: unarmed crisis responders, violence interrupters, housing, mental-health and addiction treatment, youth programs and investment in the neighborhoods that have been both over-policed and under-served. Every call handled by someone else is an encounter that cannot end in a police killing.
Evidence: In nine cities’ 911 data, no more than 7 percent of calls involved violent crime and about 19 percent could have gone to unarmed crisis responders (Vera, https://www.vera.org/publications/911-analysis); Denver’s civilian STAR pilot cut reports of targeted low-level crimes by 34 percent with no detectable rise in serious crime, at far lower cost than a police response (https://tom-dee.github.io/files/sciadv.abm2106.pdf); of police killings in 2025 in one national dataset, 116 followed mental-health or welfare checks and 112 traffic stops (https://policeviolencereport.org/); Chalfin and colleagues find that each added officer makes about seven more low-level arrests a year, with the burden falling disproportionately on Black residents, and that cities with the largest Black populations get the smallest homicide benefits (https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28202/w28202.pdf); state and local police spending grew from $47 billion to $135 billion in real terms from 1977 to 2021 (https://www.urban.org/policy-centers/cross-center-initiatives/state-and-local-finance-initiative/state-and-local-backgrounders/criminal-justice-police-corrections-courts-expenditures).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that more police reduce homicide and that the 2020 surge shows the danger of pulling back, the camp answers that the alternatives it proposes also reduce violence, that the 2020 increase happened nationwide, including in places that never cut a dollar, and that the question is not police against nothing but which mix of spending saves the most lives with the least harm. To the point that most Black Americans want police to stay, it answers that the same surveys show overwhelming demand for major change, and that residents asked what keeps them safe name jobs, housing and services as often as officers. To the warning that dispatchers cannot predict danger, it answers that Eugene’s CAHOOTS teams were dispatched to 17,700 calls in 2019 and called for police backup 311 times, according to the city police department’s own analysis (https://www.eugene-or.gov/DocumentCenter/View/56717/CAHOOTS-Program-Analysis), and that police can be called in when a weapon is reported.
Variation: Adherents differ on scale and destination. Some want deep, immediate budget cuts; others, including many city officials who adopted the language of reallocation in 2020, favor gradual shifts through attrition, moving specific functions such as mental-health response, school safety and traffic enforcement to civilian agencies. Some see this as an end in itself, a smaller and more accountable police force; others, including many in the Movement for Black Lives, see it as a step toward abolition.

Camp: Abolish police and build other ways to keep people safe
Adherents: Critical Resistance, the abolitionist organization co-founded by Angela Davis and Ruth Wilson Gilmore, which “supports abolitionist reforms to dismantle the systems of policing and works to create viable alternatives in our communities” and whose mission statement says that “Because we seek to abolish the PIC [prison-industrial complex], we cannot support any work that extends its life or scope” (https://criticalresistance.org/abolish-policing/, https://criticalresistance.org/mission-vision/); the organizer Mariame Kaba, whose New York Times essay of 12 June 2020, titled “Yes, We Mean Literally Abolish the Police,” argued that police cannot be reformed and that their funding should go instead to health care, housing, education and jobs (https://www.nytimes.com/2020/06/12/opinion/sunday/floyd-abolish-defund-police.html). In Minneapolis, the group MPD150 and several council members called for dismantling the city’s police department in 2020.
Case: Policing in the United States grew out of slave patrols, strikebreaking and the control of poor and colonized people, and it still does what it was built to do: manage poverty and race through surveillance, punishment and force. That is why a century of reform has not changed the outcomes. Commissions, training, body cameras, civilian review and consent decrees are absorbed, and police budgets keep growing whatever the results. Police mostly arrive after harm is done and rarely solve the crimes that devastate communities; what actually produces safety is people having housing, health care, income, and relationships and institutions that can respond to conflict and harm without cages and guns. Abolition is not the sudden disappearance of police tomorrow but a project: shrink police step by step, refuse reforms that expand their reach or legitimacy, and build community-based ways of preventing and repairing harm until police are no longer needed.
Evidence: Killings by police rose to records in the years after the reforms of 2014 and 2020, with at least 1,383 in 2024 and 1,314 in 2025 (https://campaignzero.org/wp-content/uploads/campaign-zero-mapping-police-violence-2025-report.pdf); fewer than one in three officers charged with murder or manslaughter for on-duty shootings since 2005 have been convicted (https://capitalbnews.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/count.murd_.and_.mansl_.onduty.shootings.111924.1007.pdf); before the recent improvement, nearly half of murders went unsolved, with a clearance rate of 52.3 percent in 2022 (https://www.murderdata.org/2026/08/heres-reason-for-americas-good-news.html); only a small share of 911 calls involve violent crime (https://www.vera.org/publications/911-analysis); in 2020, 15 percent of Americans and a third of those under 35 supported abolishing police departments (Gallup, https://news.gallup.com/poll/315962/americans-say-policing-needs-major-changes.aspx).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that someone must respond when a person is being attacked now, abolitionists answer that police today rarely prevent such attacks and often fail to solve them, that most violence is between people who know each other and is better prevented by changing conditions than by response, and that the goal is to build the capacity, from trained community responders to transformative-justice processes, that makes armed police unnecessary, not to leave people without help. To the evidence that more police reduce homicide, they answer that the comparison is to a world without the investments they propose, which the studies do not measure. To the low public support, they answer that most transformative changes, from the abolition of slavery to the end of legal segregation, began as minority positions.
Variation: Abolitionists differ on pace and on which interim reforms to back. Most support “non-reformist reforms” that shrink police, such as budget cuts, removing police from schools and ending pretextual stops, and oppose reforms that expand police resources, such as more training money or body-camera purchases. Some are prison abolitionists first, for whom policing is one part of the system they seek to end.

## Crux

Crux: How much safety do armed police uniquely provide that other responders and investments could not, and how much intrusion, force and risk of wrongful death, which falls most heavily on poor and Black communities, is it acceptable to accept in exchange for that safety?

Evidence could settle: The size of the trade-offs. That means better estimates of how many homicides and shootings additional officers prevent, and which police activities, visible presence, investigations, focused enforcement on high-risk people and places or broad proactive stops, produce the effect; how much of that safety civilian crisis responders, violence interrupters and social investments could deliver for the same money, tested at the scale of whole cities rather than small pilots; how much specific accountability tools, such as de-escalation training, independent investigations, decertification, consent decrees and changes to qualified immunity, reduce police killings and misconduct, and whether they change crime or police activity; how much of the racial disparity in stops and force reflects officers’ decisions rather than where and when they encounter people; and what caused the rise in killings in 2020 and the steep fall since. National data are still thin: there is no complete official count of killings by police, use-of-force reporting is voluntary in many states, and most evidence on alternatives comes from a few programs. Complete national use-of-force data and large randomized or staggered rollouts of alternatives and accountability rules could narrow the range a great deal.

Value choice: How to weigh different harms, and who should bear them. One view holds that the state’s first duty is to protect people from violence, that the victims of crime are concentrated in the same neighborhoods as the complaints about police, and that some intrusion and error are the price of protecting them. Another holds that a killing or abuse by an agent of the state is a distinct wrong, worse than its count suggests because it is done in the public’s name, and that no gain in average safety justifies burdens imposed disproportionately by race. A third holds that the question of how much policing to buy is the wrong one, because safety is better produced by meeting people’s needs than by force. Related choices follow: how much discretion and legal protection officers deserve when they make split-second decisions, and whether communities, states or the federal government should decide what their police may do. No study can settle how a society should trade freedom from crime against freedom from the police, or whose experience of the police should count most.
