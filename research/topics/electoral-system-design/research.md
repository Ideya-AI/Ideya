# Electoral Rules — research

## Overview

This topic is about the rules that turn citizens’ choices into governments, in two linked debates. The first is how votes become seats: whether a legislature should be elected from single-member districts in which the candidate with the most votes (first-past-the-post) or a majority (two-round or ranked-choice/instant-runoff voting) wins, as in the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, India, France and Australia’s lower house, or by proportional representation, in which parties win seats roughly in line with their vote share, through party lists (the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden), the single transferable vote (Ireland, Malta) or mixed-member systems (Germany, New Zealand). The second is who gets to vote and how easily: voter registration, photo identification at the polls, documentary proof of citizenship, mail and early voting, and the cleaning of voter rolls, where measures meant to make elections more secure are weighed against measures meant to make voting easier. The first debate is livest in Britain and the other Westminster-style democracies; the second is livest in the United States, with a British echo in the voter ID requirement introduced in 2023. The topic deliberately excludes campaign finance and donations (covered in the money in politics topic), the wider health of democracy and populism (covered in the democratic backsliding topic), alternatives to electoral democracy (covered in the democracy and its rivals topic), the US Electoral College and presidential selection, term limits, the voting age, and redistricting and gerrymandering except where they bear on the case for or against single-member districts.

Why now: on 29 September 2026, in his first Labour conference speech as Prime Minister, Andy Burnham announced a National Commission on Electoral Reform, invited every party to take part, and said “We will contest the next election with a manifesto commitment to change the electoral system, so that everyone’s vote counts and everyone’s voice is heard,” warning that under first-past-the-post “a divisive movement” could take power on less than 30 percent of the vote (Politics.co.uk, https://www.politics.co.uk/2026/09/andy-burnham-announces-national-commission-on-electoral-reform/; Reuters via TimesLIVE, https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/world/2026-09-29-uk-pm-burnham-wants-to-scrap-the-first-past-the-post-electoral-system/). Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch rejected the plan on 1 October as a distraction intended to “lock in the left” (Irish News/PA, https://www.irishnews.com/news/uk/badenoch-tories-will-turn-tide-on-burnhams-destructive-socialist-agenda-BR5SJXJKKRLKNAITBQQAYAW5VQ/). The next UK general election is due by 2029. The day after Burnham’s speech, on 30 September 2026, the US Senate failed for the last time before the midterms to advance the SAVE America Act, which would require documentary proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote in federal elections; the vote was 53–47, short of the 60 needed (Senator Alex Padilla, https://www.padilla.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/padilla-leads-final-pre-midterm-effort-to-defeat-anti-voter-save-act-condemns-trump-corruption/; bill text summary from its sponsor, https://www.lee.senate.gov/2026/1/senator-mike-lee-introduces-save-america-act-with-congressman-chip-roy). In the same weeks the courts were deciding how far President Trump’s Executive Order 14399 of 31 March 2026, “Ensuring Citizenship Verification and Integrity in Federal Elections,” could take effect before the 3 November 2026 midterms. The order directs the Department of Homeland Security to compile state citizenship lists and the Postal Service to deliver mail ballots only to people on them (White House, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/03/ensuring-citizenship-verification-and-integrity-in-federal-elections). The Supreme Court on 24 August 2026 lifted one injunction against it by 6–3 (NBC News, https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/supreme-court/supreme-court-hands-preliminary-win-trump-mail-ballots-executive-order-rcna590120); Judge Indira Talwani blocked the Postal Service’s implementing rule again on 4 September (NBC News, https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/judge-blocks-trump-effort-restrict-voting-mail-ballots-start-going-rcna596097); and on 14 September the Supreme Court declined to let that rule take effect (SCOTUSblog, https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/09/supreme-court-denies-trump-administrations-request-to-implement-parts-of-usps-rule-on-mail-in-vo/). Earlier in 2026 the Court held 5–4 in Watson v. Republican National Committee (29 June) that federal law lets states count mail ballots postmarked by Election Day that arrive afterward (SCOTUSblog, https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/06/justices-uphold-state-law-allowing-for-late-arriving-mail-in-ballots/). On 3 November voters in Arizona, California, Nevada, North Carolina and Ohio decide voter ID measures, after Oklahoma’s in August, the most statewide voter ID measures in a single year (Ballotpedia, https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/07/27/voters-to-decide-at-least-six-statewide-voter-id-measures-in-2026-most-in-a-single-year/), and Alaskans vote again on repealing the top-four primary and ranked-choice general election they adopted in 2020 (Ballotpedia, https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/09/25/voters-in-three-cities-will-decide-on-ballot-measures-to-implement-ranked-choice-voting-in-local-elections-on-nov-3/).

The debate is live because both sets of rules are moving at once and because the evidence is contested at the margins that matter. In Britain, the 2024 general election gave Labour 63 percent of seats on 34 percent of the vote and Reform UK 5 seats on 14 percent, the most disproportionate result in British history by the Electoral Reform Society’s measure; Wales elected its parliament by list proportional representation for the first time in May 2026; and British Social Attitudes found a record 60 percent of the public favoring a change to the voting system. On one side are the Liberal Democrats, the Greens, the Electoral Reform Society, Make Votes Matter and now the Labour leadership; on the other the Conservative Party, which says first-past-the-post keeps the link between an MP and a constituency and gives voters a clear choice of government, and Reform UK, which promised proportional representation in its 2024 “Contract with You” but whose chairman, Zia Yusuf, said in May 2025, as the party led the polls, that it was “firmly in favour of first past the post” (The Spectator, https://spectator.com/article/reform-changes-its-tune-on-voting-reform/). In the United States, Republicans, the Trump administration and groups such as the Heritage Foundation and the Honest Elections Project argue that photo ID, proof of citizenship and tighter mail voting are needed to keep ineligible people off the rolls and restore confidence; Democrats, the Brennan Center, the ACLU and voting rights groups argue that fraud is vanishingly rare and that the new requirements would block millions of eligible citizens. Between them are bodies such as the Bipartisan Policy Center and the Carter-Baker tradition, which pair ID with free identification and broad early and mail voting, and a reform movement led by FairVote and others that wants to keep single-winner races but let voters rank candidates, a change 19 states have now banned. Proportional representation for the US House, long a fringe idea, has an organized campaign in Fix Our House.

Context: “Readily” matters: most of the 21.3 million have such a document somewhere but could not easily produce it, for example because it is lost, held by a relative or in another state; just under 2 percent, over 3.8 million, have none at all. The same survey estimated that just over 1 percent of adult citizens, nearly 2.6 million people, have no government-issued photo ID of any kind, and that 15 percent, over 34.5 million, either lack a driver’s license or state ID or have one without their current name or address, which can cause problems under the strictest photo ID rules. The survey’s sponsors oppose proof-of-citizenship requirements, and the Brennan Center cites the 21.3 million figure as the number of eligible citizens the SAVE America Act would put at risk (https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/brennan-center-letter-senate-opposing-save-america-act). Supporters of the bill reply that most people without ready access could obtain a document, that the bill accepts several alternatives, and that registering is a one-time act. The disagreement is less about the survey number than about how many people would actually fail to register as a result.

## Questions

Question: Should a legislature’s seats track each party’s share of the vote, or should elections be built to produce a clear governing majority?
Tag: value
Poles: Seats match votes / Voters pick a government
Why contested: Every electoral system trades some proportionality for some decisiveness, and no evidence can say how much of one is worth how much of the other. The choice also has winners and losers, so parties’ positions tend to follow their interests, which makes each side suspicious of the other’s principles.
Seats match votes: A parliament exists to represent the people, and a system that gives one party 63 percent of seats on 34 percent of the vote, or Reform UK five seats on more than four million votes, fails that basic test. Proportional systems give every vote similar weight wherever it is cast, end the need to vote tactically for the “least bad” viable candidate, and force governments to assemble a real majority of voters’ representatives, which is what most democracies in Europe and much of the world already do. A government that must win the agreement of parties representing more than half the electorate has a stronger democratic claim than one that won a plurality in the right places.
Voters pick a government: A general election chooses a government as well as a legislature, and first-past-the-post lets voters see which party will govern and on what program before they vote, then remove it cleanly when it fails. Under proportional representation the government is assembled after the election in negotiations among party leaders, manifestos become bargaining chips, and a small party can hold the balance of power out of proportion to its votes. Each voter also has one identifiable local representative to hold to account. On this view, proportionality is one democratic value among several, and accountability and clarity of choice matter more.

Question: How often do ineligible people register or vote, and how much would stricter verification catch?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Rare and caught / Underdetected risk
Why contested: Ineligible voting is a crime that is hard to observe directly, so each side reads the same small numbers differently: as proof the problem is tiny, or as the visible tip of something larger. Audits are improving but the federal SAVE database itself has produced errors, so even new data are disputed.
Rare and caught: Every systematic search has found very little. Georgia’s 2024 audit of 8.2 million registrations found 20 noncitizens; the Kansas trial court found at most 39 noncitizens on the rolls in 19 years while the state’s proof-of-citizenship rule blocked 31,089 applicants; Britain’s Electoral Commission and the Electoral Reform Society report that in-person impersonation is extremely rare. Registration already requires an attestation of citizenship under penalty of perjury, a crime that risks deportation, and existing database checks, audits and prosecutions catch the few cases that occur. Building new barriers for a problem this small, on this view, costs far more eligible votes than it saves.
Underdetected risk: Those who favor tighter checks argue that the low numbers reflect how weakly eligibility is checked, not how rarely it is violated: most states verify citizenship only by self-attestation, and audits find only what their data can match. The Heritage Foundation’s database listed more than 1,400 proven cases of election fraud by January 2023 (https://www.heritage.org/election-integrity/commentary/election-fraud-database-tops-1400-cases), courts have overturned results over absentee ballot fraud, as in North Carolina’s 9th Congressional District in 2018, and the 2024 US House race in California’s 13th District was decided by 187 votes. When elections are this close and confidence this low, they argue, closing gaps before they are exploited is prudent, and the SAVE database checks begun in 2025 have flagged thousands of registrations for review.

Question: Do photo ID and proof-of-citizenship requirements keep eligible citizens from voting?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real barrier / Negligible effect
Why contested: The best nationwide study finds no measurable average effect of strict ID on turnout, while surveys find millions of citizens lack the required documents and specific rollouts, such as Kansas’s, blocked tens of thousands of applicants. The two kinds of evidence answer different questions, and each side leads with the one that supports it.
Real barrier: Over 9 percent of voting-age citizens, about 21.3 million people, cannot readily lay hands on a birth certificate or passport, and 2.6 million have no government photo ID at all, disproportionately young, low-income, rural, Black and Hispanic citizens and women whose names changed at marriage. Kansas’s proof-of-citizenship law blocked 31,089 applicants; the GAO found turnout fell 2 to 3 points more in Kansas and Tennessee after they tightened ID rules; in Britain about 16,000 people were turned away in 2024. Averages can hide concentrated harm, and mobilization by campaigns may mask a burden that falls on people who should not need a campaign to vote.
Negligible effect: The largest study, covering every state over a decade and 1.6 billion observations, found strict ID laws changed turnout by about zero for every racial, age, gender and party group. Most citizens already carry ID, states with ID laws provide free cards, and voters adapt; in Georgia, which requires photo ID and tightened ID rules for mail ballots in 2021, more than 2 million people had voted early by 23 October 2024. In Britain 0.08 percent of in-person voters could not vote for lack of ID, and two-thirds of those initially turned away came back. Survey counts of people “without ready access” to documents overstate the problem, since most could obtain them once they had a reason to.

Question: Does proportional representation calm polarization and extremism, or give fringe parties more power?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Moderates politics / Empowers extremes
Why contested: Countries differ in too many ways for clean comparisons, and both sides can point to cases: multiparty consensus democracies in northern Europe, and coalition crises or hard-right gains in proportional systems. The answer may depend on details such as district size and thresholds.
Moderates politics: Advocates such as Lee Drutman argue that two-party systems turn every election into a binary contest over national identity, feeding a “doom loop” of negative partisanship, while multiparty systems let voters support parties that reflect their views and force those parties to cooperate in shifting coalitions. Under proportional representation an extreme party gets its share of seats but cannot win a majority on a minority of the vote, whereas first-past-the-post can hand a Commons majority to a movement with less than 30 percent of the vote, the danger Andy Burnham cited in calling for reform. Bringing protest votes inside parliament, on this view, is safer than leaving them unrepresented and resentful.
Empowers extremes: Those who defend majoritarian systems argue that the high bar to winning seats forces parties to build broad coalitions before the election and pulls them toward the median voter, keeping fringe movements out of government. Proportional systems let small parties enter parliament on a few percent of the vote and sometimes make them kingmakers, giving them leverage over policy far beyond their support; several European democracies have seen hard-right and hard-left parties grow and enter government through coalition. Single-member districts also let voters remove a specific representative, which they see as a check on extremists that party lists lack.

Question: Do proportional systems produce less stable or less accountable governments?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Stable and accountable / Gridlock and deal-making
Why contested: Stability can be measured by how long governments last, how long they take to form, or how consistent policy is, and the measures point different ways. Britain’s own record since 2016, with several prime ministers in a decade under first-past-the-post, has unsettled the old assumption that majoritarian systems are the stable ones.
Stable and accountable: Reformers note that Germany, the Nordic countries and the Netherlands have long been governed by proportional systems with steady policy and strong economies, while Britain under first-past-the-post has seen sharp swings between single-party governments that reverse one another’s policies. Burnham pointed to seven prime ministers in ten years. Coalitions, on this view, give policy continuity because the center of the coalition changes less than the governing party does in a two-party system, and the 2026 Senedd election produced a workable outcome under list proportional representation.
Gridlock and deal-making: Defenders of majoritarian systems point to long government-formation periods under proportional rules, such as Belgium’s more than 500 days without a full government in 2010 and 2011, and to fragile coalitions that fall over single issues. When no party can win a majority, voters cannot remove a government whose parties reassemble in a new combination, and policy is decided in private bargaining after the vote. A single-party government may make mistakes, but voters know whom to blame.

Question: Should any voter be able to vote by mail without giving a reason?
Tag: value
Poles: Mail voting for all / In person by default
Why contested: Mail voting trades the supervision of a polling place for convenience and participation, and the parties now split sharply: 83 percent of Democrats but 32 percent of Republicans back letting any voter vote by mail. Both sides agree mail ballots need safeguards but disagree on whether the remaining risk is acceptable.
Mail voting for all: About a third of 2024 voters used it, including large numbers of older, rural, disabled and military voters of both parties; eight states run elections mainly by mail with high turnout and few problems; and 98.8 percent of returned mail ballots were counted in 2024, with signature checks, ballot tracking and cure processes catching errors. Restricting it would make voting harder for millions to address risks that audits in mail-voting states have not found, and the Supreme Court’s Watson decision confirmed that states may set their own receipt deadlines.
In person by default: Voting in person, with ID, at a supervised polling place is the most secure way to confirm that the right person cast a ballot freely and in secret; ballots sent to addresses on outdated rolls, collected by third parties or completed at home cannot be supervised in the same way. North Carolina’s 2018 congressional election was overturned for absentee ballot fraud, and Britain’s Tower Hamlets mayoral election was voided in 2015 for corrupt practices including postal vote abuses. Those who hold this view would keep mail voting for people with a real need, such as the disabled, the elderly and the military, rather than as a default.

Question: Do stricter election rules restore public confidence, or does confidence depend on what leaders say?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Rules build trust / Trust follows leaders
Why contested: Confidence in US elections has collapsed among Republicans, from 55 percent in 2016 to 28 percent in 2024, but it is unclear whether new safeguards would raise it or whether trust mainly follows elite cues about who won and whether the count was fair.
Rules build trust: Supporters of verification argue that visible safeguards, such as photo ID, citizenship checks and clean rolls, reassure voters regardless of party, which is why large majorities of both parties favor ID; that confidence fell as mail voting expanded and rules changed quickly in 2020; and that a system has to be trusted by losers as well as winners to work.
Trust follows leaders: Those who favor access note that Republican confidence fell most sharply after the 2020 election, when nothing about the rules had become less secure, and that confidence in one’s own local polling place stayed high across parties. In Britain, introducing photo ID left public confidence in polling-station security about where it had been before, according to Electoral Commission data cited by the Electoral Reform Society. On this view, confidence depends mainly on whether leaders accept results, and new restrictions to answer unfounded claims risk legitimizing them.

Question: When no candidate wins a majority, should voters’ ranked preferences decide the winner?
Tag: value
Poles: Rank the candidates / Most votes wins
Why contested: Ranked-choice voting changes what counts as winning, and both sides appeal to fairness: one to majority support, the other to a simple count of first choices. It has also become partisan in the United States, adopted mostly in Democratic-leaning places and banned in 19 mostly Republican-led states.
Rank the candidates: Advocates such as FairVote and Katherine Gehl argue that plurality voting lets candidates win with a minority when votes split, punishes voters who back a third choice, and rewards candidates who mobilize a narrow base. Ranking lets voters express their real preferences without fear of “spoilers,” ensures the winner has majority support among ballots still counting in the final round, and encourages candidates to seek second choices from rivals’ supporters, which they say reduces negative campaigning. Australia has used it for its House for a century, and Maine and Alaska use it statewide.
Most votes wins: Opponents, including the Foundation for Government Accountability and the legislatures that banned it, argue that ranked-choice voting departs from the principle that the candidate with the most votes wins, confuses voters, delays results while ballots are re-tabulated, and discards “exhausted” ballots whose ranked candidates have all been eliminated, so the winner’s “majority” is of remaining ballots only. A simple single mark is easier to understand and audit, which they regard as essential for trust.

Question: Who should set the rules for national elections: the national government or states and localities?
Tag: value
Poles: National standards / Local control
Why contested: In the United States the Constitution gives states primary control over elections but lets Congress alter the rules for federal elections, and both parties have proposed national rules when they hoped to win by them: Democrats with the Freedom to Vote Act, Republicans with the SAVE America Act and President Trump’s executive order. In Britain the question is whether a change as large as a new voting system needs a referendum, as in 2011, or can be decided by Parliament after a commission.
National standards: Citizens voting for the same Congress or Parliament should face the same basic rules wherever they live, whether those rules guarantee access (early voting, mail voting, automatic registration) or guarantee security (ID, proof of citizenship). National minimums protect voters from states that make voting too hard or too loose, and federal data such as citizenship and Social Security records can verify eligibility better than any state alone.
Local control: Decentralized administration across thousands of jurisdictions is itself a security feature, making nationwide manipulation hard, and lets states experiment and fit rules to local conditions, as Oregon and Utah did with mail voting and Georgia with its ID law. Federal mandates, from either party, impose unfunded costs on election officials, invite partisan overreach by whoever controls Washington, and in the case of an executive order raise constitutional questions the courts are still deciding.

## Positions

Ordering note: the first two camps divide over how votes become seats and the next two over who may vote and how; together they are the four poles a quick reader most needs. The fifth camp rejects the access-versus-security framing of the second debate, and the sixth is the hybrid of the first debate, keeping single-member districts but changing the ballot. Within the access and security debate, most members of the public hold a mix (large majorities favor both photo ID and early voting), which is closest to the fifth camp, but organized advocacy is concentrated in the third and fourth.

Camp: Adopt proportional representation
Adherents: Electoral Reform Society (institution), which campaigns to replace first-past-the-post with the single transferable vote: “In a representative democracy, we ought to have a Parliament that matches the way people actually vote” (https://electoral-reform.org.uk/whats-wrong-with-first-past-the-post/). Lee Drutman, senior fellow at New America and co-founder of Fix Our House, which campaigns for proportional representation for the US House (https://www.fixourhouse.org/; quoted on his 2026 polling in OPB/NPR, https://www.opb.org/article/2026/08/10/proportional-representation-has-majority-support-in-new-poll/). Also Make Votes Matter, Fair Vote Canada, the Liberal Democrats and the Green Party, and, since 29 September 2026, Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who has committed Labour to changing the system without yet naming one.
Case: A democracy’s legislature should look like its electorate. First-past-the-post turns 34 percent of the vote into a landslide and 14 percent into almost nothing, so most voters’ ballots elect no one and millions vote tactically against the party they fear rather than for the one they want. That distortion was tolerable when two parties took nine votes in ten; in today’s fragmented politics it hands total power to whoever wins a plurality in the right places, and can do so for a movement with under a third of the vote. Proportional representation gives every vote similar weight, lets new parties compete without “wasting” votes, and makes governments rest on a real majority of voters’ representatives. Most established democracies already use it without the instability its critics predict. In the United States, multi-member districts would end gerrymandering’s power to predetermine results, give Republicans in cities and Democrats in rural areas a voice, and break the two-party “doom loop” in which every election becomes an existential contest.
Best evidence: The 2024 UK result, with Labour on 63.2 percent of seats from 33.7 percent of votes and Reform UK and the Greens taking 9 seats on about 21 percent; 823,522 votes per Reform UK MP against 23,622 per Labour MP; a record 60 percent of Britons favoring change in British Social Attitudes, including majorities of every party’s supporters; the higher share of women elected under proportional and mixed systems in the IPU’s 2025 data (31.1 versus 25.5 percent); and the 2026 Senedd election, where list proportional representation produced a parliament far closer to the vote than Westminster’s.
Answer to the strongest objection: Critics say proportional representation replaces voters’ choice of government with backroom coalition deals and weakens the constituency link. Reformers answer that coalitions are negotiated in the open among parties that together represent a majority of voters, which is more democratic than a single party governing on a third of the vote; that systems such as the single transferable vote and mixed-member proportional keep local representatives, which is why Burnham’s spokesman stressed the constituency link; and that Britain’s own instability, with seven prime ministers in ten years, shows first-past-the-post does not guarantee stable government.
Variation: The camp divides over which system. The Electoral Reform Society prefers the single transferable vote in multi-member constituencies; others favor mixed-member proportional systems, as in Germany, New Zealand and the Scottish Parliament, which keep single-member seats topped up by lists; closed party lists, as used in Wales from 2026, are the least popular with British reformers. In the United States, Fix Our House and FairVote back the Fair Representation Act’s multi-member districts with ranked ballots, while others favor open-list or party-list systems.

Camp: Keep first-past-the-post
Adherents: The Conservative Party (institution), whose 2024 manifesto said “We remain committed to the First Past the Post system for elections, maintaining the direct link with the local voter” (https://public.conservatives.com/static/documents/GE2024/Conservative-Manifesto-GE2024.pdf); its leader Kemi Badenoch called Burnham’s commission on 1 October 2026 “a distraction and something that he is trying to do to lock in the left” (https://www.irishnews.com/news/uk/badenoch-tories-will-turn-tide-on-burnhams-destructive-socialist-agenda-BR5SJXJKKRLKNAITBQQAYAW5VQ/). Richard Johnson, Queen Mary University of London, whose 2025 policy brief argues that first-past-the-post outperforms proportional systems on “prospective government clarity” and “retrospective government accountability” (https://www.qmul.ac.uk/mei/media/mei/qm-policy-hub/briefs/Queen-Mary-Policy-Brief---Is-First-Past-the-Post-Undemocratic.pdf). Also Reform UK’s leadership since 2025, and in the United States the Foundation for Government Accountability and the legislatures that have banned ranked-choice voting in favor of plurality rules.
Case: An election is not an opinion poll that a parliament should mirror; it is the moment voters choose who governs and judge who has governed. First-past-the-post lets them do both: parties put a program to the country, the winner governs on it, and if it fails voters can throw it out in a single stroke. Every voter has one MP who answers for a defined place and can be removed personally. Proportional systems make coalition government the norm, so the real choice of government and program is made by party leaders after the votes are counted, small parties can extract concessions far beyond their support, and voters often cannot remove a party that keeps returning in new combinations. The demand for change also tends to come from parties that would gain seats from it, and voters rejected change by two to one when asked in the 2011 referendum.
Best evidence: Johnson’s 2024 election polling, which found 60 percent support and 8 percent opposition for having just one MP per constituency, and 65 percent support and 10 percent opposition for electing the candidate with the most first-preference votes; the 67.9 percent vote to keep first-past-the-post in the 2011 alternative vote referendum; the repeated decisive removal of governments in 1979, 1997 and 2024; long government-formation periods in proportional systems, such as Belgium’s more than 500 days without a full government in 2010 and 2011; and the 2026 Senedd result, in which no party won a majority and the government depends on post-election arrangements.
Answer to the strongest objection: Reformers say first-past-the-post wastes votes and gave Labour a landslide on a third of the vote. Defenders answer that every system distorts something, and this one distorts in favor of a clear outcome that voters can punish next time, as they punished the Conservatives in 2024; that the low votes-per-seat cost of the winner is the price of decisive government, not a defect; and that the British Social Attitudes question frames change as “fairer” and the status quo only as “effective,” which is not the same as support for any particular proportional system, as the 2011 referendum showed.
Variation: Some in this camp would accept modest changes that keep single-member seats, such as the Jenkins Commission’s 1998 “AV plus” proposal of single-member seats topped up by a small number of list members. In the United States the parallel argument is a defense of single-member plurality districts and a rejection of ranked-choice voting, made mostly by Republicans; Reform UK’s shift shows that a party’s view of the system often follows its prospects under it.

Camp: Tighten voter eligibility checks
Adherents: The Trump administration (institution), whose Executive Order 14399 of 31 March 2026 directs federal citizenship lists for every state and restricts mail ballots to people on them, stating that “The Federal government has a duty to prevent violations of Federal criminal law in Federal elections and to maintain public confidence in election outcomes” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/03/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-ensures-citizenship-verification-and-voter-eligibility-in-federal-elections/). Senator Mike Lee, sponsor of the SAVE America Act, which would require documentary proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote, “to ensure that our federal elections are decided by U.S. citizens—and U.S. citizens alone” (https://www.lee.senate.gov/2026/1/senator-mike-lee-introduces-save-america-act-with-congressman-chip-roy). Also the Heritage Foundation, the Honest Elections Project, the Republican National Committee, Reform California (sponsor of California’s Proposition 39 voter ID measure), and in Britain the Conservative Party, which introduced photo ID at polling stations.
Case: Only citizens may vote in federal elections, yet in most states citizenship is checked only by a box the applicant ticks. Voter rolls are full of people who have moved or died, mail ballots go out to addresses no one has verified, and ballots are counted that no official ever saw cast. Asking people to show who they are, as they must to board a plane or open a bank account, and to prove citizenship once when they register, is a modest, popular safeguard that most democracies take for granted. Elections are now decided by thousands or hundreds of votes, and the losing side of a close election must be able to trust the result; visible checks give it reason to. Mail voting should be the exception for those who need it, with secure envelopes and verification, not the default.
Best evidence: Public support, with 83 percent of Americans favoring photo ID (Pew, 2025), including 71 percent of Democrats, and 83 percent favoring proof of citizenship at first registration (Gallup, 2024); Heritage’s database of more than 1,400 proven fraud cases by January 2023; the overturning of North Carolina’s 2018 congressional election for absentee ballot fraud and the voiding of the 2014 Tower Hamlets mayoral election in London for corrupt practices; the 2,724 Texas registrations SAVE flagged as potential noncitizens; and the Cantoni and Pons finding that strict ID laws did not reduce turnout, which they read as showing the burden objection is overstated.
Answer to the strongest objection: Critics say fraud is vanishingly rare and the new requirements would block millions of eligible citizens. This camp answers that the low numbers reflect weak detection, since self-attestation is not verification and audits find only what they can match; that every ineligible ballot cancels an eligible one; that ID laws have not reduced turnout where adopted, and states provide free IDs; and that people who lack ready access to a birth certificate can obtain one once, for a right they will use for life. Kansas’s problems, they argue, came from a poorly run rollout before today’s federal databases.
Variation: The camp ranges from photo ID at the polls, which many state Republicans and some Democrats support, to documentary proof of citizenship for registration, federal citizenship lists, ending most mail voting and Election Day receipt deadlines, which divide even Republicans. Some members favor state action only and oppose federal mandates.

Camp: Expand access to voting
Adherents: The Brennan Center for Justice (institution), which told the Senate on 17 March 2026 that the SAVE America Act “would be the most restrictive voting bill ever passed by Congress” and would block millions of eligible citizens (https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/brennan-center-letter-senate-opposing-save-america-act). Senator Alex Padilla, ranking member of the Senate Rules Committee, who led opposition to the bill and said its ID rule “would impose a far stricter and more burdensome federal standard than all 50 states” (https://www.padilla.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/padilla-leads-final-pre-midterm-effort-to-defeat-anti-voter-save-act-condemns-trump-corruption/). Also the ACLU, the League of Women Voters, Democratic congressional leaders, VoteRiders, and in Britain the Electoral Reform Society, which wants the voter ID requirement scrapped.
Case: Voting is a right of citizenship, and the system should make it easy for every eligible citizen to use it: automatic registration, early voting, mail voting for anyone who wants it, and no documentary hurdles that eligible people cannot clear. The threat these new rules claim to answer barely exists. Every serious search for noncitizen or impersonation voting finds a handful of cases among millions, already caught by existing checks and punished by severe penalties. The burdens, by contrast, are concrete: 21.3 million citizens cannot readily produce a birth certificate or passport, and they are disproportionately young, poor, Black, Hispanic, rural, or married women whose names changed. Rules that block thousands of eligible voters to stop a few dozen ineligible ones get the trade-off backwards, and claims of fraud used to justify them have themselves damaged trust in elections.
Best evidence: Kansas, where the proof-of-citizenship rule canceled or suspended 31,089 applications while at most 39 noncitizens had reached the rolls in 19 years, and courts struck it down; Georgia’s audit finding 20 noncitizens among 8.2 million registrants; the CDCE and Brennan Center survey of documentary access; the GAO’s finding of turnout drops in Kansas and Tennessee after ID laws; 584,463 mail ballots rejected in 2024, mostly over signatures, as a sign that verification already works and already costs votes; and Britain’s 16,000 people turned away for lack of ID in 2024 with no sign that public confidence in polling-station security rose.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that ID is popular, common in other democracies and has not been shown to reduce turnout overall. This camp answers that other democracies with ID requirements issue national ID cards free and register voters automatically, so the comparison supports universal access to ID rather than requirements without it; that average turnout effects near zero can coexist with real harm to specific voters, offset by mobilization campaigns that should not be needed; and that proof-of-citizenship rules, unlike ID at the polls, have a documented record of blocking eligible applicants at scale.
Variation: Members differ on photo ID itself. Some, including many Democratic voters, accept ID at the polls if it is free, flexible and paired with alternatives such as signed affidavits, while the Brennan Center and ACLU oppose strict ID laws. Nearly all oppose documentary proof of citizenship for registration and restrictions on mail voting.

Camp: Pair voter ID with guaranteed access
Adherents: The Bipartisan Policy Center (institution), whose 2022 working group recommended that “Pairing voter identification standards with the expansion of voting by mail and early voting options addresses both Republican and Democrat priorities” (https://bipartisanpolicy.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/AchievableFederalElectionReformV2.pdf). Former President Jimmy Carter and former Secretary of State James A. Baker III, whose 2005 Commission on Federal Election Reform proposed a photo ID requirement phased in over five years with states providing free ID cards and mobile units to issue IDs and register voters, concluding that “a free and fair election requires both ballot security and full access to voting” (The Carter Center, https://www.cartercenter.org/news/voter_id/). Also Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger, who defends Georgia’s photo ID requirement alongside heavy early voting, saying in October 2024, “This is proof in Georgia, it’s easy to vote and hard to cheat” (Atlanta News First, https://www.atlantanewsfirst.com/2024/10/23/secretary-state-give-update-early-voting-georgia/); the same slogan is used by the Honest Elections Project, in the verification camp.
Case: The fight between access and security is a false choice that both parties find useful. Most Americans want both, and so should the law: a simple identity check for every voter, in person and by mail, together with free IDs issued proactively, automatic or easy registration, ample early voting and mail voting with verification and a chance to fix errors. Each side gets the guarantee it says it needs, and each loses the argument it uses against the other. Rules built this way can be adopted on a bipartisan basis, which is the only way to make them durable and to give both winners and losers reason to trust the result.
Best evidence: Polling showing that the same public backs both sets of measures, with 83 percent for photo ID and 80 percent for two weeks of early voting (Pew, 2025); the Cantoni and Pons finding that strict ID laws have had no measurable average effect on turnout, which suggests ID can be required without depressing participation; Georgia’s heavy early voting under a photo ID regime, with more than 2 million early votes, about 27 percent of registered voters, cast by 23 October 2024 (Atlanta News First, https://www.atlantanewsfirst.com/2024/10/23/secretary-state-give-update-early-voting-georgia/); and the low mail-ballot rejection rate where signatures or ID numbers are checked and voters can cure problems.
Answer to the strongest objection: Access advocates object that any ID requirement will exclude someone, and verification advocates that ID is meaningless without proof of citizenship and limits on mail voting. This camp answers that the first objection is met by making ID free, easy to obtain and broadly defined, with fallback options, and the second by identity checks on mail ballots and better use of existing government data to verify citizenship, rather than requirements that block eligible applicants as Kansas’s did. Carter and Baker themselves warned that ID laws imposed abruptly, without free cards and outreach, failed their test.
Variation: Members differ on how strict the ID should be (photo only or any of several documents), whether proof of citizenship should be checked by documents or by databases, and whether national standards or state laws should implement the bargain. Britain’s Labour government sits loosely in this camp: it kept the voter ID rule it inherited while widening the accepted documents to bank cards and adding automatic registration.

Camp: Rank candidates in single-winner races
Adherents: FairVote (institution), which campaigns for ranked-choice voting because it means “better choices, better campaigns, and better representation” (https://fairvote.org/our-reforms/ranked-choice-voting/). Katherine Gehl, founder of the Institute for Political Innovation, who advocates “Final Five Voting,” a nonpartisan top-five primary followed by a ranked-choice general election (The Fulcrum, https://thefulcrum.us/electoral-reforms/katherine-gehl). Also Alaskans for Better Elections, Unite America, and in Australia the long-standing use of the alternative vote for the House of Representatives.
Case: The problem with American and British elections is less that districts elect one person than that the winner can be chosen by a minority and voters are punished for voting their conscience. Ranking candidates solves both without abandoning the local, accountable representative: if no one has a majority, the last-place candidate is eliminated and those ballots move to their next choice until someone does. Third-party and independent candidates can run without being spoilers, candidates have to reach beyond their base for second preferences, and costly runoff elections are folded into one. It is a practical reform that cities and states can adopt today, and it has been used for a century in Australia and for decades in Ireland’s presidential elections.
Best evidence: Adoption in 49 US jurisdictions reaching nearly 14 million voters, including statewide use in Maine and Alaska and New York City’s primaries, by FairVote’s March 2026 count; Alaska voters’ narrow retention of their system in 2024; Australia’s long use of the alternative vote alongside compulsory voting and 90.7 percent turnout in 2025; and FairVote’s count that 31 of 39 places holding ranked-choice elections in the two years to June 2025 released results on Election Day or the day after (https://fairvote.org/79-of-jurisdictions-release-rcv-results-within-24-hours/).
Answer to the strongest objection: Opponents say ranked-choice voting is confusing, slow, and discards exhausted ballots, so the winner’s majority is not real. Advocates answer that voters in jurisdictions using it report understanding it and rank candidates in large numbers, that exhausted ballots are ballots whose voters chose not to rank the finalists, just as voters skip a separate runoff, and that results are slower only where tabulation is centralized. They read the wave of state bans as partisan, adopted before most of those states’ voters had ever used the system.
Variation: Many in this camp, including FairVote, also back multi-winner ranked-choice voting (the single transferable vote) in multi-member districts, which belongs to the proportional representation camp; others, including Gehl, want ranked ballots only in single-winner races combined with nonpartisan primaries. In Britain, the alternative vote was the option rejected in 2011, and most reformers there now favor proportional systems instead.

## Crux

When the rules for casting and counting votes force a trade-off, should they favor including and representing every eligible voter as fully as possible, or producing results that are decisive, verifiable and accepted by the side that loses, and how large are those trade-offs in practice?

What evidence could settle: Much of the disagreement is about magnitudes that can be measured. For voting rules: how many eligible citizens a given ID or proof-of-citizenship requirement actually stops from registering or voting, as opposed to how many lack documents on a survey; how many ineligible registrations and ballots exist and how many each safeguard would catch; and whether new safeguards raise the confidence of the side that doubts results or leave it unchanged. Kansas’s court record, Georgia’s audit, the federal SAVE checks now under way, turnout panels such as Cantoni and Pons’s and the GAO’s, and Britain’s Electoral Commission data are all partial answers that better audits and natural experiments could extend. For electoral systems: whether proportional representation in practice lowers or raises polarization, extremist influence, policy swings and government instability compared with single-member systems, and whether ranked ballots change who wins and how candidates campaign. Comparative and within-country studies, including Wales’s first list election and any reform Britain’s new commission recommends, can narrow these questions even if they cannot close them.

What is a value choice: Even with perfect data, people would weigh the outcomes differently. One eligible citizen turned away and one ineligible ballot counted are both errors, and no measurement says how many of one is worth one of the other. A parliament that mirrors the electorate and a government that voters choose directly and can remove cleanly are both democratic ideals, and a system cannot maximize both. Some hold that an election’s legitimacy rests mainly on everyone eligible being able to take part and be represented; others that it rests mainly on the result being checkable and trusted by those who lost. Which of these comes first, and who should decide the rules when the parties writing them stand to gain or lose by them, are questions for citizens, not evidence.
