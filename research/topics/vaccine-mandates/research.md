# Vaccine Mandates — research

## Overview

Governments and employers can make vaccination a condition of something people want or need: enrolling in school or child care, keeping a job in a hospital or the military, boarding a plane, or entering a restaurant or concert hall. This topic covers those requirements and the exemptions carved out of them (medical, religious, personal belief), who gets to set them (legislatures, health officials, employers, courts), and the standing emergency powers that let governors and health officials order vaccination, testing, masking, closures or quarantines when the next pandemic arrives. It covers the United States in most detail, because that is where school-entry requirements are both oldest and most contested, and compares Europe, where more than half of EU and EEA countries require no vaccines at all. It does not cover whether particular vaccines are safe or effective as a scientific matter, except where that dispute drives the argument over compulsion; it does not cover research on dangerous pathogens (see gain-of-function-research), antimicrobial resistance, or presidential power in general (see executive-power-separation).

Why now: on 10 August 2026 President Trump signed Executive Order 14420, “Delivering Gold Standard Childhood Vaccine Recommendations for Americans” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/eo-14420.pdf). It declares a shorter list of vaccines recommended for all children (11 diseases, the list a January 2026 CDC schedule change cut from 17 before a federal court stayed that change in March: https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/childhood-vaccines/state-us-vaccine-policy-special-edition-mar-17-2026), advises states to “consider updating” their school-entry requirements to match, and directs the Attorney General to back lawsuits against states that deny religious exemptions, while the Departments of Justice, Education and HHS must ensure that grantees, including states, provide religious and medical exemptions. A week later, on 17 August, the CDC reported that 4.2 percent of US kindergartners in the 2025–26 school year had an exemption from at least one required vaccine, the highest rate on record, and that MMR coverage had fallen to 92.4 percent (https://www.cdc.gov/schoolvaxview/data/index.html). The United States had 3,659 confirmed measles cases in 2026 as of 24 September, the most since 1991 (https://www.cdc.gov/measles/data-research/index.html), and a regional commission is due to review in November 2026 whether the country has lost the measles elimination status it has held since 2000 (https://www.paho.org/en/news/2-3-2026-update-review-measles-elimination-status). On 8 September the Supreme Court declined, with Justices Samuel Alito and Neil Gorsuch noting they would have granted relief, to let a New York kindergartner attend school under a religious exemption the state abolished in 2019 (https://thehill.com/regulation/court-battles/6077588-supreme-court-vaccine-religious-exemption-new-york/).

The debate is live because two long-settled arrangements are being reopened at once. School-entry requirements date to the nineteenth century, and the Supreme Court upheld compulsory vaccination in Jacobson v. Massachusetts (1905) and school vaccination requirements in Zucht v. King (1922). Yet the COVID-19 pandemic brought workplace and public-venue mandates to adults on a scale not seen before, and the backlash reached back to childhood requirements. Medical bodies such as the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Medical Association want nonmedical exemptions abolished, as California, Connecticut, Maine and New York have done. The federal administration, West Virginia’s governor and a group of 20 state attorneys general want religious exemptions protected everywhere. Florida’s surgeon general and a coalition that includes Children’s Health Defense want school mandates ended outright, though Florida’s legislature declined to do so in 2026. On emergency powers, legislatures in at least 26 states had curbed by late 2021 what governors and health officials can order, a trend public health lawyers warn will leave the country slower in the next pandemic. Many European countries, meanwhile, reach high coverage with no mandates at all, which both sides cite.

### 2. Kindergarten coverage with two doses of MMR vaccine
- Value: 92.4
- Unit: percent of kindergartners
- Comparison (percent of US kindergartners with two MMR doses, by school year): 2016–17 95.0; 2019–20 95.2; 2020–21 93.9; 2021–22 93.0; 2022–23 93.1; 2023–24 92.7; 2024–25 92.5; 2025–26 92.4
- Data year: 2025–26 school year
- Source label: CDC, SchoolVaxView, Vaccination Coverage and Exemptions among Kindergartners
- Source URL: https://www.cdc.gov/schoolvaxview/data/index.html
- Note: 92.4 percent of US kindergartners had both MMR doses in 2025–26, below the 95 percent generally needed to stop measles spreading.
- Context: The CDC estimates about 280,000 kindergartners began school in 2025–26 without documentation of a complete MMR series. Coverage has fallen every year since 2019–20 except for a flat 2022–23. The 95 percent figure is the WHO’s threshold for preventing measles outbreaks, which follows from how contagious measles is: one infected person can infect up to 18 others (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles). Coverage with DTaP (92.0 percent) and polio (92.4 percent) fell too. Ten states had MMR coverage of 95 percent or more in 2025–26, against 19 in 2019–20 (https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20260818/cdc-kindergarten-vaccine-exemptions-exceed-4-nationwide).

- Unit: percent of US adults supporting a state policy letting parents decline vaccination for religious reasons
- Comparison (percent supporting, spring 2019 then January 2025): opt-out for religious reasons 20 then 39; opt-out for personal or philosophical reasons 17 then 35; opt-out for medical reasons 36 then 63; state requiring vaccination as a condition of school attendance 71 then 52

- Source label: Annenberg Public Policy Center, University of Pennsylvania

- Value: 95.6
- Unit: percent of kindergartners with all required vaccines
- Comparison (percent with all required immunizations, by school year): 2013–14 90.2; 2014–15 90.4; 2015–16 92.8; 2016–17 95.6; 2017–18 95.1; 2018–19 94.8; 2019–20 94.3
- Data year: 2013–14 to 2019–20
- Source label: California Department of Public Health, 2019–2020 Kindergarten Immunization Assessment
- Source URL: https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CID/DCDC/CDPH%20Document%20Library/Immunization/archive/school/2019-2020CAKindergartenSummaryReport.pdf
- Note: The share of California kindergartners with all required vaccines rose from 90.4 percent in 2014–15 to 95.6 percent in 2016–17, the first year SB 277 fully applied.

## Questions

### 1. Do strict school vaccine requirements substantially raise coverage and prevent outbreaks?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Requirements raise coverage” / “Little lasting effect”
- Why contested: Coverage is highest in states and countries with strict requirements, and it rose where requirements were tightened. But the families who refuse are few and determined, some find other routes (medical exemptions, home schooling, virtual schools), and several European countries with no mandates at all report high coverage. Whether strictness itself does the work, or merely tracks places that already trust vaccines, decides how much a mandate is worth.
- Case for “Requirements raise coverage”: When California ended personal-belief exemptions, the share of kindergartners fully vaccinated rose from 90.4 percent in 2014–15 to 95.6 percent in 2016–17. Italy’s 2017 law lifted measles coverage from 87.3 percent to 94.1 percent within two years. Across 29 European countries, mandatory vaccination was associated with 3.71 percentage points higher measles coverage, and with lower measles incidence where no nonmedical exemptions were allowed. In 2025–26 the four US states allowing only medical exemptions, plus West Virginia, had MMR coverage from 95.7 to 98.9 percent, while Idaho, with the most exemptions, had 75.2 percent. For measles, a few points separate a protected school from an outbreak, so a policy that moves coverage by a few points is decisive.
- Case for “Little lasting effect”: The strictest laws mostly reshuffle the same families. California’s medical exemptions rose fivefold after SB 277, and when those were tightened, the share of children in settings the requirement did not reach rose from 1.62 to 2.13 percent. The European evidence is cross-country correlation that cannot separate mandates from the conditions that produce them, and 17 of 30 EU/EEA countries, including Sweden, Norway and Denmark, require no vaccines. What drives coverage down is falling trust (Gallup finds the share calling childhood vaccination extremely important fell from 58 percent in 2019 to 40 percent in 2024), and a law cannot compel trust. Access, reminders, and pediatricians who take the time to talk to parents move coverage without the political costs.

### 2. Should parents be able to opt out of school vaccine requirements for religious or conscience reasons?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “Protect conscience” / “Protect classmates”
- Why contested: This is a direct conflict between two things most people value: a family’s freedom to raise its children by its own religious or moral lights, and every child’s safety at school, including children who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons. Public support for religious opt-outs roughly doubled between 2019 and 2025 (from 20 to 39 percent in Annenberg’s surveys), while most medical and public health bodies want them abolished.
- Case for “Protect conscience”: Freedom of conscience is among the first liberties, and forcing parents to choose between their faith and their child’s education is exactly the kind of coercion religious liberty protections exist to prevent. The number of objectors is small, and most states have kept religious exemptions for decades while achieving high coverage. Exemptions also release pressure: a family that can opt out stays in the public school and in contact with the health system, while a family denied one may leave both. Where outbreaks occur, health officials can exclude unvaccinated children temporarily without abolishing the exemption.
- Case for “Protect classmates”: A vaccine requirement protects children who cannot be vaccinated because of cancer treatment, immune disorders or age, and they cannot choose their classmates. Exemptions cluster geographically, in particular schools and communities, so a low state average can hide individual schools with far higher rates, well past the point where measles spreads. Few religions actually forbid vaccination, and many exemptions labeled religious are personal objections. The parent’s liberty ends where it puts other people’s children at risk, and no one is forced to vaccinate: a family can educate at home.

### 3. Does the Constitution require states to offer religious exemptions from school vaccine requirements?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “Exemption required” / “States may refuse”
- Why contested: For a century courts treated school vaccination requirements as valid exercises of state power (Zucht v. King, 1922), and neutral, generally applicable laws need not exempt religious objectors (Employment Division v. Smith, 1990). Recent Supreme Court decisions have strengthened parental and religious claims: in December 2025 the Court vacated an appeals ruling upholding New York’s repeal of its religious exemption and sent it back for reconsideration in light of Mahmoud v. Taylor (2025), which upheld parents’ right to opt children out of lessons that burden their religion. The Second Circuit again upheld New York’s law on 30 June 2026, and the plaintiffs’ lawyers plan to return to the Supreme Court; on 8 September 2026 the Court refused emergency relief to a different New York family, with two justices noting they would have granted it.
- Case for “Exemption required”: A state that exempts children for medical reasons but not religious ones treats secular reasons better than religious ones, which the Court’s recent free-exercise decisions forbid. Mahmoud v. Taylor holds that the government may not condition a public benefit, including school, on parents giving up the religious upbringing of their children, and Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972) already protected Amish families from compulsory schooling rules. Denying an Amish community school the right to operate unless every child is vaccinated is a heavier burden than the curriculum opt-out Mahmoud protected.
- Case for “States may refuse”: Medical exemptions protect the child who would be harmed by a vaccine, which serves the law’s own health purpose; religious exemptions do not, so they are not comparable. Jacobson v. Massachusetts (1905) and Prince v. Massachusetts (1944), which said the right to practice religion “does not include liberty to expose the community or the child to communicable disease,” remain good law. Since Mahmoud, the Second Circuit (Miller v. McDonald, June 2026) and, 2–1, the Fourth Circuit (Perry v. Marteney, April 2026, on West Virginia’s law: https://www.wdtv.com/2026/04/09/appeals-court-sides-with-upshur-county-school-officials-vaccine-exemption-case/) have upheld school requirements that allow no religious exemption.

### 4. Do vaccine mandates erode trust and reduce vaccination in the long run?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Mandates backfire” / “Mandates build habits”
- Why contested: COVID-19 mandates were followed by a sharp partisan fall in trust in vaccines and health agencies, and by rising exemptions from childhood vaccines. Whether the mandates caused that fall, or merely coincided with a broader political realignment, is hard to establish, and the answer shapes how any future emergency should be handled.
- Case for “Mandates backfire”: Coercion signals that officials do not trust people to decide, and people return the distrust. Bardosh and colleagues (BMJ Global Health, 2022) argued that mandates and passports risked “detrimental long-term impacts on trust in government and scientific institutions” and lower uptake of future vaccines, including routine immunizations. That is what followed: nonmedical kindergarten exemptions doubled from 1.9 percent in 2020–21 to 4.0 percent in 2025–26, support for school requirements in Annenberg’s surveys fell from 71 to 52 percent between 2019 and 2025, and the share of Republicans calling childhood vaccination extremely important halved. A policy that raises one year’s uptake while convincing a third of the country that public health is an adversary is a net loss.
- Case for “Mandates build habits”: Studies of actual behavior, as opposed to stated attitudes, mostly find no backfire. A review of COVID-19 mandates, passports and incentives found “little evidence that these policies backfire” at the population level (Fayaz-Farkhad and Jung, 2024), certificates in France and Italy raised uptake among the young (Mills and Rüttenauer, 2021), and US states that mandated COVID-19 vaccination saw higher, not lower, take-up of later boosters and flu vaccines (Fitzgerald, PNAS, 2024: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39348537/). The fall in trust is concentrated among Republicans in every state, including states that never mandated anything, which points to political messaging rather than mandates as the cause.

### 5. Who should decide on emergency measures in the next pandemic: health officials acting quickly, or legislatures?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “Officials act fast” / “Legislatures decide”
- Why contested: Epidemics move in days, and legislatures meet for months; but orders that close businesses, require vaccination or restrict gatherings affect basic liberties for long periods. After COVID-19, 43 states and DC enacted 191 laws on public health authority between January 2021 and May 2022, 65 of them contracting emergency powers and 17 expanding them.
- Case for “Officials act fast”: The value of early action is measured in lives, because infections grow exponentially and every week of delay multiplies the eventual toll. Health officials have the expertise and the information; legislatures are slower, more partisan and poorly suited to evaluating fast-changing evidence. Courts already check abuses, as the Supreme Court did when it blocked the OSHA rule. Laws that let legislatures cancel orders, cap them at a few weeks or ban whole categories of response in advance mean that in the next pandemic, whatever it turns out to be, the tools may not be there when needed.
- Case for “Legislatures decide”: In a democracy the people’s elected representatives make the laws, and a declared emergency cannot become a way for one official to govern by decree for two years, as happened in several states during COVID-19. Requiring legislative ratification within weeks keeps fast action possible at the start while ensuring that long-lasting orders win public consent, which also makes them more likely to be obeyed. Many COVID-19 orders were inconsistent or poorly tailored (liquor stores open, churches closed), and legislative review would have caught more of those mistakes.

### 6. Did COVID-19 vaccine mandates for workers, students and public venues do more good than harm?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Saved lives” / “Cost outweighed benefit”
- Why contested: COVID-19 vaccines saved millions of lives, mostly among older adults, but mandates reached far beyond them: to healthy young workers, college students and restaurant customers. How much the mandates added to uptake, how much the vaccines reduced transmission to others once Omicron arrived, and how to weigh lost jobs and trust against lives saved are all disputed.
- Case for “Saved lives”: Mandates and certificates measurably raised vaccination (by about 4.9 million extra doses in France after certification, by one estimate), and more vaccination meant fewer deaths: estimates of lives saved by COVID-19 vaccines range from about 2.5 million over four years to 14.4 million in the first year alone. Health care worker requirements protected patients who had no choice about who treated them. The measures came at a moment of mass death, hospital overload and incomplete knowledge, and judging them by what was learned later is hindsight.
- Case for “Cost outweighed benefit”: The benefit of COVID-19 vaccines was concentrated in older people: Ioannidis and colleagues attribute 90 percent of lives saved to people aged 60 and over and 0.01 percent to children and adolescents. Once Omicron spread, vaccination did little to stop transmission, removing the main justification for requiring it of others, and most people already had some immunity from infection. Meanwhile more than 8,000 service members were discharged, health workers were fired during staffing shortages, and trust in vaccines fell in ways that now threaten measles control. Policies aimed at the people who most needed protection would have achieved most of the benefit.

### 7. Should school requirements cover the whole recommended schedule, or only vaccines against diseases that spread at school?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “Whole schedule” / “Only contagious threats”
- Why contested: The case for compulsion is strongest when an unvaccinated child endangers others, as with measles. Some required vaccines mainly protect the child (tetanus, which is not contagious) or target diseases spread mostly outside school (hepatitis B). Florida’s health department proposed in 2025 to drop hepatitis B, chickenpox, Hib and pneumococcal vaccines from its requirements, and the August 2026 executive order advises states to align requirements with a shorter federal list; a federal court had stayed the January 2026 schedule change in March.
- Case for “Whole schedule”: Each required vaccine prevents serious disease in children, and requirements are the main reason US coverage is high for all of them, not just measles. Hepatitis B vaccination has made childhood infection rare in the United States, and chickenpox and Hib do spread in schools and day care. Trimming the list on the theory that some vaccines “only” protect the child treats children as their parents’ property rather than as people the state also has a duty to protect. The schedule is set by expert review, and legislatures should not pick vaccines by political pressure.
- Case for “Only contagious threats”: The ethical basis for overriding a parent’s decision is protecting other people, and that basis is weakest for vaccines that protect only the recipient. Requiring vaccines against diseases that are rare in schools stretches the state’s power and spends public trust needed for the requirements that matter most, such as measles. Peer countries recommend fewer childhood vaccines and require fewer still; the US can keep strong requirements for the clearly contagious diseases and make the rest a recommendation.

### 8. Should the federal government set vaccine exemption rules for the states?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “National standard” / “States decide”
- Why contested: School vaccination law has always been state law, but the federal government has reached into it in both directions: through the COVID-19 workplace mandates of 2021, and now through an August 2026 executive order directing the Justice Department to back lawsuits against states that deny religious exemptions and conditioning federal grants on providing them. Positions often reverse depending on which direction the federal pressure runs.
- Case for “National standard”: Diseases do not stop at state lines, and a measles outbreak seeded in a low-coverage state spreads to others. Constitutional rights, whether to religious liberty or to equal protection, are national, and the federal government has a duty to enforce them against states that violate them. A patchwork of 50 rules also means a child’s protection depends on the accident of where she lives.
- Case for “States decide”: Public health and education are core state powers, and states have run school vaccine laws for 170 years with results that voters can judge and change. Federal overreach in either direction, whether mandating vaccines for 84 million private workers or pressing states to add exemptions, substitutes national politics for local accountability. States also serve as laboratories: California’s strict law and Idaho’s permissive one show the consequences of each approach, which a national rule would hide.

## Positions

### 1. Require vaccination for school, allowing medical exemptions only
- Adherents: American Academy of Pediatrics (institution), whose July 2025 policy statement holds that “policies that make immunization a uniform condition of school attendance and permit only medical exemptions are an equitable choice and strike the optimal balance between parental authority and public safety,” and calls for ending nonmedical exemptions — https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/156/2/e2025072714/202656/Medical-vs-Nonmedical-Immunization-Exemptions-for (summary with quotes: https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/childhood-vaccines/aap-calls-end-nonmedical-vaccine-exemptions-school-attendance) ; American Medical Association (institution), which in June 2019 adopted policy to advocate for legislation that leads states “to eliminate non-medical exemptions from mandated pediatric immunizations” — https://www.ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/ama-policy-advocates-eliminate-non-medical-vaccine-exemptions . Related: the legislatures of California (2015), New York and Maine (2019) and Connecticut (2021), which repealed nonmedical exemptions; New York’s attorney general, who defended the repeal in Miller v. McDonald.
- Variation inside the camp: many also support requiring vaccination of health care workers against influenza and other diseases that patients can catch from staff. Some would keep a narrow religious exemption with strict procedures (counseling, annual renewal) rather than repeal it outright, as a step toward elimination. Some also want medical exemptions reviewed by the state rather than granted by any physician, as California did in 2019 after medical exemptions surged. Views on COVID-19 workplace mandates divide the camp more than school requirements do.
- The case: Children cannot choose their classmates, and some cannot be vaccinated at all because they are too young or have cancer or immune disorders. They depend on everyone around them being vaccinated. A school requirement is the most effective, least intrusive way to protect them: it asks every family to do the same thing, it reaches families who are not opposed but simply behind, and it makes vaccination the default. Nonmedical exemptions turn that shared protection into a private preference, and because exemptions cluster in particular schools and communities, they create exactly the pockets where measles returns. Medical exemptions protect the child for whom a vaccine would be dangerous; religious and personal exemptions protect no one’s health. No one is forced to be vaccinated: a family that objects can educate at home. But a family that uses the public school, or any school, takes on the obligation not to endanger other people’s children.
- Best evidence: California’s kindergarten coverage with all required vaccines rose from 90.4 to 95.6 percent in two years after it ended personal-belief exemptions. In 2025–26 the four medical-only states and West Virginia had MMR coverage of 95.7 to 98.9 percent, against 92.4 nationally and 75.2 in Idaho. Across Europe, mandates were associated with 3.71 points higher measles coverage and, only where no nonmedical exemptions were allowed, with much lower measles incidence. Italy’s 2017 mandate lifted measles coverage from 87.3 to 94.1 percent. The United States recorded 3,659 measles cases in 2026 by late September, the most since 1991, about 89 percent among people unvaccinated or of unknown status.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from conscience advocates: forcing parents to choose between their faith and their child’s schooling violates religious liberty): Religious liberty has never included the right to endanger others; as the Supreme Court said in Prince v. Massachusetts (1944), it “does not include liberty to expose the community or the child to communicable disease.” Few religious traditions forbid vaccination, and major faiths endorse it. The requirement applies to everyone equally and targets no belief, and families keep the option of home education. Courts continue to agree: the Second and Fourth Circuits upheld requirements without religious exemptions in 2026, and the Supreme Court declined to block New York’s law in September 2026.

### 2. Keep school requirements while letting families opt out for religion or conscience
- Adherents: the White House (institution), whose Executive Order 14420 of 10 August 2026 declares it US policy that “Federal programs and funding should support maximal parental choice over childhood vaccines,” directs the Attorney General to support lawsuits against states that fail to provide religious and medical exemptions, and advises states to narrow their requirements rather than abolish them — https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/eo-14420.pdf ; West Virginia Governor Patrick Morrisey, who ordered a religious exemption process in January 2025 and told the state Supreme Court in May 2026 that “Parents should not be forced to choose between their sincere religious beliefs and their child’s right to an education,” adding that “This case is not about whether vaccines are effective” — https://governor.wv.gov/article/governor-morrisey-files-amicus-brief-defending-religious-liberty-parental-rights-vaccine . Related: Alabama and 19 other states, which filed in support of the Amish plaintiffs in Miller v. McDonald (https://ww3.ca2.uscourts.gov/decisions/OPN/24-681_2_opn.pdf); the HHS Office for Civil Rights, which told West Virginia in August 2025 that it must honor religious exemptions (https://wvmetronews.com/2025/08/22/federal-health-agency-says-west-virginia-should-honor-religious-exemptions-to-vaccination/); the Florida Senate, which in 2026 passed a bill adding a conscience exemption that died in the House.
- Variation inside the camp: some would allow only religious exemptions, as 45 states and DC do; others also allow personal or philosophical ones, as 16 states do. Some want exemptions easy to obtain (a downloadable form); others accept procedural steps such as an education session, as Mississippi requires, so long as the exemption is available in the end. The federal administration also wants the list of required vaccines shortened; others in the camp accept the current list.
- The case: Vaccination requirements for school are a reasonable public health rule, and most families follow them. But a free society makes room for the minority whose religious or moral convictions forbid a medical act, as it does for conscientious objectors to military service. For decades almost every state paired its requirements with a religious exemption and still achieved high coverage; the exemption did not cause the problem. Denying it forces devout families out of schools they pay for and into court, turns a health measure into a religious test, and feeds the distrust that drives hesitancy far beyond the few who object. A state that exempts children for medical reasons but not religious ones is choosing which reasons count, and the Constitution does not let it treat faith as the lesser reason.
- Best evidence: Nearly every state kept religious exemptions through decades of high coverage: national kindergarten MMR coverage was 94.7 to 95.2 percent in the four school years before the pandemic (2016–17 to 2019–20), when all but a handful of states allowed nonmedical exemptions. Mississippi added a court-ordered religious exemption in 2023 and still had 97.0 percent MMR coverage in 2025–26. Public support for religious opt-outs rose from 20 to 39 percent between 2019 and 2025, and among parents, 81 percent back school requirements “with some medical or religious exceptions.” The Supreme Court in December 2025 ordered the Second Circuit to reconsider New York’s repeal in light of Mahmoud v. Taylor.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from requirement supporters: exemptions cluster and let measles back into schools): The rise in exemptions since 2020 reflects a collapse of trust after COVID-19, not the existence of religious exemptions, which were available all along. Abolishing exemptions punishes sincere believers for other people’s loss of confidence and pushes families out of schools rather than into vaccination, as California’s rise in home schooling and independent study shows. Outbreaks can be handled directly by excluding unvaccinated children during an outbreak, which states with exemptions already do.

### 3. End vaccine mandates and leave the choice to individuals and parents
- Adherents: the Idaho Legislature (institution), whose Medical Freedom Act (2025) bars businesses and state and local governments from requiring any “medical intervention,” including vaccination, for employment, services or entry, with an exception for employers funded by Medicare or Medicaid, and says schools shall not mandate one “subject to” the state’s parental rights and school health laws — https://legislature.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/sessioninfo/2025/legislation/S1210E2.pdf ; Florida Surgeon General Joseph Ladapo, who announced in September 2025 that the state would work to end all vaccine mandates, saying “Every last one of them is wrong and drips with disdain and slavery” and “Who am I, as a government or anyone else… to tell you what you should put in your body?” — https://kffhealthnews.org/health-industry/ending-vaccine-mandates-schools-florida-joseph-ladapo-measles/ . Related: the Medical Freedom Act Coalition of at least 15 nonprofits, including Children’s Health Defense, led by Leslie Manookian of the Health Freedom Defense Fund, which is seeking bills in every state to repeal school and day care requirements (https://gvwire.com/2026/02/13/kennedy-allies-target-states-to-overturn-vaccine-mandates-for-schoolchildren/); Stand for Health Freedom.
- Variation inside the camp: some argue from liberty alone: even a safe and effective vaccine should not be compelled. Others, including Children’s Health Defense, also argue that the childhood schedule is inadequately tested and that vaccine injuries are underreported, so compulsion adds an unfair risk. Some would end mandates in one step; others accept broad exemptions as an interim goal. Nearly all oppose standing emergency powers to require vaccination, and several states in this camp have banned vaccine passports and employer COVID-19 mandates by law.
- The case: A person’s body is the first thing they own, and a parent is the one who answers for a child’s health. The government may inform, recommend and make vaccines freely available, but it should not force a medical intervention on anyone as the price of education, a job or a seat at a restaurant. Mandates also corrode what they claim to protect. When the state compels, every adverse event becomes the state’s doing, honest questions are treated as disloyalty, and trust in medicine falls. The COVID-19 years showed how far officials will go once compulsion is accepted: people lost jobs, service members were discharged, and children were kept from school over a vaccine that did little to stop transmission and posed little risk to them. More than half of European countries, including Sweden, Denmark and Norway, do not mandate any childhood vaccine. Parents who vaccinate their children are protected by the vaccine; those who do not are exercising a right the state should respect.
- Best evidence: 17 of 30 EU/EEA countries have no mandatory childhood vaccines. Ioannidis and colleagues attribute 0.01 percent of lives saved by COVID-19 vaccines to children and adolescents. More than 8,000 US service members were discharged under the military’s COVID-19 mandate, which Congress later ordered repealed. The federal Vaccine Injury Compensation Program has paid $4.89 billion on 12,409 awards since 1989. Gallup’s measure of how many Americans call childhood vaccination extremely important fell more between 2019 and 2024 (58 to 40 percent) than in the previous 18 years (64 to 58).
- Answer to the strongest objection (from requirement supporters: unvaccinated children endanger others, including those who cannot be vaccinated, and measles is surging): Vaccinated children are protected by their own vaccination, and the small number who cannot be vaccinated deserve targeted protection, not a regime of compulsion over everyone else. Outbreaks spread in close-knit communities whose objections no mandate has overcome, and compulsion elsewhere does little to change that. Persuasion by trusted doctors, not coercion, is what moves hesitant parents, and a free-choice regime can still offer vaccines everywhere at no cost.

### 4. Put pandemic emergency orders under legislative time limits
- Adherents: American Legislative Exchange Council (institution), whose Emergency Power Limitation Act, adopted as model policy on 8 January 2021, provides that statewide emergency orders infringing constitutional rights expire after a short period unless the legislature ratifies them, must be “narrowly tailored to serve a compelling public health or safety purpose,” and get expedited judicial review — https://alec.org/model-policy/emergency-power-limitation-act/ ; Pacific Legal Foundation (institution), which argues that “Unlimited, dictatorial rule cannot be allowed to continue indefinitely” and proposes that “Emergency orders will expire after a short period of time unless the state legislature extends them” — https://pacificlegal.org/separation-of-powers/emergency-powers/ . Related: Pennsylvania’s legislature and voters, who in May 2021 amended the state constitution to cap emergency declarations at 21 days unless the legislature extends them (https://ballotpedia.org/Pennsylvania_Question_2,_Emergency_Declarations_Amendment_(May_2021)).
- Variation inside the camp: some would only add time limits and legislative ratification, leaving officials free to act in the first weeks. Others would also ban specific measures in advance, such as vaccine passports, employer vaccine mandates, church closures or local mask orders. Some states have also barred state officials from enforcing any WHO directive, which the WHO Pandemic Agreement does not in any case authorize.
- The case: Emergency powers exist so that government can act in the days before the legislature can meet. They were never meant to let one official govern a state by decree for months or years, which is what happened in many states during COVID-19: governors and health officers issued and reissued orders that closed businesses and churches, set rules for weddings and funerals, and required vaccination, without any vote by elected representatives. Laws affecting millions of lives for that long need democratic consent, both because that is how self-government works and because orders that the public has had a voice in are more likely to be followed. Automatic expiration keeps the first response fast and puts the burden on officials to persuade legislators, and through them the public, that an order is still needed.
- Best evidence: Many COVID-19 orders ran for long periods without a legislative vote until courts or legislatures intervened. The Supreme Court found that a federal agency had exceeded its power when it ordered 84 million workers to be vaccinated or tested weekly. Pennsylvania voters approved limits on emergency declarations in 2021. Public support for school vaccine requirements fell after COVID-19 (from 71 to 52 percent in Annenberg’s surveys, 2019 to 2025), consistent with the view that unilateral measures spent public trust.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from public health lawyers: legislative limits will leave the country unable to respond quickly in the next pandemic): Time limits do not stop fast action; they stop indefinite action. A governor can still act on day one, and a legislature that agrees can extend the order in a single vote. If an order cannot win a majority of elected legislators after several weeks, that is a sign it has lost the public consent it needs to work.

### 5. Preserve health officials’ power to act fast in the next pandemic
- Adherents: Temple University’s Center for Public Health Law Research (institution), whose researchers found state laws “changing in ways that threaten to reduce response capacity” and warned that “when the next outbreak hits, new laws limiting health authority will make such action even more difficult” — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36791354/ ; Michelle Mello of Stanford University, whose 2024 BMJ analysis with CPHLR colleagues concluded that “Political forces have pushed reform efforts strongly in one direction—weakening public health legal powers—with potentially dangerous consequences” — https://healthpolicy.fsi.stanford.edu/news/states-adopt-dangerous-legal-reforms-undercutting-public-health-emergency-powers . Related: the Network for Public Health Law, which tracks and opposes bills limiting public health authority (https://www.networkforphl.org/resources/50-state-survey-summary-of-bills-introduced-to-limit-public-health-authority/); the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials.
- Variation inside the camp: many, Mello among them, accept added accountability, such as public reporting of the evidence behind orders, periodic review and judicial oversight, but oppose automatic expiration and advance bans on whole categories of measures. Some would go further and modernize public health laws to give officials clearer authority for vaccination requirements in health care and schools during an emergency.
- The case: In an epidemic, the cost of delay compounds daily, because each infection seeds more. The first weeks decide how many people die, and the tools that matter most, from quarantine to vaccination requirements for health care workers, have to be available immediately, in the hands of officials who can read the evidence as it arrives. Legislatures are not built for that: they are part-time in many states, they are more polarized than health departments, and they cannot track a moving epidemic. Courts already check officials who go too far. The laws passed since 2021 were written in anger at COVID-19 measures, but they will apply to the next pandemic, which may be far deadlier to children or young adults, and they may leave officials forbidden from using measures that would then be clearly justified.
- Best evidence: State legislatures enacted 65 laws contracting emergency health authority and only 17 expanding it between January 2021 and May 2022, out of 1,531 bills introduced. Mello and colleagues found the reforms moved almost entirely in one direction. COVID-19 vaccines are estimated to have saved between about 2.5 million lives over four years and 14.4 million in their first year, and the speed of rollout and uptake shaped how many. Certificates and mandates measurably increased vaccination where uptake had lagged.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from legislative-limit advocates: emergency powers let officials rule by decree for months): The answer to overlong orders is procedural accountability, such as transparency, sunset review with a real opportunity to renew, and judicial review, not stripping powers in advance or letting them lapse automatically while a legislature is out of session. Many of the new laws go beyond time limits and ban measures outright, whatever the next pathogen turns out to be.

### 6. Raise vaccination through trust and access rather than compulsion
- Adherents: Nuffield Council on Bioethics (institution), whose report Public Health: Ethical Issues concluded that “there is not sufficient justification in the UK for moving beyond the current voluntary system and implementing incentivised or quasi-mandatory policies for routine childhood vaccinations,” while identifying circumstances, such as highly contagious and serious diseases, where compulsion is more likely to be justified — https://cdn.nuffieldbioethics.org/wp-content/uploads/Public-health-ethical-issues.pdf ; Kevin Bardosh and eight co-authors (BMJ Global Health, 2022), who argued that “Mandating vaccination is one of the most powerful interventions in public health and should be used sparingly and carefully to uphold ethical norms and trust in institutions” and recommended “empowering strategies based on trust and public consultation” — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35618306/ . Related: Heidi Larson, founder of the Vaccine Confidence Project at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, whose work emphasizes building trust through local ownership and trusted community messengers (https://undark.org/2021/05/21/heidi-larson-interview/); she has not, in the sources checked here, taken a position against specific mandates.
- Variation inside the camp: some accept existing school requirements but oppose tightening them, and would invest instead in access and communication; others favor the European voluntary model outright. Both Nuffield and Bardosh’s group accept mandates in narrow cases, Nuffield for highly contagious and serious diseases and Bardosh’s group as an intervention to be used “sparingly and carefully,” which distinguishes them from those who oppose mandates in principle. Some favor incentives such as payments or tax credits; others worry incentives also undermine voluntariness.
- The case: The question is not whether the state may compel vaccination but whether compulsion is the best way to get people vaccinated, and usually it is not. Coverage depends on trust: in the vaccine, in the doctor recommending it and in the institutions behind them. Coercion can raise uptake in the short run among the persuadable, but it hardens opposition among the resistant, turns a health decision into a political identity, and spends trust that future campaigns will need. Norway, Denmark, Finland and Sweden reach first-dose measles coverage of 93 to 96 percent without any mandate, at or above the US level of 92, through universal free access, reminder systems, and clinicians who have time to talk with parents. Where coverage falls, the causes are usually missed appointments, access barriers and lost confidence, and those have their own remedies. The money and political capital spent fighting over exemptions would do more good spent on the relationships that make people want to vaccinate.
- Best evidence: 17 of 30 EU/EEA countries require no childhood vaccines. The fall in stated confidence after COVID-19 mandates was steep and partisan: the share of Republicans calling childhood vaccination extremely important halved between 2019 and 2024, and support for school requirements fell from 71 to 52 percent between 2019 and 2025. Mills and Rüttenauer found COVID-19 certificates raised uptake only where it was below average and had no effect in Germany, where it was already average. In most of the world the barrier is access: 13.5 million children received no vaccine at all in 2025.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from requirement supporters: voluntary systems work only where trust is already high, and the United States is not such a place): That is exactly why trust has to be rebuilt rather than bypassed. A mandate imposed on a divided public invites the backlash now visible in state legislatures, which are moving to weaken or repeal requirements. Keeping existing requirements while avoiding new compulsion, and investing in the doctor-patient relationship, protects coverage without feeding the movement against it.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others is this: when one person’s decision not to be vaccinated raises the risk to other people, how much added risk to others justifies overriding that person’s, or that parent’s, own judgment, and does overriding it actually leave the community better protected over time?

The part evidence can settle is the second half. How much do strict requirements raise coverage once families’ workarounds (medical exemptions, home schooling, virtual schools) are counted? How much does compulsion depress trust and future voluntary vaccination, and for how long? How much does a few points of coverage change the size and frequency of outbreaks in real schools, where exemptions cluster? The United States is now running the experiment: the gap in kindergarten exemptions runs from 0.1 percent in California, New York and West Virginia to 17.5 percent in Idaho, states are moving in opposite directions, the federal government is pressing states to widen exemptions, and measles is at its highest level since 1991. Over the next several years, outbreak and coverage data from states that loosen and states that tighten will show much of what each side predicts. The same holds for emergency powers: the next serious outbreak will test whether states that limited their officials’ authority respond more slowly, and whether the limits cost lives or preserved compliance.

The part no data can close is the first half. Even with perfect evidence, people will weigh differently a small increase in risk to a classmate who cannot be vaccinated against a family’s religious conscience or a person’s control over their own body. Some hold that protecting others from a contagious disease is a basic condition of sharing schools and workplaces, and that the state may set that condition. Others hold that a medical intervention can never be the price of an education or a livelihood, whatever the risk to others. In between are those who accept requirements but want room for conscience, or who accept compulsion only for the most contagious and dangerous diseases. The same division runs through emergency powers: how much speed a society should buy at the cost of democratic consent is a choice about who should decide, not a fact to be discovered.
