# Climate Adaptation — research

Slug: climate-adaptation
Scope: Adapting to warming already under way: flood defenses, insurance, disaster aid and managed retreat from high-risk places. Wildfire is its own topic.
Primary category: climate-energy (Climate & Energy); also economy, governance
Researched: 2026-11-26

## Overview

Climate adaptation is the work of living with the warming that has already happened and the further warming that is now unavoidable, as distinct from mitigation, which tries to limit how much more warming there will be. This topic covers the four instruments at the center of the public fight over adaptation: physical protection (levees, sea walls, storm-surge barriers, drainage, elevated buildings, early-warning systems and building codes), insurance against weather losses (public flood insurance, private homeowners cover, insurers of last resort and public reinsurance pools), disaster aid (who pays for response and rebuilding, from national emergency agencies to international adaptation finance and the new loss and damage fund), and managed retreat (buyouts, planned relocation and rules against rebuilding in the riskiest places). The question running through all four is who bears the cost of climate risk and whether public help reduces that risk or keeps people in its way. It deliberately excludes wildfire, which has its own topic (wildfires) and its own fuels-and-hardening debate; the pace of emission cuts (net-zero-pace); solar geoengineering (geoengineering); and the physical-science question of abrupt thresholds (climate-tipping-points). Heat, drought and farm adaptation are real parts of adaptation but appear here only where they touch these four instruments.

The why-now is a fortnight of decisions on both the international and the American side of the question. On 2026-11-22 the UN climate conference in Antalya, Türkiye (COP31, opened 2026-11-09 under a Turkish presidency with Australia presiding over negotiations) closed nearly 50 hours late without agreement on its “Antalya Pact” cover decision, which the EU, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Belize, Norway and Vanuatu rejected; an attempt to put a number on the COP30 pledge to triple adaptation finance by 2035 (at least $120 billion a year against a $40 billion 2025 baseline in draft texts) failed to make it into any final text, while parties did agree that the Adaptation Fund may draw a 5 percent share of proceeds from the Paris Agreement’s new carbon-crediting mechanism (Carbon Brief, https://www.carbonbrief.org/cop31-key-outcomes-agreed-at-the-un-climate-talks-in-antalya). Two weeks earlier, on 2026-11-04, UNEP’s Adaptation Gap Report 2026, titled “Ready? Not”, put developing countries’ adaptation needs at about $305 billion a year by 2035 against $28.2-31.7 billion of international public adaptation finance delivered in 2024 (UNEP, https://www.unep.org/en/news-and-stories/press-release/world-heads-overshoot-15degc-adaptation-too-slow; text via https://www.eco-business.com/press-releases/as-world-heads-for-overshoot-of-15c-adaptation-is-too-slow/). In the United States, the National Flood Insurance Program’s authority to write and renew policies expires at midnight on 2026-12-11 unless Congress extends it again (Congressional Research Service, https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-11_IN10835_1177d2de1e2916fd50e80100ff618a10dbb6bb04.html), while Congress weighs the FEMA Review Council’s final report of 2026-05-07, which proposes shifting most disaster response to states, raising the damage threshold for federal declarations and moving most flood policies to private insurers (CBS News/AP, https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fema-review-council-federal-disaster-support-redesign/).

The debate is live because losses are large and rising in absolute terms while the institutions that absorb them are under strain at every level: Munich Re counted about $224 billion of natural-disaster losses worldwide in 2025, about half of them uninsured (https://www.munichre.com/en/company/media-relations/media-information-and-corporate-news/media-information/2026/natural-disaster-figures-2025.html), the US flood program owes $22.525 billion to the Treasury (Congressional Research Service, 2026-10-05), and the new international loss and damage fund received $2.8 billion of requests against a first envelope of $342 million (UNEP, 2026-11-04). Almost no one in the debate opposes adaptation as such. The disagreement is over who pays and what public money should buy. Development agencies, the UN system and most emergency managers want much more spent on protection before disasters strike. Economists and fiscal conservatives in the SmarterSafer tradition want insurance priced to risk so that it steers people and building away from danger, with buyouts for those who want to leave. Coastal and river-state lawmakers, realtors, homebuilders and policyholder groups want cover kept affordable so that communities are not priced out of their homes. Small island and least developed countries, and US states with climate superfund laws, want the fossil fuel producers and high-emitting countries that caused the warming to pay. The current US administration wants states to carry most disasters themselves. And a smaller group around Bjorn Lomborg and the Breakthrough Institute argues that adaptation and economic growth deliver more protection per dollar than rapid emission cuts.

## Questions

Question: Who should carry the cost of climate risk: the people exposed to it, taxpayers at large, or those who caused the warming?
Tag: value
Poles: Those at risk pay / The public shares it (with a third answer, emitters pay, pressing on both)
Why contested: Every adaptation instrument is a decision about who pays. A premium charged to a coastal homeowner, a federal disaster grant funded by all taxpayers, and a fee levied on fossil fuel producers are three different answers to the same question, and each has a moral claim and a practical one behind it. The choice is not settled by any fact about the climate, because the facts are compatible with all three.
Case for “those at risk pay”: People who choose where to live and build know their own circumstances best, and they will weigh risk properly only if they bear its cost. When a household in a floodplain pays the expected cost of flooding, it has a reason to elevate, floodproof, move, or not build there in the first place; when the cost is spread across everyone, the riskiest choices are subsidized by people who made safer ones, often people with less money. On this view the fairest and most effective arrangement is prices that tell the truth about risk, with direct, means-tested help for those who cannot afford them rather than hidden discounts for everyone in harm’s way, including owners of expensive second homes on the coast.
Case for “the public shares it”: Most people in exposed places did not choose a risk that was unknown when they bought, and many cannot sell or move without losing everything they own. Climate risk is a shared hazard created collectively, and insurance against catastrophe works only when the pool is broad; pricing each household by its own risk unravels the pool and leaves the most exposed, often poorer and older, with nothing. On this view disasters are a national problem that national budgets and broad-based insurance should absorb, as with defense or public health. Those who add that emitters should pay argue that neither the household nor the taxpayer caused the warming that changed the risk, and that the companies and countries that profited from emissions should bear the cost of the damage, as polluters do under existing pollution law.

Question: Does public protection and disaster aid make places safer, or keep people in harm’s way?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Help builds resilience / Help invites exposure
Why contested: Geographers since Gilbert White in the 1940s have described a “levee effect,” in which protection encourages building behind the levee until a larger flood causes a larger loss; economists describe the same pattern as moral hazard, in which expected aid weakens the incentive to prepare. Against this, countries that invested in protection and warning have seen deaths fall sharply. Both effects are real, and the dispute is over which dominates and under which policies.
Case for “help builds resilience”: The evidence that protection saves lives and money is strong and repeated. Bangladesh’s cyclone deaths fell from hundreds of thousands in 1970 to 26 in Cyclone Amphan in 2020 after shelters and warning; the National Institute of Building Sciences estimated about $6 saved per $1 of federal mitigation grants and $11 per $1 for modern building codes; WMO finds disaster mortality nearly six times lower where early warning is comprehensive. Communities without protection do not usually empty out; they stay and suffer worse losses. Aid after disasters lets families rebuild rather than fall into poverty, and well-designed aid can require rebuilding higher and stronger.
Case for “help invites exposure”: The pattern of repeated loss is visible in the public record. GAO reports that properties with two or more flood losses have received 48 percent of federal flood insurance claim dollars and that the number of repetitive loss properties grew about 73 percent from 2009 to 2026. The IPCC warns that sea walls reduce harm in the short term but can lock in exposure in the long term unless they are part of a long-term plan. When premiums are discounted and rebuilding is publicly financed, people keep building and rebuilding where losses will recur, and each new defense raises the stakes of the flood it fails to stop.

Question: Should insurance prices reflect each property’s full risk even when that makes cover unaffordable?
Tag: value
Poles: Price the full risk / Cap what households pay
Why contested: The US flood program’s Risk Rating 2.0, phased in since October 2021, ties each premium to an individual property’s modeled risk and is moving many policies toward full-risk rates within statutory caps on annual increases (18 percent a year for most individual properties); members of the congressional Flood Resilience Caucus report an average premium of $1,808 a year, 103.6 percent above legacy rates (Flood Resilience Caucus letter, 2026-08-21, https://troycarter.house.gov/media/press-releases/congressman-carter-leads-bipartisan-flood-resilience-caucus-response-fema). Private homeowners insurance is moving the same way in high-risk states. Price signals and affordability pull in opposite directions, and the question is which yields when they collide.
Case for “price the full risk”: A price that understates risk is a subsidy for building and staying in the most dangerous places, and it is paid by taxpayers and by lower-risk policyholders. Accurate prices tell buyers, lenders and towns what a place really costs, reward owners who elevate or floodproof, and make room for private insurers to compete. Affordability problems are real but should be met with targeted, means-tested vouchers and mitigation grants, not by hiding the risk from everyone; the coalition SmarterSafer says risk-based pricing “must be paired with affordability assistance and mitigation support” (https://www.smartersafer.org/2026/06/16/press-release-smartersafer-submits-comments-supporting-fema-council-recommendations/).
Case for “cap what households pay”: Full-risk prices arrive on people who bought homes under the old prices and cannot recover the lost value; they cut take-up, so that more people go uninsured and fall back on disaster aid or ruin. Insurance exists to pool risk across people and time, and catastrophic risk concentrated in particular places cannot be carried by the households in those places alone. The UK’s Flood Re, which sets the flood part of premiums by council tax band rather than flood risk and plans a transition to risk-reflective pricing by 2039 (Flood Re annual report 2025-26, https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a43d98d93349b85b718d9a0/flood-re-ara-2025-26.pdf), shows that a country can choose affordability for a generation while it adapts.

Question: When should a community retreat rather than defend itself in place?
Tag: value
Poles: Plan retreat early / Defend where people live
Why contested: Some places will become impossible or ruinous to protect as seas rise; the IPCC already reports “soft” limits for some coastal settlements. But where that line falls, who decides, and whether moving people is a policy or an admission of defeat, are questions about community, identity and money as much as engineering. Retreat so far is small in the United States: about 69,000 FEMA-funded buyouts since 1989.
Case for “plan retreat early”: Moving before the disaster is cheaper and kinder than moving after it, and every year of rebuilding in the riskiest places adds to the eventual bill. Planned retreat lets a community move together, with time to choose a new site, keep its institutions and be paid fairly, instead of being scattered by a storm and a slow buyout. Fiji’s national relocation guidelines and trust fund, and the resettlement of Isle de Jean Charles in Louisiana, which PBS NewsHour reported in 2016 had lost 98 percent of its land over the past 60 years (https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/native-community-louisiana-relocates-land-washes-away), show it can be done; the longer governments wait, the more retreat happens chaotically, through price collapse and insurance withdrawal, with the poorest last to leave.
Case for “defend where people live”: People’s homes, livelihoods, cemeteries, churches and kin networks are in those places, and telling them to leave asks them to give up things money cannot replace. Many coastal and river cities are too valuable and too large to abandon and can be protected, as the Netherlands has shown for centuries with dikes and storm-surge barriers; its Delta Programme aims to give everyone behind a primary flood defense a protection level of at least 1 in 100,000 a year by 2050 (https://english.deltaprogramma.nl/three-topics/flood-risk-management/delta-plan). Retreat programs have a history of falling hardest on poorer and more diverse neighborhoods, and planners’ maps of “unprotectable” places often look like maps of who has least political power.

Question: Should disaster response and recovery be led nationally or by states and localities?
Tag: value
Poles: National backstop / States take the lead
Why contested: In the United States many federal declarations now cover modest events: by the FEMA Review Council’s draft estimate, 29 percent of declarations from 2012 to 2025 would not have met its proposed threshold (NPR, 2026-02-17). The council proposes reserving federal help for catastrophic events, paying states directly within 30 days and raising the damage threshold for declarations; critics say states cannot absorb what FEMA now carries. The same tension appears elsewhere as the balance between national and local adaptation budgets.
Case for “national backstop”: Disasters strike unevenly; a state hit by a hurricane in a given year faces costs that no single state budget can plan for, while the national government can pool risk across all states and years. A federal system also brings expertise, surge staff and standards that smaller and rural states cannot maintain on their own. Pulling federal support back, its defenders say, would not save lives or money; it would shift losses onto the poorest states and the people least able to recover, and slow recovery when speed matters most.
Case for “states take the lead”: Disaster response is best run by those closest to it, who know their communities and answer to them. When the federal government pays most of the bill for even modest events, states have little reason to prepare, save or enforce building codes; a higher threshold would return routine events to states and reserve federal money for true catastrophes. The council proposes direct, formula-based payments to states within 30 days of a disaster in place of today’s reimbursement process, which can take years; council member Glenn Youngkin said the recommendations “are all about accelerating federal dollars” (CBS News/AP, https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fema-review-council-federal-disaster-support-redesign/).

Question: Do high-emitting countries and fossil fuel producers owe adaptation and loss and damage finance as an obligation?
Tag: value
Poles: Owed as reparation / Given as assistance
Why contested: Under the UN climate treaties developed countries pledged to provide finance, but they have resisted any language of liability. The International Court of Justice’s advisory opinion of 2025-07-23 said breaches of climate obligations can be wrongful acts carrying a duty of reparation, a reading developing countries embrace and major emitters dispute in its application. In the United States, New York and Vermont passed climate superfund laws in 2024 to charge fossil fuel producers for adaptation costs; federal judges struck down New York’s in August and September 2026, and Vermont’s faces a federal challenge.
Case for “owed as reparation”: The countries and companies whose emissions caused most of the warming grew rich from them, while the countries suffering most contributed least. Paying for the harm is not charity but the ordinary principle that those who cause damage pay for it, as with any other pollution. Voluntary finance has consistently fallen short of what was promised and what is needed, as the gap between the $305 billion UNEP estimates is needed and the $28-32 billion delivered shows; only a sense of obligation will close it.
Case for “given as assistance”: Emissions were produced over two centuries by billions of people, most of them before the harm was understood, and assigning legal liability to particular countries or companies today is both unfair and unworkable. Finance for adaptation is better treated as investment and aid, directed to where it does the most good and backed by voters who are willing to give it, rather than as reparations that donor publics reject and courts may not enforce. In the US the argument adds a constitutional point: two federal courts held that New York’s superfund law intruded on federal authority over interstate and international emissions.

Question: Can adaptation and economic growth substitute for rapid emission cuts?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Adapt and grow / Limits require cuts
Why contested: Adaptation often pays back quickly and locally, while emission cuts pay back slowly and globally, so the comparison tempts those who weigh costs. Whether that makes adaptation a partial substitute depends on how much warming can be adapted to, at what cost, and how fast.
Case for “adapt and grow”: Humanity has adapted to every climate on Earth, and wealth is the best protection: the Netherlands lives below sea level, and disaster death rates have fallen more than 90 percent in a century of warming. Adaptation delivers its benefits now and to the people who pay for it, while net-zero policies are expensive, slow and often not delivered. Bjorn Lomborg argues that development, adaptation and innovation, rather than near-term net-zero targets, would protect the world’s poorest best for the money.
Case for “limits require cuts”: The IPCC finds that adaptation has soft and hard limits that tighten with every increment of warming, that it does not prevent all losses even before those limits, and that sea level is committed to rise for centuries. Between very low and very high emissions the projected rise by 2100 differs by roughly half a meter, the difference between defending and abandoning many coasts. Adaptation also depends on a predictable climate; the faster the change, the less of it can be planned for, and the poorest countries cannot buy their way to Dutch-style defenses in time.

Question: Is the rise in disaster losses driven mainly by a changing climate or by more people and property in harm’s way?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Mostly climate / Mostly exposure
Why contested: Absolute losses have risen, and the US count of billion-dollar disasters went from about three a year in the 1980s to about 20 a year in 2016-2025. But losses depend on hazard, exposure and vulnerability together, and separating them is methodologically hard. The answer matters for policy: if exposure dominates, land use and building codes are the main levers; if climate dominates, losses will keep rising whatever local policy does.
Case for “mostly climate”: Attribution science now links many specific events, such as heavier hurricane rainfall and more intense heat waves, to warming, and the IPCC attributes the acceleration of sea level rise since at least 1971 mainly to human influence. Sea level has risen about 20 centimeters since 1901 and the rate has roughly tripled, raising every storm surge. Climate Central counts 23 billion-dollar US disasters in 2025 with the threshold already adjusted for inflation.
Case for “mostly exposure”: More people and more expensive property sit in coastal and floodplain zones than ever, and that growth alone raises losses even in a constant climate. Roger Pielke Jr. argues that weather-related catastrophe losses as a share of global GDP have not risen since 1990 and were about 0.18 percent in 2025, below the long-term average of 0.22 percent. On this reading, the most effective way to cut losses is to change where and how people build.

Question: Does public pre-disaster spending pay for itself?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Pays back many times / Returns are overstated
Why contested: Benefit-cost estimates for mitigation are high, but they come from samples of funded projects, depend on assumptions about future disasters and the value of avoided deaths, and are produced in part by the institutions that advocate the spending. The current US administration cancelled the main federal pre-disaster grant program in 2025, calling it “wasteful and ineffective,” until a court ordered it restored.
Case for “pays back many times”: Study after study finds high returns: about $6 per $1 for federal mitigation grants and $11 per $1 for modern building codes in National Institute of Building Sciences estimates, and 2:1 to 10:1 across the investments the Global Commission on Adaptation reviewed. Losses avoided are real money that would otherwise come from disaster budgets, insurers and households, and the cheapest measures, such as warnings and codes, have the highest returns.
Case for “returns are overstated”: Averages from selected projects do not mean the next dollar earns the same; well-chosen projects are funded first, and grant programs can drift toward politically favored projects with weaker returns. Many estimates count benefits such as avoided deaths at assumed values and assume disasters will strike where the projects are. FEMA said in 2025 that BRIC had become too bureaucratic and focused on “climate change” initiatives (PBS NewsHour/AP), and competitive grant programs tend to reward places with the staff to apply: Mach and colleagues found that wealthier, more populous counties were more likely to run FEMA buyouts. On this view the realized return is lower than the headline ratios, and the money is better left to states and localities that bear the consequences.

## Positions

Camp: Invest in protection before disasters strike
Adherents:
  Global Commission on Adaptation (institution; led by Ban Ki-moon, Bill Gates and Kristalina Georgieva, managed by the Global Center on Adaptation and the World Resources Institute). Its 2019 report Adapt Now estimated $7.1 trillion in net benefits from $1.8 trillion of adaptation investment; Ban Ki-moon: “Adaptation is not only the right thing to do, it is also the smart thing to do.” https://www.wri.org/news/2019/09/global-leaders-call-urgent-action-climate-adaptation-commission-finds-adaptation-can
  Washington State Attorney General Nick Brown and a coalition of 22 states and the District of Columbia (institution), which sued and won an order restoring FEMA’s BRIC pre-disaster grant program; Brown said its cancellation left communities “more vulnerable because they have been unable to fortify against disasters.” https://www.atg.wa.gov/news/news-releases/washington-and-multistate-coalition-secure-victory-preventing-billions-cuts
  Also in this camp: UNEP’s Executive Director Inger Andersen, who said after the 2026 Adaptation Gap Report that nations “must focus on intensifying efforts to achieve the COP30 Global Mutirão aspiration of tripling adaptation finance” (https://www.eco-business.com/press-releases/as-world-heads-for-overshoot-of-15c-adaptation-is-too-slow/); the National Institute of Building Sciences.
Case in its own voice: Warming is already here, and the cheapest disaster is the one that never happens. Every serious study of protection finds it pays: warnings, shelters and evacuation took Bangladesh from hundreds of thousands of cyclone deaths to dozens; modern building codes return about $11 for every $1; federal mitigation grants about $6. Yet the world spends a small fraction of what is needed, and governments still pour money into rebuilding after disasters while starving the programs that would have prevented them. The answer is to treat adaptation as core infrastructure, funded steadily before the storm: levees and drainage designed for the climate to come, codes that every jurisdiction adopts, early warnings for every person on Earth, and international finance that matches developing countries’ needs instead of a tenth of them. Adaptation is not a substitute for cutting emissions, and it is not a surrender; it is how people stay safe while the world does both.
Best evidence: The long fall in disaster death rates, more than 90 percent per person over a century (Our World in Data); WMO’s finding that disaster mortality is nearly six times lower where early warning is comprehensive; the National Institute of Building Sciences’ benefit-cost ratios; the Global Commission on Adaptation’s 2:1 to 10:1 returns; UNEP’s estimate that developing countries need about $305 billion a year against $28-32 billion delivered.
Variation inside the camp: Development institutions emphasize international finance and early warning in poor countries; US advocates emphasize pre-disaster grants and building codes; engineers favor hard defenses while many ecologists favor wetlands, mangroves and room for rivers, which the IPCC treats as less prone to lock-in.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, pressed by those who favor risk pricing and retreat, is that protection invites more building in harm’s way and locks in exposure. The camp answers that the remedy is better design, not less protection: pair defenses with land-use rules, require rebuilding to higher standards, and spend first on measures such as warnings and codes that cannot encourage risky building because they make every building safer. To those who say the federal role should shrink, it answers that pre-disaster money is exactly the spending that reduces the later federal bill, and that the 2025 cancellation of BRIC was ruled unlawful.

Camp: Keep insurance and recovery affordable where people live
Adherents:
  The congressional Flood Resilience Caucus, led by Rep. Troy Carter (D-La.) with co-chairs Reps. Mike Ezell (R-Miss.), Lizzie Fletcher (D-Tex.) and Randy Weber (R-Tex.) (institution), whose August 2026 letter on the FEMA Review Council report called for a flood insurance program that “keeps coverage affordable, narrows the insurance gap, and rewards mitigation” and opposed moving the lowest-risk policies to private insurers. https://troycarter.house.gov/media/press-releases/congressman-carter-leads-bipartisan-flood-resilience-caucus-response-fema
  National Association of Realtors and National Association of Home Builders (institutions), whose June 2026 comments on the FEMA Review Council report raised concerns about NFIP privatization and reduced federal disaster aid and called for a long-term reauthorization. https://www.nar.realtor/news/washington-report/nar-and-nahb-submit-comments-on-fema-review-council-report
  Also in this camp: the National League of Cities, which warns that moving flood insurance to the private market could create affordability and availability problems (https://www.nlc.org/article/2026/05/14/fema-review-council-final-report-signals-major-shift-in-federal-disaster-policy/); the National Low Income Housing Coalition on disaster housing aid; the UK government and insurers that created Flood Re.
Case in its own voice: People live in river towns and on coasts because that is where their families, jobs and communities have been for generations, and they bought their homes under the rules and prices that existed then. Abruptly repricing every policy to a model’s estimate of full risk does not move anyone to safety; it strips equity from working families, pushes them to drop coverage, and leaves them dependent on disaster aid or ruined when the water comes. Insurance is supposed to pool risk, and a national flood program exists precisely because catastrophic risk concentrated in particular places cannot be carried by those places alone. Keep premiums within reach, give long-term certainty instead of repeated short-term extensions and lapses, invest in mitigation that lowers the risk itself, and treat recovery as a national commitment rather than a bill sent to whoever was unlucky.
Best evidence: The caucus’s figure that the average NFIP premium is now $1,808 a year, 103.6 percent above legacy rates; low take-up of flood insurance outside the mortgage requirement (CRS cites a 37 percent take-up rate for New York City floodplain properties without mortgages); the Treasury’s finding that the highest-risk fifth of ZIP codes pay 82 percent more for homeowners cover and face about 80 percent higher nonrenewal rates; the NFIP lapse from 2025-10-01 through 2025-11-12, when no policies could be sold or renewed (CRS, https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-11_IN10835_1177d2de1e2916fd50e80100ff618a10dbb6bb04.html); Flood Re’s demonstration that a country can cap flood premiums by property tax band while it plans a transition to risk-reflective prices by 2039.
Variation inside the camp: Some want to keep the NFIP public and add affordability discounts; some want a federal reinsurance backstop for all catastrophic perils; some, including many coastal lawmakers, combine affordability with large public spending on defenses, which overlaps with the first camp.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that subsidized premiums keep people and building in the most dangerous places and send the bill to taxpayers and lower-risk policyholders. The camp answers that affordability and mitigation are not opposites: caps can be tied to mitigation, so that the help goes to owners who elevate or floodproof, and new construction can be priced and regulated differently from homes people already own. It adds that the alternative is not people moving to safety but people staying uninsured, with the loss falling on disaster aid anyway.

Camp: Price climate risk honestly and help people move out of harm’s way
Adherents:
  SmarterSafer coalition (institution), which in June 2026 broadly supported the FEMA Review Council’s call for risk-based NFIP pricing and said risk-based pricing “must be paired with affordability assistance and mitigation support to protect vulnerable households.” https://www.smartersafer.org/2026/06/16/press-release-smartersafer-submits-comments-supporting-fema-council-recommendations/
  Natural Resources Defense Council (institution), whose Rob Moore wrote that some of the most flood-prone properties have been “repeatedly rebuilt” when it “would be less expensive to help homeowners move to higher ground,” and proposed a guaranteed buyout through the flood insurance policy for owners who no longer want to rebuild. https://www.nrdc.org/bio/rob-moore/seeking-higher-ground-climate-smart-solutions-flooding
  Also in this camp: R Street Institute, which lists ending “environmentally destructive taxpayer subsidies through the National Flood Insurance Program” among its priorities (https://www.rstreet.org/issues/flood-insurance/); the FEMA Review Council on flood insurance pricing; managed-retreat researchers such as A.R. Siders and Katharine Mach.
Case in its own voice: The cheapest flood is the one with no house in its path. For decades the United States has charged too little for flood risk, paid to rebuild the same houses again and again, and let new construction go up where the water will certainly return; the flood program’s $22.5 billion debt and the fact that properties with two or more losses have drawn 48 percent of its claim dollars are the result. Honest prices tell buyers, lenders and towns what a place really costs and reward every owner who elevates or moves. Prices alone are not enough, and they should not be cruel: help low-income owners directly with means-tested vouchers and mitigation grants, and offer anyone in the riskiest places a fast, guaranteed buyout so that moving is a choice made in advance rather than a collapse after the storm. Planned retreat, done early and fairly, is cheaper and kinder than the unplanned retreat that insurance withdrawal and repeated disasters will otherwise force.
Best evidence: GAO’s 2026 testimony on repetitive loss properties (about 2.5 percent of policies unmitigated; 227,000 repetitive loss properties, up about 73 percent since 2009); NFIP debt of $22.525 billion and 36 short-term reauthorizations since FY2017; the IPCC’s warning that sea walls can lock in long-term exposure; research finding about 41 million Americans in the 1-in-100-year floodplain, roughly three times FEMA’s mapped figure, which means much of the risk is unpriced and unknown to those who hold it.
Variation inside the camp: Market-oriented groups such as R Street emphasize moving flood risk to private insurers and reinsurers; environmental groups such as NRDC emphasize buyouts, floodplain restoration and risk disclosure at sale; both accept means-tested help for low-income owners.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from the affordability camp, is that full-risk prices fall on families who bought under the old rules and cannot sell. The camp answers that hiding risk does not protect those families; it only delays the reckoning until a disaster or an insurer’s exit wipes out their equity at once. Targeted help can protect lower-income owners without subsidizing every coastal property, and buyouts offered in advance let people leave with their equity rather than after it is gone.

Camp: Make polluters pay for adaptation and loss
Adherents:
  Government of Vanuatu (institution), which led the UN General Assembly request for the International Court of Justice’s 2025 advisory opinion on climate obligations and was among the countries that rejected the COP31 Antalya Pact; its climate minister Ralph Regenvanu called the ICJ ruling “an important milestone in the fight for climate justice.” https://www.carbonbrief.org/icj-what-the-world-courts-landmark-opinion-means-for-climate-change/
  Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.), lead sponsor with Rep. Jerry Nadler and Rep. Judy Chu of the Polluters Pay Climate Fund Act of 2025, which would charge the largest fossil fuel companies for a $1 trillion fund for infrastructure, disaster assistance and other climate costs; its summary states that “those most responsible for creating the climate crisis should pay the most to address its harmful impacts.” https://www.vanhollen.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/polluters_pay_climate_fund_act_two-pager_119.pdf
  Also in this camp: the legislatures of Vermont and New York, which passed climate superfund laws in 2024; the Center for Climate Integrity and the Union of Concerned Scientists; the Alliance of Small Island States and the Least Developed Countries group in UN negotiations; Harj Narulla, counsel for the Solomon Islands, who said the ICJ ruling “provided a legal pathway for developing states to seek climate reparations from developed States.”
Case in its own voice: The people paying for sea walls, relocations and rebuilt homes are not the people who caused the warming. A small number of fossil fuel companies and wealthy countries produced most of the emissions, profited from them, and in many cases knew the consequences; small island states and poor countries produced almost none and now face the largest bills relative to their size. Every other kind of pollution follows a simple rule, that the polluter pays for the cleanup, and the federal Superfund law has applied it to toxic waste for decades. Voluntary aid has never come close to the need: developing countries need about $305 billion a year for adaptation and receive about a tenth of it, and the loss and damage fund had $342 million to answer $2.8 billion of requests. Charging those responsible is fairer than charging taxpayers or the victims, and it is the only source large enough.
Best evidence: The ICJ advisory opinion of 2025-07-23, which found that breaches of climate obligations can be internationally wrongful acts carrying a duty of reparation; UNEP’s adaptation finance gap; the loss and damage fund’s first-round shortfall; Vermont’s and New York’s climate superfund laws as legislative models; New York’s would have collected a fixed $75 billion in adaptation infrastructure costs from fossil fuel producers in proportion to their emissions (Foley Hoag, https://foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/law-and-the-environment/2026/october/second-decision-to-block-new-york-climate-change-superfund-act-issued/).
Variation inside the camp: Internationally the claim is made against high-emitting states through finance obligations and courts; in the United States it is made against fossil fuel producers through state superfund laws, federal bills and lawsuits. Some advocates want liability established in court; others want negotiated levies, such as taxes on fossil fuel extraction, aviation or shipping, earmarked for adaptation.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that historic emissions were made by billions of people under legal activity and that assigning liability now is unfair and unenforceable; in August and September 2026 two federal courts in New York held the state’s superfund law preempted by federal law and permanently enjoined it, while Vermont’s remains in effect pending a challenge. The camp answers that the companies’ share of emissions is measurable, that they continued producing and in some cases misleading the public long after the harm was known, and that the law adapts to new harms, as it did with toxic waste. Losing the first round in court, it argues, is how most accountability laws begin, and appeals and the international route remain open.

Camp: Hand disaster response back to the states
Adherents:
  The FEMA Review Council (institution; appointed by President Trump, co-chaired by the Secretaries of Homeland Security and Defense), whose final report of 2026-05-07, in its members’ words, would make disaster response “locally executed, state managed and federally supported,” and recommends that federal help be reserved for catastrophic events, that states receive direct formula-based payments, and that most flood policies move to the private market; council member Kevin Guthrie, Florida’s emergency management director, told states, “States, figure it out,” and “Do what’s best for you.” https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fema-review-council-federal-disaster-support-redesign/ and https://www.nlc.org/article/2026/05/14/fema-review-council-final-report-signals-major-shift-in-federal-disaster-policy/
  Cato Institute (institution), whose budget analyst Dominik Lett called the council’s proposed declaration formula a “promising idea” and said, “We should be charting a path that shifts financial responsibility back to the states, for disasters.” https://www.npr.org/2026/02/17/nx-s1-5698320/fema-reform-trump-hurricane-earthquake
  Also in this camp: President Trump, who has said he wants responsibility moved back to the states and has threatened to dismantle FEMA; the Heritage Foundation, which has long proposed higher declaration thresholds and a lower federal cost share.
Case in its own voice: Disasters are local, and those closest to them respond best. Over decades Washington has turned into the first payer for events that states once handled themselves, declaring disasters for storms well within a state’s means, and that has weakened the incentive for states and towns to prepare, save and enforce building codes. The federal reimbursement system is slow, paper-heavy and audited for years, so money reaches communities late. Raise the threshold so that routine events return to the states, give states fast, formula-based money they can direct as they see fit, reserve federal help for true catastrophes, and move flood insurance toward a private market that can price risk without a $22 billion federal debt. A leaner federal role is not abandonment; it is a clearer division of responsibility.
Best evidence: The council’s estimate, as reported by NPR, that 29 percent of disasters declared from 2012 to 2025 would not have met its proposed threshold yet accounted for only $1.5 billion, showing how many declarations cover small events; the per capita indicator ($1.97 in FY2026) that the council argues has not kept up with inflation; the NFIP’s debt and repeated lapses; the National Emergency Management Association’s statement that it “broadly supports the overarching principles outlined by the council.”
Variation inside the camp: The council stopped short of abolishing FEMA and moved away from its December draft’s 50 percent staff cut; President Trump has spoken of dismantling the agency; Cato and Heritage focus on cost shares and thresholds; many governors support faster block payments without supporting a smaller federal role.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that states, especially small and rural ones, cannot absorb catastrophic costs or build the surge capacity FEMA now provides. The camp answers that the proposal keeps the federal government for catastrophes and changes only the routine events states can handle, and that states already run response on the ground. Faster direct payments, it argues, would leave states better resourced after a real disaster than today’s years-long reimbursements.

Camp: Put growth and adaptation ahead of rapid net zero
Adherents:
  Bjorn Lomborg, president of the Copenhagen Consensus Center, whose 2024 Hoover Institution paper argues that net-zero within a few decades is “incredibly costly,” that adaptation “often offers immediate and very localized advantages” while emission cuts offer “modest benefits,” and that “lifting countries out of poverty becomes a crucial complementary approach to mitigating the damage caused by climate change.” https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/research/docs/Lomborg_ClimateChange_web_240903.pdf
  Breakthrough Institute (institution), whose writing on adaptation, including the essay Forget Adapting to Climate Change and the report Adaptation for a High-Energy Planet, argues that general economic development and energy access explain the historical success of adaptation and that the goal should be a continual fall in deaths from natural disasters. https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/energy/forget-adapting-to-climate-change (the site blocks automated access; content as indexed by search, not read in full)
  Related view: Bill Gates’s October 2025 memo before COP30 urged the climate fight to shift focus from curbing emissions toward reducing human suffering, while he continues to back clean-energy innovation (PBS NewsHour/AP, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/bill-gates-calls-for-climate-fight-to-shift-focus-from-curbing-emissions-to-reducing-human-suffering).
Case in its own voice: Climate change is a real problem, not the end of the world, and the question is how to do the most good with limited money. Humanity has always adapted, and wealth is the most powerful adaptation there is: disaster death rates have fallen by more than 90 percent per person in a century, the Netherlands lives below sea level, and Bangladesh went from hundreds of thousands of cyclone deaths to dozens as it grew richer. Adaptation pays back immediately and to the people who fund it, while rapid net-zero policies are enormously expensive, fall hardest on the poor, and change temperatures only slowly. The best climate policy for the world’s poorest is growth, cheap energy, early warnings, defenses and innovation that makes clean energy cheaper than fossil fuels, not a race to targets that are not being met.
Best evidence: The long decline in disaster deaths (Our World in Data, Munich Re’s thirty-year average of 41,900 against 17,200 in 2025); Pielke’s finding that weather losses as a share of global GDP have not risen since 1990; the high benefit-cost ratios of adaptation even in mainstream estimates; the IPCC’s finding that vulnerability tracks poverty, with disaster mortality 15 times higher in highly vulnerable regions.
Variation inside the camp: Lomborg favors a modest carbon tax and research spending over regulations and targets; Breakthrough emphasizes energy abundance, including nuclear, as both development and decarbonization; Gates keeps emission cuts as a goal but ranks human welfare first.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that adaptation has limits, that sea level is committed to rise for centuries, and that the poorest cannot adapt fast enough without deep emission cuts. The camp answers that it does not reject emission cuts, only their pace and cost, and that the fastest way to give the poorest the means to adapt is to make them richer; a Bangladesh with Dutch income can build Dutch defenses. It adds that innovation, not rationing, is what will eventually cut emissions in the countries whose emissions are still growing.

## Crux

The question under the others: should the cost of climate risk be left largely where it falls, so that it shapes where people live and build, or be spread across taxpayers, policyholders and those who caused the warming, so that people can stay and be protected where they already live?

Every camp answers it. Those who want risk priced honestly and buyouts offered want the cost to stay visible to the people exposed, because they believe that is what moves building out of harm’s way. Those who want insurance and recovery kept affordable want the cost pooled nationally so that families are not ruined for living where they always have. Those who want polluters to pay want it moved to the emitters. Those who want states to lead want it moved from the federal budget to state and local ones. Those who want protection funded before disasters, and those who put growth and adaptation ahead of rapid net zero, want public money to shrink the cost itself. Settle who should carry the cost, and most of the specific fights, over Risk Rating 2.0, FEMA thresholds, the loss and damage fund and buyouts, become questions of design.

What evidence could settle: how strongly subsidized insurance, disaster aid and public defenses actually increase building and rebuilding in exposed places (the size of the levee effect and of moral hazard, measured rather than assumed); how much protection, codes and warning reduce losses at the margin, beyond the average benefit-cost ratios of projects already chosen; how many households drop coverage, sell or move when premiums rise to full risk, and who they are; how much of the rise in losses comes from exposure rather than from the hazard itself; and where the soft and hard limits to coastal protection will fall under different emission paths. Better flood maps, property-level premium and claims data, and follow-up of bought-out and priced-out households would narrow most of these.

What no data can close: how much weight to give a family’s claim to stay in its home and community against the claim of other taxpayers not to pay for that choice; whether people who bought under old prices are owed protection from new ones; whether the harm of warming is a debt owed by those who caused it or a misfortune the fortunate should help with; and how much of these burdens a nation should share across its states and regions rather than leave to each. Two people who agree on every number can still answer these differently, and that difference is the debate.
