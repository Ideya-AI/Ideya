# GM Crops — research

## Overview

Genetically modified (GM) crops are plants whose DNA has been changed with laboratory techniques rather than by crossing and selection alone. Since the first commercial plantings in 1996 they have spread to roughly 210 million hectares a year, almost all of it four crops: soybeans, maize, cotton and canola, engineered mainly to tolerate herbicides or to make their own insecticidal proteins (from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis, hence “Bt”). This topic covers GM crops as part of the food supply: whether they raise yields and help farmers cope with pests, drought and disease; whether foods made from them are safe to eat and safe for the environment; how far a few seed and chemical companies control the seeds and the rules around them; and whether foods should say on the label that they come from GM crops. It includes the newer gene-edited crops, because the main fight in law right now is over whether they count as GM. It deliberately leaves aside gene drives and de-extinction, which have their own topic, as do the land-sparing argument about farm intensity and the climate effects of livestock; those appear here only where a GM argument depends on them. Questions about glyphosate weedkiller appear only where they are bound up with herbicide-tolerant crops.

Why now: on 17 June 2026 the European Parliament gave final approval to the EU’s regulation on plants made with “new genomic techniques,” ending a three-year legislative fight (Regulation (EU) 2026/1388, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1388). Gene-edited plants whose changes could also arise naturally or through conventional breeding (“category 1”) will be treated like conventional plants, without GMO risk assessment, traceability or consumer labeling; only their seed bags must be labeled, they are barred from organic farming, and herbicide tolerance is excluded from the category; other gene-edited plants stay under the GMO rules (European Parliament, 4 December 2025, https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20251201IPR31710/new-genomic-techniques-deal-to-support-the-green-transition-in-farming). The regulation entered into force on 16 July 2026 and applies from July 2028 (European Commission, https://food.ec.europa.eu/plants/new-genomic-techniques_en). Eight days after the Parliament vote, on 25 June 2026, the US Supreme Court ruled 7–2 in Monsanto Co. v. Durnell that federal pesticide law preempts state failure-to-warn claims seeking a cancer warning on Roundup, the glyphosate herbicide sold alongside herbicide-tolerant GM seed (opinion, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1068_n7ip.pdf). And on 3 August 2026 a federal district court gave the US Department of Agriculture until 1 January 2028 to close gaps in the national “bioengineered” food label after an appeals court found its exemption for highly refined foods unlawful (Hogan Lovells summary, https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/federal-court-sets-january-2028-deadline-for-changes-to-key-elements-of-bioengineered-food-disclosu).

The debate is live because the evidence and the stakes point in different directions for different people. Major scientific bodies have found no substantiated evidence that approved GM foods are riskier to eat than conventional ones, yet in a 2019–2020 Pew survey of 20 publics a median of 48% said GM foods are unsafe. Farm economists report large gains for farmers who grow insect-resistant crops, especially in poorer countries, while critics point to herbicide-resistant weeds, pests evolving around Bt crops and seed prices that have risen far faster than crop prices. Seed companies, farm groups in the big exporting countries and most agricultural scientists want GM and gene-edited crops approved faster and judged by the product rather than the method. Consumer organizations want pre-market review and labels kept for every GM food, old or new. Small-farmer movements, organic farming groups and some governments, Mexico’s most prominently, want GM crops kept out of their fields altogether. A growing set of governments, now including the EU, have taken a middle path that frees gene editing while keeping transgenic crops under GMO rules.

## Questions

Question: Are foods from GM crops as safe to eat as foods from conventional crops?
Tag: empirical
Poles: As safe as conventional / Safety not yet shown
Why contested: Every major review by scientific academies and food-safety regulators has found no substantiated evidence that approved GM foods carry greater health risks, and billions of people and animals have eaten them for three decades. But much of the safety data comes from developers, long-term independent feeding studies are few, and absence of evidence of harm is not the same thing as proof of safety. Majorities in many countries remain unconvinced.
Case for “As safe as conventional”: The US National Academies examined almost 900 publications in 2016 and found no persuasive evidence of adverse health effects directly attributable to eating GM foods; reviews by the European Commission’s research programs, the World Health Organization and food-safety agencies in dozens of countries reached the same conclusion for the products they assessed. Conventional breeding, including the radiation and chemical mutagenesis used to create many familiar varieties, changes far more of a plant’s DNA, unpredictably, and is not tested at all. More than 95% of US food animals have eaten GM feed since the late 1990s without any change in health trends. If a specific GM product has a specific risk, such as a new allergen, it is caught by case-by-case assessment; there is no reason to think the technique itself creates risk.
Case for “Safety not yet shown”: The 300-plus signatories of a 2015 statement in Environmental Sciences Europe argued that the published evidence is too thin and contradictory to support a claim of consensus, and that most studies are short-term, funded by developers or both. Scientific agreement about the products assessed so far says little about the next one, and population-level health effects of a single dietary change are hard to detect without labels to trace who ate what. The precautionary principle, written into EU law, puts the burden of proof on those introducing a new technology, not on the public. Herbicide-tolerant crops also carry residues of the herbicides sprayed on them, and glyphosate itself was classed as probably carcinogenic by the International Agency for Research on Cancer in 2015.

Question: Have GM crops raised yields, or mainly cut losses and costs?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real yield gains / Little yield gain
Why contested: Farm-level studies find large average yield gains, but national yield trends in the United States did not visibly speed up after GM crops arrived. The answer depends on the trait (insect resistance versus herbicide tolerance), the place (heavy versus light pest pressure) and the method (comparing fields versus comparing decades).
Case for “Real yield gains”: The 2014 meta-analysis of 147 studies by Klümper and Qaim found average yield gains of 22%, larger for insect-resistant crops and larger in developing countries, where pest damage is heavier and farmers often cannot afford effective sprays. A randomized trial of Bt eggplant in Bangladesh found 42% higher net yields. Insect-resistant traits protect the yield a plant already has, which is what matters to a farmer who loses a third of a crop to bollworms. Yield is also not the only gain: herbicide-tolerant crops saved labor and made no-till farming easier, and the profits farmers report are why adoption stays above 90% wherever they are allowed to choose. New gene-edited traits aimed directly at yield, such as India’s Kamala rice with its reported 19% higher yield in trials, are only beginning to arrive.
Case for “Little yield gain”: The National Academies found “no evidence that GE crops had changed the rate of increase in yields” of US maize, soybeans and cotton, and the two traits that dominate GM acreage were never designed to raise a crop’s potential yield. In India, Kranthi and Stone found that cotton yield gains tracked fertilizer and irrigation better than Bt adoption, and that yields stagnated once pests adapted. A 2016 New York Times analysis comparing the United States and Canada with Western Europe, which grows almost no GM crops, found no discernible yield advantage for the GM-growing countries in corn and rapeseed [unverified]. Conventional breeding, better agronomy and agroecological methods have delivered most of the yield gains of recent decades, and the money and attention spent on GM traits could have gone to them.

Question: Have GM crops reduced the harm from pesticides, or deepened dependence on them?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Less pesticide harm / More herbicide dependence
Why contested: Insect-resistant crops clearly cut insecticide spraying at first; herbicide-tolerant crops increased glyphosate use many times over. Both sides have real data, and the dispute turns on which effect is larger, whether weight of chemical is the right measure, and whether gains last once weeds and insects adapt.
Case for “Less pesticide harm”: Bt crops let farmers stop spraying broad-spectrum insecticides that kill beneficial insects and poison farmworkers; the National Academies found some evidence of fewer insecticide poisonings, and Bangladesh’s Bt eggplant cut use of the most ecologically harmful pesticides by 82%. Bt maize suppressed the European corn borer so widely that non-GM neighbors gained more than $4.3 billion over 14 years. Glyphosate is less toxic than many herbicides it replaced, so more kilograms can mean less harm. Resistance is a management problem common to every pest control method, and it is solved by stacking traits, rotating modes of action and planting refuges, not by abandoning the tools.
Case for “More herbicide dependence”: Herbicide-tolerant crops, which make up most GM acreage, were designed to sell more of the company’s own herbicide, and global glyphosate use rose almost 15-fold after their launch. Sixty-two weed species have since evolved resistance to glyphosate, pushing farmers to older, more volatile herbicides like dicamba and 2,4-D through a new generation of tolerant crops. Insects have adapted too: documented cases of resistance to Bt proteins rose from 3 in 2005 to 16 in 2016, and in India pink bollworm resistance has left farmers spending more on pesticides than before Bt arrived. A technology that relies on a single chemical or a single protein builds in its own obsolescence.

Question: Should gene-edited crops be regulated like conventional crops or like GMOs?
Tag: value
Poles: Like conventional crops / Like GMOs
Why contested: Gene editing can make small, targeted changes that are often indistinguishable from natural mutations, without leaving foreign DNA. The EU, the UK, India, Argentina, Japan and others now exempt many such edits from GMO rules, while critics call them “new GMOs.” Evidence bears on how different edited plants really are, but the deeper question is whether regulation should follow the product or the process that made it.
Case for “Like conventional crops”: Risk comes from what a plant is, not how it was bred; the National Academies put it plainly in 2016: “It is the product and not the process that should be regulated.” A gene-edited plant whose change could have arisen naturally or through conventional mutagenesis, which has never required safety testing, poses no new kind of risk, and often cannot be told apart from its conventional cousin by any test. Treating it as a GMO would add years and tens of millions of dollars to each variety, which only the largest firms can afford, and would shut out public breeders and small seed companies developing drought-tolerant, disease-resistant or lower-input crops for a changing climate.
Case for “Like GMOs”: Gene editing is a powerful technique of genetic engineering, and its errors, such as off-target edits and unintended changes near the target site, are not the same as those of conventional breeding. Exempting edited plants from risk assessment, traceability and labeling removes the tools needed to detect problems after release and to recall a product if one appears. Without labels, consumers and organic farmers who do not want them cannot avoid them, and the EU’s own Court of Justice ruled in July 2018 that organisms obtained by the new mutagenesis techniques are GMOs under EU law (case C-528/16). Edited traits can also be patented, extending the reach of the companies that already dominate seed markets.

Question: Should foods made from GM crops carry a mandatory label?
Tag: value
Poles: People’s right to know / A label that misleads
Why contested: Large majorities in polls support mandatory labels, and most countries with GM rules require them. Opponents argue that a label based on the breeding method, rather than any property of the food, implies a health warning that the evidence does not support, and that it raises costs. The US label survived political fights only to be partly struck down in court in 2025.
Case for “People’s right to know”: People choose food for many reasons beyond physical safety, including religious and ethical convictions, concern about herbicide use, support for particular farming systems or distrust of particular companies, and they cannot act on any of them without information. In a 2016 survey 91% of Americans agreed people have a right to know when they eat GM food. Labels cost little; the EU has required them since 1997 and GM-derived foods remain on sale there. Labels also enable traceability, so that if an unforeseen problem appears, scientists can study who was exposed. Consumer organizations such as BEUC argue that the same reasoning applies to gene-edited foods.
Case for “A label that misleads”: A mandatory label is a government signal, and consumers reasonably read it as a warning; where GM labels are required, retailers often drop labeled products, so the label works as a ban by other means. The National Academies concluded that mandatory labeling “is not justified to protect public health,” and labeling a process rather than a property tells the shopper nothing about nutrition, safety or environmental impact. People who want to avoid GM ingredients already can, through voluntary labels such as USDA Organic and Non-GMO Project Verified, which put the cost on those who want the information. Forcing labels on gene-edited foods that are indistinguishable from conventional ones would also be practically impossible to enforce by testing.

Question: Do seed patents and industry concentration hurt farmers more than they help them?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Patents fund innovation / Concentration squeezes farmers
Why contested: The same facts, that two companies supply about 70% of US corn seed and that GM-dominated seed prices rose 463% from 1990 to 2020, are read as the price of innovation by one side and as market power by the other. USDA’s own economists found both effects at work.
Case for “Patents fund innovation”: A new GM trait costs about $115 million and 16.5 years to bring to market, according to an industry survey, and no company would spend that without the ability to recover it. Patent and variety protection let the large seed firms raise research spending from under $2 billion in 1990 to more than $7 billion by 2014, and farmers keep buying the seed because it pays: farm-level studies find average profits rise with GM adoption. Much of the concentration is driven by the cost of regulation itself, which favors the few firms that can afford it; streamlining approval, not breaking up the industry, is what would let smaller breeders back in.
Case for “Concentration squeezes farmers”: Seed for GM-dominated crops rose 463% in price over 30 years while the prices farmers received rose 56%. Patents bar farmers from saving and replanting seed, a practice as old as farming, and companies have sued farmers who did. When the same few firms own both the seeds and the herbicides they are engineered to tolerate, research is steered toward products that sell more chemicals rather than toward what farmers or the public need most. Groups from the European Parliament, which in 2024 voted to ban patents on gene-edited plants, to farmers’ movements in Africa and India argue that the ownership of crop genetics matters as much as the genetics themselves.

Question: Can GM crops help smallholder farmers in poor countries, or do they deepen dependence?
Tag: empirical
Poles: A lifeline for smallholders / A trap of dependence
Why contested: The gains measured in farm-level studies are largest in developing countries, but so are the risks of debt, failed pest management and loss of local seed systems. India’s Bt cotton is cited as both a triumph and a warning, and publicly developed crops like Bangladesh’s Bt eggplant and Golden Rice complicate the picture of GM as a corporate product.
Case for “A lifeline for smallholders”: Yield and profit gains from GM crops are larger in developing countries, by 14 and 60 percentage points respectively in the Klümper and Qaim meta-analysis, because pest pressure is higher and alternatives fewer. In India, Bt cotton raised household living standards by 18% among the smallholders Kathage and Qaim followed. Many of the crops now arriving were developed by public institutions and given away royalty-free: Bt eggplant by Bangladesh’s Agricultural Research Institute, Golden Rice by public researchers for vitamin A–deficient populations, insect-resistant cowpea in Nigeria and Ghana, and gene-edited rice by India’s ICAR. Blocking them in the name of protecting the poor denies the poor the choice farmers in rich countries already have; a group of more than 100 Nobel laureates asked in 2016 how many poor people must die before opposition to Golden Rice is considered “a crime against humanity.”
Case for “A trap of dependence”: Smallholders who buy hybrid GM seed every year at prices set by distant companies lose the seed-saving and exchange that has protected them against bad years for millennia. In India, Bt cotton’s early gains faded as pink bollworm became resistant, and Kranthi and Stone find farmers now spend more on pesticides than before. The Alliance for Food Sovereignty in Africa argues that agroecology, farmer-managed seed systems and participatory breeding deliver higher yields, better soil and more resilience “without corporate dependency or ecological risk,” and that money spent on GM and gene editing is a “costly distraction” from them. Vitamin A deficiency, the target of Golden Rice, is caused by poverty and poor diets and can be addressed with supplements, diverse diets and conventionally bred orange-fleshed crops, without the regulatory and ecological questions of a GM crop.

Question: Who should decide whether GM crops are grown: shared international standards or each country and community?
Tag: value
Poles: Shared science-based rules / National food sovereignty
Why contested: Trade agreements require food restrictions to rest on scientific risk assessment, and a 2024 trade panel ruled Mexico’s GM corn limits broke them. Mexico, Kenyan courts and many farmers’ movements answer that a country’s choice about its staple crops, its seed heritage and its farming system is not only a question of food safety.
Case for “Shared science-based rules”: When every country can set its own rules for reasons other than evidence, trade becomes hostage to politics, and the losers are both exporting farmers and importing consumers who pay more for food; the United States exported $4.8 billion of corn to Mexico in ten months of 2024. Mexico agreed to science-based standards in the USMCA, and the panel found its measures were not based on science on all seven claims. Divergent rules also slow the arrival of useful crops in the poorest countries, which often follow European rules out of fear of losing export markets, and they leave importing countries dependent on foreign GM feed they will not let their own farmers grow.
Case for “National food sovereignty”: Food is culture and livelihood, not only a commodity. Mexico is the birthplace of maize and home to dozens of native varieties, and its government argues that protecting them from cross-pollination by GM corn is a matter of national heritage that no trade panel should overrule; it wrote a ban on planting GM corn into its constitution in 2025. In Kenya, the Philippines and India, courts have halted GM approvals over inadequate public consultation and biosafety review, and India’s Supreme Court ordered a national policy on GM crops in 2024 after splitting on GM mustard. A risk assessment can say whether a food is safe to eat; it cannot decide what kind of farming a society wants.

## Positions

Camp: Keep safety reviews and labels for all GM foods
Adherents: BEUC, the European Consumer Organisation, which holds that organisms obtained from new genomic techniques “must continue being regulated as GMOs, incl. pre-market risk assessment and approval,” and that “traceability and labelling of products produced using these techniques must guarantee consumers’ right to know and freedom of choice” (https://www.beuc.eu/farm-fork-strategy-consumer-view); Just Label It, the US campaign for mandatory GM labeling, which argues that “without labeling of GMOs, we cannot make informed choices about our food” and frames its aim as labels rather than removing GM foods from shelves (https://www.justlabelit.org/right-to-know-center/right-to-know/). Others include the natural-foods retailer Natural Grocers, lead plaintiff in the case that struck down parts of the US label rule in 2025, and, in the EU debate over gene editing, organic farming groups such as IFOAM Organics Europe.
Case: GM foods can be sold, but only after an independent, case-by-case check that each one is safe for people and the environment, and with a clear label so that everyone can decide for themselves whether to buy it. People choose food for reasons of health, conscience, religion and politics, and a market cannot respect those choices if the information is hidden. Labels are cheap and familiar: the EU has required them since 1997, more than 60 countries require them, and GM-derived foods are still sold. Traceability is also a safety tool: if a problem emerges years after approval, it is the only way to find out who was exposed and to recall the product. The same principles must apply to gene-edited foods, which are produced by genetic engineering and should not escape review simply because the technique is newer. Public trust in food comes from transparency, and large majorities tell pollsters they want it.
Variation: Members differ on how strict the review should be and on what counts as GM. European consumer groups want gene-edited foods assessed and labeled exactly like older GMOs; some US label advocates have focused on the label alone and accept the existing federal safety system. They also differ on format: most insist on words on the package, and in the United States they successfully challenged QR-code-only disclosure as unusable for many shoppers.
Best evidence: In a 2016 US survey 88% supported mandatory labeling of foods containing GMOs and 91% agreed people have a right to know (Annenberg Public Policy Center, https://www.annenbergpublicpolicycenter.org/americans-support-gmo-food-labels-but-dont-know-much-about-safety-of-genetically-modified-foods/). The EU has combined GM labeling with continued authorizations of GM crops for food and feed import, showing the two can coexist (European Commission, 16 December 2025, https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety-news/commission-authorises-and-renews-use-four-safe-genetically-modified-crops-food-and-animal-feed-2025-12-16_en). In October 2025 the US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit agreed with label advocates that USDA could not exempt highly refined GM foods merely because modified DNA cannot be detected in them, or allow QR codes and text messages to stand in for on-package disclosure (https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/10/31/22-16770.pdf).
Answer to the strongest objection: Those who favor expanding GM crops say a label implies a danger the science does not support. This camp answers that a label is information, not a warning, and that consumers routinely see labels for facts that carry no health risk, such as country of origin, organic status or whether fish is farmed. If people avoid labeled products, that is their choice to make, not a reason to withhold the facts; and the remedy for unfounded fear is more openness, not less. On gene-edited foods that cannot be detected by testing, they answer that the EU already relies on paperwork and traceability rather than lab tests for many labels, and that a developer who knows what it edited can disclose it.

Camp: Expand GM and gene-edited crops
Adherents: Alliance for Science, a nonprofit founded at Cornell University that conducts “policy advocacy, communications campaigns, and research amplification, to promote an enabling environment for science-based solutions” and promotes agricultural biotechnology in Africa and Asia (https://allianceforscience.org/about/); Richard J. Roberts, Nobel laureate in medicine, who organized the 2016 letter in which more than 100 laureates wrote that crops improved through biotechnology have been found “as safe as, if not safer than those derived from any other method of production” and urged Greenpeace to end its opposition to Golden Rice (https://www.supportprecisionagriculture.org/nobel-laureate-gmo-letter_rjr.html). Others include the seed and crop-protection industry through CropLife International, US farm groups such as the National Corn Growers Association, and most agricultural economists and plant scientists who study the crops.
Case: Thirty years and billions of meals have shown that approved GM crops are as safe as any others, and every major scientific academy that has looked agrees. The traits in use today have let farmers grow more with fewer insecticide sprays, less tillage and less labor; the average farmer who adopts them earns more, and the gains are largest for poor farmers facing heavy pest pressure. The obstacle is not the science but the rules: approval of a single trait now takes about 16 years and over $100 million, much of it regulatory, so only the largest companies can afford to bring a crop to market, and public projects like Golden Rice wait decades. Regulation should judge each crop by its traits and risks, not by how it was bred, and gene editing should be free to deliver drought-tolerant, disease-resistant and more nutritious crops quickly. A warming climate and a growing population cannot afford to leave these tools on the shelf.
Variation: The camp includes seed companies that rely on patents and public breeders who give their crops away. Companies emphasize regulatory streamlining and strong intellectual property; public institutions such as Bangladesh’s Agricultural Research Institute and India’s ICAR emphasize royalty-free crops for smallholders. Some in the camp would deregulate gene editing first and transgenic crops later; others hold that transgenic crops are equally safe and should be judged the same way. Most oppose mandatory process-based labels, though some accept the US disclosure standard as a political settlement.
Best evidence: The US National Academies found “no substantiated evidence of a difference in risks to human health” between commercial GM crops and conventional ones after examining almost 900 publications (NASEM, 2016, https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2016/05/genetically-engineered-crops-experiences-and-prospects-new-report). A meta-analysis of 147 studies found average yield gains of 22%, pesticide reductions of 37% and profit gains of 68% (Klümper & Qaim, 2014, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0111629). A randomized trial in Bangladesh found Bt eggplant raised net yields by 42% and cut pesticide costs by 47% (Ahmed et al., 2019, https://cgspace.cgiar.org/items/075500ed-de58-4a2d-9863-01db93eb5d72). More than 100 billion farm animals have been raised on GM feed with no adverse trend in health (Van Eenennaam & Young, 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25184846/).
Answer to the strongest objection: Critics point to resistant weeds and insects and to rising seed prices. The camp answers that resistance is a problem of how any pest control is used, not of genetic engineering; conventional herbicides and insecticides face the same evolution, and the remedy is diverse traits and good management, which new technology makes easier. Seed prices have risen because the seed does more, replacing sprays and labor, and farmers keep choosing it where alternatives exist. Concentration is real, but the heaviest force behind it is the cost of regulation, which shuts out small firms; the fastest way to more competition is cheaper, product-based approval.

Camp: Keep GM crops out of farms and food
Adherents: Alliance for Food Sovereignty in Africa, a coalition of African farmer, faith and civil society networks, which declared in November 2025 that “Africa does not need genome editing to feed its people,” called for a moratorium on releases of genome-edited organisms and urged that public resources go to agroecology and farmer-led research (https://afsafrica.org/blog/genome-editing-is-not-an-african-priority-it-is-a-costly-distraction/); the Government of Mexico under President Claudia Sheinbaum, which proposed and enacted a constitutional ban on planting GM corn to protect the country’s native maize, describing the reform as promoting farming “free from genetic modification” (Presidency of Mexico, 26 January 2025, https://www.gob.mx/presidencia/prensa/presidenta-claudia-sheinbaum-enviara-al-congreso-de-la-union-reforma-constitucional-para-proteger-el-maiz-mexicano). Others include Greenpeace, La Via Campesina, Navdanya and its founder Vandana Shiva, the Kenyan Peasants League, and the Filipino farmer-scientist network MASIPAG, which won the 2024 court order halting Golden Rice.
Case: GM crops are a solution designed for an industrial farming system that is itself the problem. They lock farmers into buying patented seed and matching chemicals every season, concentrate power over the world’s food in a handful of corporations, and spread their genes into native varieties and organic fields where they cannot be recalled. Their headline traits have bred glyphosate-resistant superweeds and Bt-resistant pests, and their long-term effects on health and ecosystems have never been studied independently at the scale their spread demands. The real causes of hunger are poverty, inequality and waste, not a shortage of patented genes. Agroecology, farmer-managed seed systems, crop diversity and participatory breeding already raise yields, restore soils and build resilience without dependence, and they keep decisions about food in the hands of those who grow and eat it. New gene-edited crops are GMOs by another name and should face the same refusal.
Variation: Members differ in scope and method. Some, like Mexico, ban cultivation of a culturally central crop while still importing GM grain for feed; others, like AFSA and La Via Campesina, oppose GM crops and gene editing in all forms. In Europe several groups in this camp argue in practice for keeping strict GMO rules and labels, as the furthest they can win, while still opposing GM cultivation. Some emphasize health, some ecology, and some sovereignty and farmers’ rights.
Best evidence: Over 20 years in India, Kranthi and Stone found Bt cotton adoption a poor predictor of yield, with pink bollworm resistance and surging secondary pests leaving farmers spending more on pesticides than before Bt (Nature Plants, 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32170289/). Sixty-two weed species have evolved resistance to glyphosate (International Herbicide-Resistant Weed Database, https://www.weedscience.org/Pages/SOASummary.aspx), and global glyphosate use rose almost 15-fold after herbicide-tolerant crops appeared (Benbrook, 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27752438/). Seed prices for GM-dominated US crops rose 463% from 1990 to 2020, against a 56% rise in crop prices, while two firms came to supply 72% of US corn acres (USDA ERS, https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2023/august/expanded-intellectual-property-protections-for-crop-seeds-increase-innovation-and-market-power-for-companies). More than 300 scientists signed a 2015 statement that there is “no scientific consensus on GMO safety” (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12302-014-0034-1).
Answer to the strongest objection: Supporters of GM crops say the camp denies poor farmers a proven tool and ignores the scientific consensus on safety. The camp answers that a consensus built largely on industry data and short-term studies is not the same as independent proof, and that safety on a plate is only one question: the others are who owns the seed, what happens to soils, insects and native varieties, and whether farmers can still save seed. The measured gains are averages that hide failures, such as India’s pink bollworm, and they are compared against chemical-intensive farming rather than against agroecological alternatives that have never received comparable investment. Choosing not to adopt a technology is a legitimate democratic decision, not a denial of science.

Camp: Free gene editing but keep GMO rules for transgenic crops
Adherents: The European Commission, whose regulation, adopted in 2026, treats gene-edited plants as conventional when they contain “genetic changes that could also occur naturally or through conventional breeding,” while plants with more complex modifications stay under GMO risk assessment, authorization, traceability and labeling (https://food.ec.europa.eu/plants/new-genomic-techniques_en); the UK Government, which stated when announcing its precision-breeding reforms that “gene editing is different from genetic modification, because it does not result in the introduction of DNA from other species” and that “GMO regulations would continue to apply where gene editing introduces DNA from other species” (Department for Environment, Food & Rural Affairs, 29 September 2021, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/plans-to-unlock-power-of-gene-editing-unveiled). Others include the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, which describes its genome-edited rice varieties as containing “no foreign DNA” and “comparable to conventionally bred types” in a country that has not approved any GM food crop for commercial cultivation (ICAR, 4 May 2025, https://www.iari.res.in/files/Latest-News/ICAR_Genome_Edited_Rice_04052025.pdf), and Japan, Argentina and other countries that exempt edits without foreign DNA from GMO rules.
Case: The line that matters is not between old and new breeding techniques but between changes nature could make and changes it could not. A targeted edit that switches off a gene or swaps a single letter of DNA produces a plant indistinguishable from one found in a field of natural mutants or produced by the radiation breeding farmers have used for decades, and it should be regulated the same way. Inserting genes from other species, such as a bacterial insecticide gene or a herbicide-tolerance gene, creates combinations nature does not, and it is reasonable to keep case-by-case assessment, traceability and labels for those. Drawing the line here lets public breeders and small seed companies use precise tools to develop climate-resilient crops quickly, while keeping safeguards where the public’s concerns are strongest. It also excludes the traits that drew the most criticism of first-generation GM crops: the EU law keeps herbicide-tolerant plants out of the conventional-like category, bars all gene-edited plants from organic farming, and labels the seed so farmers know what they plant.
Variation: Countries drawing this line place it in different places. The EU sets numerical limits on the number and kind of edits for its category 1 and labels category 1 seed; the UK covers changes that could have arisen through traditional processes; India exempts the simplest classes of edits. Some supporters see it as a stable settlement; others, including some in the expand camp, accept it as a first step toward regulating all crops by product.
Best evidence: India’s first genome-edited rice varieties, cleared under its simplified framework for edited crops, yielded 19% more than their parent variety in trials (Kamala) and 10 to 30% more under drought and salt stress (Pusa DST Rice 1), according to ICAR. After three years of negotiation the European Parliament approved the EU regulation by 431 votes to 201 in June 2026 (USDA Foreign Agricultural Service, https://www.fas.usda.gov/data/gain/2026/06/european-union-european-parliament-passes-landmark-new-genomic-techniques-regulation), after member governments adopted it in April 2026, showing that a majority of elected representatives in a famously GM-skeptical region accept the distinction. The law’s design answers specific criticisms: herbicide tolerance is excluded from category 1, all gene-edited plants are barred from organic production, and seed bags must be labeled (European Parliament, https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20251201IPR31710/new-genomic-techniques-deal-to-support-the-green-transition-in-farming).
Answer to the strongest objection: From one side, the expand camp says the line is unscientific because transgenic crops have proved just as safe; this camp answers that regulation must also earn public trust, and that freeing edits now while keeping transgenic rules is the change the public will accept. From the other, critics say edited crops are GMOs by another name and should be labeled; this camp answers that a label on a product that no test can distinguish from conventional food would be unenforceable and misleading, that farmers still get a label on the seed, and that edited plants with novel or complex changes stay under the full GMO rules.

## Crux

Is what matters about a crop what it is, or also how it was made and who controls it, and when a new kind of crop shows no evidence of harm, who should carry the burden of proof?

Every camp on this page accepts that individual GM crops can be tested and that some have delivered real benefits to some farmers. They split over whether genetic engineering is a meaningful category at all. If the risks and benefits of a crop depend only on its traits, then the breeding method is irrelevant, labels by method are misleading, and the case for strict GM-specific rules collapses into ordinary crop and food-safety regulation. If the method itself carries distinct risks, or if it is inseparable from patents, seed concentration and herbicide-based farming, then GM-specific review, labeling or refusal follows. The hybrid that the EU, the UK and India have now written into law draws the line inside genetic engineering, at whether DNA from another species is introduced.

What evidence could settle: whether foods from GM and gene-edited crops show any health or nutritional difference from conventional counterparts over long periods, which long-term independent studies could test further; whether gene editing produces unintended changes more often or of a different kind than conventional mutagenesis; how much of the yield, pesticide and income gains measured on farms persist after pests and weeds adapt, where the record runs from Hutchison’s area-wide corn borer suppression to India’s resistant pink bollworm; whether rising seed prices reflect value delivered to farmers or market power, which USDA’s economists say is both; and whether mandatory labels change what people buy and what retailers stock.

What no data can close: how much proof of safety should be demanded before a new technology is used, and whether the burden lies with its developers or its critics; whether people have a right to know how their food was produced even when the method does not change the food; whether the private ownership of crop genetics is an acceptable price for faster innovation; and who should decide what a country grows, its scientists and regulators, its trade partners, or its farmers and citizens voting on the kind of agriculture they want.
