# School Choice — research

Slug: school-choice-public-education
Scope: Public funds following students to private and charter schools.
Researched: 2026-11-23

## Overview

This topic is about whether public money for schooling should follow each child to a school the family picks, including private and religious schools and independently run public charter schools, or should go mainly to the public school systems that local districts run. It covers the main instruments: vouchers (a state payment toward private school tuition), education savings accounts or ESAs (a state-funded account a family can spend on tuition, tutoring, therapy, curriculum or other approved costs), tax-credit scholarships (donors get a tax credit for giving to nonprofits that fund scholarships, including the new federal credit that starts in 2027), and charter schools (public schools funded per student but run outside the district under a performance contract). It covers the evidence on what these programs do to the students who use them, to the students who stay in district schools and to state budgets, and the value questions underneath: who should decide how a child is educated, whether public money should pay for religious instruction, and what rules should follow public money into private schools. It deliberately leaves aside debates about what is taught in any school (curriculum, books, diversity programs, which belong to dei-in-institutions and related topics), school funding formulas between districts except where choice programs change them, homeschooling regulation in general, and higher education. The debate is mostly American, because the United States is where public funding of private schooling has expanded fastest and most contentiously in recent years; other countries, such as the Netherlands, Sweden, Chile and Australia, fund non-government schools in ways both sides cite as models or warnings.

The why-now event: the November 3, 2026 elections handed governorships in several of the states with the largest new choice programs to Democrats who campaigned against or promised to rein them in. Education Week reported that Democrats won governorships in Georgia, Iowa, Ohio and Texas; that Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa pledged to repeal the state’s new education savings account program; that Iowa Governor-elect Rob Sand called for more oversight; that Ohio Governor-elect Amy Acton plans more accountability and transparency; and that Arizona Governor Katie Hobbs, reelected, campaigned on income caps and stricter oversight of the state’s billion-dollar program (Education Week, November 5, 2026, https://www.edweek.org/policy-politics/after-elections-democrats-will-run-school-choice-programs-theyve-blasted/2026/11). Democrats also won both chambers of the Arizona Legislature, and their leaders say they will start from a proposed $150,000 income cap rather than full repeal (KJZZ, November 12, 2026, https://www.kjzz.org/politics/2026-11-12/how-democrats-plan-to-reform-arizonas-school-voucher-program). In Iowa, Ohio and Texas, Republicans keep control of at least one legislative chamber, which limits repeal (Education Week, same article). Two other recent events sit alongside the election. On October 15, 2026, a unanimous three-judge panel of Ohio’s 10th District Court of Appeals held that the state’s EdChoice voucher program violates the Ohio Constitution’s requirement of “a thorough and efficient system of common schools,” affirming a June 2025 trial ruling; the program keeps operating while an appeal to the Ohio Supreme Court is expected (Associated Press via Spectrum News, October 15, 2026, https://spectrumnews1.com/oh/columbus/news/2026/10/15/court-declares-ohio-edchoice-program-unconstitutional). And the federal scholarship tax credit created by the July 4, 2025 budget law, worth up to $1,700 per taxpayer for gifts to scholarship-granting organizations, takes effect for tax years ending after December 31, 2026 in states that opt in (26 U.S.C. § 25F, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/25F). The IRS listed 27 participating states on June 8, 2026 (https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/more-than-half-the-us-states-signed-up-to-participate-in-the-federal-scholarship-tax-credit-program-enacted-under-the-one-big-beautiful-bill); a tracker kept by Democrats for Education Reform counted 32 as of November 2026, including Democratic-led New York, which opted in in October (https://dfer.org/fstc/opt-in-tracker/). Education Week reports that every Republican governor except Vermont’s has opted in, that New Mexico’s outgoing Democratic governor opted in the day after the election, and that Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger plans to withdraw her state.

The debate is live because private school choice has grown from a set of small, targeted programs into a mainstream way of funding schooling in much of the country, faster than research on the new universal programs can report. EdChoice, a pro-choice research and advocacy group, counts more than 1.5 million students in 75 private choice programs, and Education Week counts 18 states that make virtually every student eligible, all of which went universal in the four years before its January 2026 count. On one side are Republican governors and legislators, the Trump administration, groups such as the American Federation for Children, EdChoice, the Heritage Foundation and the Cato Institute, and many religious school communities, who argue that parents, not districts, should control their children’s share of public education money. On the other are the two national teachers unions, most Democratic officeholders, public school superintendents and school boards, and groups such as the Network for Public Education, who argue that the programs drain and fragment the public system while subsidizing families already in private school. Between them sit people who support charter schools but not vouchers, people who accept private choice only if it is targeted to need and tied to testing and audits, and a small set of libertarians and homeschoolers who oppose public money for private schooling because they expect government rules to follow it.

### 4. States opted into the federal scholarship tax credit
- Label: States participating in the federal scholarship tax credit
- Value: 32
- Unit: states of 50 (tracker count); 27 on the IRS’s official list of June 8, 2026
- Data year: November 2026 (tracker); June 8, 2026 (IRS)
- Source label: Democrats for Education Reform, Federal Scholarship Tax Credit Opt-In Tracker; IRS news release IR-2026-76
- Source URL: https://dfer.org/fstc/opt-in-tracker/ ; https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/more-than-half-the-us-states-signed-up-to-participate-in-the-federal-scholarship-tax-credit-program-enacted-under-the-one-big-beautiful-bill
- Note: By November 2026, 32 states had opted into the federal credit that pays up to $1,700 per taxpayer for gifts to scholarship groups, by one tracker’s count; the IRS’s official list in June had 27.
- Context: The credit, in section 25F of the tax code, was enacted on July 4, 2025 in the budget reconciliation law and applies to tax years ending after December 31, 2026. Scholarships may go to students in households with income no more than 300 percent of the area median who are eligible to enroll in a public school, and scholarship organizations must spend at least 90 percent of their income on scholarships. Credits flow only in states whose governor or designated body elects to take part and submits a list of qualifying organizations by January 1 each year (26 U.S.C. § 25F, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/25F). Because scholarships can cover a wide range of elementary and secondary expenses, some Democratic governors, including New York’s Kathy Hochul, opted in on the stated condition that public school students benefit too. The IRS list on June 8 named Alabama, Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Virginia, West Virginia and Wyoming; the tracker adds Kansas, Kentucky, New Mexico, New York and North Carolina. Education Week reports that Virginia’s new governor plans to withdraw.

## Questions

### 1. Do private school choice programs leave students better educated?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: students gain / students lose ground
- Why contested: The best evidence comes from older, targeted programs, and it points in two directions. Lottery and matched studies of vouchers for low-income students in Louisiana, Washington, D.C., Indiana and Ohio found test-score effects that Patrick Wolf of the University of Arkansas summarizes as “neutral to negative” (Education Week, January 2026), with Louisiana’s the largest loss (stat 15). Studies of longer-run outcomes, such as high school graduation and college enrollment, more often find gains, including the 2025 study of Ohio’s targeted EdChoice program. The new universal programs have barely been studied: FutureEd found that of the first eight universal states, only Indiana and Iowa require participating students to take the same state tests as public school students, and Arizona and Oklahoma required no assessments in 2023–24 (https://www.future-ed.org/early-returns-first-results-from-the-new-wave-of-public-funding-of-private-schooling/).
- Students gain: Those who expect gains argue that test scores are the wrong yardstick for schools that are not teaching to state tests, and that what parents care about and what predicts adult success are graduation, college, safety and character. On those measures the record is better: Chingos, Figlio and Karbownik found Ohio voucher users were more likely to enroll in college and finish a bachelor’s degree; the federal evaluation of the D.C. program found it raised students’ chances of graduating from high school, based on parent reports (Institute of Education Sciences, 2010, https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20104018); and EdChoice reports that most studies of choice find positive effects. Early results from new programs are encouraging, they add: Iowa’s first-year ESA students outperformed public school students on state math and English tests, and Arkansas’s scored at the 57th percentile in math and 59th in English on national tests (Education Week, January 2026). The Louisiana results, in their reading, may reflect a heavily regulated program that deterred stronger private schools from joining.
- Students lose ground: Those who expect losses argue that the most rigorous studies, the randomized lotteries, found some of the largest test-score declines ever measured in education research, and that early gains for Iowa and Arkansas students mostly reflect who was already in private school rather than what the program did; Douglas Harris of Tulane cautions that researchers cannot yet separate program effects from selection, since schools choose students and students choose schools (Education Week, January 2026). Attainment gains come from small programs years ago, before universal programs drew in thousands of new, small and untested providers. Without common tests, they argue, no one can know whether the billions now being spent buy better learning, and a program that cannot show results should not keep expanding.

### 2. Should public money go to families who already pay for private school?
- Tag: value
- Poles: fund every child / fund new access
- Why contested: In the first years of universal programs most participants were already in private school or homeschooled: 71 percent in Arizona as of April 2025 (stat 7), and about two-thirds in Iowa and Arkansas in 2023–24 (FutureEd). Whether that is a flaw or the point depends on what the program is for. RAND’s Arizona study found participants tend to come from higher-achieving, more affluent and whiter districts, while Texas’s capped program, which prioritizes low-income students, reports that about four in five awardees are below 200 percent of the poverty line (stat 8).
- Fund every child: Those who favor universal eligibility argue that education money belongs to the child, not the institution, and every family pays the taxes that fund schools. A family that sacrifices to pay tuition while also paying for public schools is paying twice, and treating them equally is fairness, not a giveaway. Universal programs also build the broad political coalition that keeps programs funded and stable, where small programs for the poor have repeatedly been cut or capped. Means tests create cliffs and paperwork, and the share of new users coming from public schools rises as families learn about the option: among Arizona’s new recipients in grades 1–12, the share who came from public schools rose from 21 percent in 2022–23 to 48 percent in 2023–24, by FutureEd’s count.
- Fund new access: Those who favor targeting argue that the public case for choice was always about giving children without options the same choices wealthier families already have. Paying families who would have chosen private school anyway adds cost without changing where any child learns, and it pushes money toward the families who need it least; Jon Valant of the Brookings Institution told a Senate forum in June 2025 that the new programs are “much larger than past voucher programs and less targeted to students in need” (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-risks-of-universal-private-school-choice-programs/). Income caps, such as the $150,000 limit Arizona Democrats now propose, keep the option for those who need it while limiting the cost.

### 3. Does private school choice push public schools to improve or drain them?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: competition lifts / losses hollow out
- Why contested: Studies of smaller programs mostly find small positive effects on students who stay in public schools, such as the Florida study by Figlio, Hart and Karbownik (stat 16). But today’s programs are much larger, and the effects of large, concentrated enrollment losses on district finances and services have not been studied at scale. The Ohio appeals court in October 2026 found that EdChoice, as funded, creates “a downward spiral” for public schools; the state disputes this on appeal.
- Competition lifts: Those who see benefits argue that monopolies do not improve without pressure, and that districts that can lose students and money have reason to listen to parents. They point to the weight of studies finding positive or neutral effects on public school students, 26 of 29 by EdChoice’s count, including the Florida evidence that gains were largest for low-income students in schools facing the most competition. They add that public school enrollment has been falling mainly because of birth rates, that private choice has not made a major dent in aggregate public enrollment (Education Week, January 2026), and that public per-pupil spending has kept rising (stat 10).
- Losses hollow out: Those who see harm argue that district costs such as buildings, buses, special education and pensions do not fall when a few students leave each school, so losing per-student funding means cuts for the children who remain, who are disproportionately poor or have disabilities. The competition studies measure programs that were a small fraction of today’s size, they say, and the effects of a state moving a tenth of its education budget to private choice, as Arizona has, are untested. Rural districts, with few or no private schools nearby, bear costs without gaining options, which is why rural Republicans in states such as Texas and Tennessee long resisted vouchers.

### 4. What do universal choice programs really cost taxpayers?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: modest or savings / large new cost
- Why contested: Gross spending is clear, about $10.6 billion a year nationally (stat 6) and $886 million in Arizona in 2024–25 (stat 9). The net cost depends on what the state would have spent on the same students if the program did not exist, which requires assumptions about who would have stayed in public school. Estimates for Arizona differ by a factor of two or three. EdChoice’s Martin Lueken estimates the net cost of universal expansion at $178.3 million in its first year and $118.0 million in its second (https://www.edchoice.org/2025-did-arizonas-esa-expansion-blow-a-hole-in-the-budget/); the Grand Canyon Institute, an Arizona think tank, has estimated about $350 million for fiscal 2025 [secondary reports only; GCI report not opened].
- Modest or savings: Those who see a small net cost argue that each student who leaves a public school saves the state more than the account costs, since accounts are typically well below public per-pupil spending (stat 10), and that the cost of students already in private school is a one-time transition cost of treating all families equally. They note that Arizona’s budget troubles had several causes, including tax cuts and falling district enrollment that would have happened anyway, and that the Common Sense Institute, as cited by EdChoice, projects state spending on district schools about $800 million below earlier projections because of broad enrollment declines.
- Large new cost: Those who see a large cost argue that paying for students who were never in public school is new spending, full stop, and that it has repeatedly overrun projections: FutureEd reports that Arizona’s actual costs ran well above the $33 million and $64 million projected for the first two years of universal eligibility, and RAND found program spending reached about 10 percent of the state education department’s budget. Because universal programs are entitlements in many states, costs grow with demand rather than appropriations, crowding out other priorities. The federal credit adds an estimated $25.9 billion in forgone revenue over ten years (stat 5).

### 5. Should private schools that take public money follow public rules?
- Tag: value
- Poles: same rules / freedom to differ
- Why contested: Public schools must take all students, give state tests, follow special education law and meet state curriculum and teacher standards. Most private choice programs require far less, and the rules vary widely by state: some require state or national tests, some require none; most let private schools keep their own admissions policies, including religious ones. Each additional rule protects students and taxpayers in the view of one side and erodes the independence that makes private schools worth choosing in the view of the other.
- Same rules: Those who want public rules argue that public money should come with public accountability: state tests so results can be compared, financial audits, background checks, and admission open to any child the money is meant for. NEA argues that “private and religious schools can — and do — discriminate in admissions” and that students with disabilities give up federal special education protections when they use vouchers (https://www.nea.org/advocating-for-change/action-center/our-issues/vouchers). RAND found that 28 percent of Arizona account funds awarded had not been spent and that more than 6,000 vendors had registered, which, in this view, invites waste without audits.
- Freedom to differ: Those who resist rules argue that the accountability that matters is a parent’s ability to leave, and that requiring private schools to give state tests and follow public rules would turn them into the public schools families were trying to leave. Requiring a single test pushes every school toward the same curriculum; requiring open admission would force religious schools to abandon their missions. Louisiana, in this reading, shows what heavy rules can do: Frederick Hess of the American Enterprise Institute has asked whether its regulations deterred good private schools from taking part. They support basic fraud controls and financial audits but not academic mandates.

### 6. Should public money pay for religious schooling?
- Tag: value
- Poles: equal treatment / keep church and state apart
- Why contested: About three-quarters of private school students attend religious schools (stat 12). The US Supreme Court has held that a state may include religious schools in a choice program (Zelman v. Simmons-Harris, 2002) and that if a state funds private schooling it cannot exclude schools because they are religious (Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue, 2020; Carson v. Makin, 2022, https://www.oyez.org/cases/2021/20-1088). In May 2025 the Court split 4–4 on whether a state may approve a religious public charter school, leaving in place Oklahoma’s ruling against it (Oklahoma Statewide Charter School Board v. Drummond, https://www.oyez.org/cases/2024/24-394), and in April 2026 it agreed to hear Catholic preschools’ challenge to their exclusion from Colorado’s universal preschool program over nondiscrimination rules (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/supreme-court-will-hear-from-religious-preschools-challenging-exclusion-from-taxpayer-funded-program). Many state constitutions still bar aid to religious schools, and state courts in Utah, Ohio and elsewhere have ruled against programs on state constitutional grounds.
- Equal treatment: Those who favor including religious schools argue that the money goes to families, who make a private choice, so the state is no more funding religion than when a Pell Grant pays for a Catholic university. Excluding religious schools singles out faith for unequal treatment, which the Supreme Court has said the Free Exercise Clause forbids, and denies religious families, many of them poor and urban, the education they want. Many state bans on aid to religious schools, the “Blaine amendments,” date from nineteenth-century hostility to Catholic immigrants.
- Keep church and state apart: Those who oppose funding argue that taxpayers should not be compelled to pay for religious instruction they do not share, and that schools receiving public money should not be allowed to turn away students or teachers because of their faith, sexuality or family. When public money flows to schools that may lawfully reject a child, the program serves some families on terms others cannot accept. Keeping public money in schools open to all, they argue, protects both religious freedom and the public character of schooling, and the founding generation’s concern was precisely compelled support for religion.

### 7. Who should decide how a child is educated: parents or the public?
- Tag: value
- Poles: parents decide / shared civic schooling
- Why contested: This is the oldest disagreement in the debate, and it does not turn on evidence. One view holds that education is first a family responsibility that the state funds; the other holds that public schooling is a shared civic institution through which a diverse society educates its next generation together, governed by elected boards. Most Americans hold some of both, which is why the same people can like their local public school and support choice.
- Parents decide: Those who put parents first argue that parents know and love their children best and bear the consequences of their schooling, and that a system that assigns children to schools by home address gives real choice only to families who can afford to move or pay tuition. Funding students rather than systems, in Corey DeAngelis’s phrase, simply extends to everyone a freedom the affluent already have. Pluralism is a strength: families differ in their values, and a free society should not require them to accept one model of education to get their share of public support.
- Shared civic schooling: Those who put the public system first argue that public schools are one of the few institutions where children of different backgrounds, faiths and politics learn together, and where taxpayers govern through elected boards and open meetings. Splitting education money into private accounts turns a common good into a private benefit and weakens the institution most children will continue to rely on. Parents already have a voice in public schools, they argue, and improving those schools serves more children than helping some leave.

### 8. Do charter schools serve students better than the district schools they draw from?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: charters do better / no real advantage
- Why contested: CREDO’s national study found average charter students gained modestly more than matched district peers, with much larger gains in schools run by charter networks and in some cities, and weaker results for students in special education and in some states (stat 14). Critics dispute how the gains are measured and argue that charter students differ from district students in ways matching cannot capture. The question matters because charters are the form of choice that keeps schools public, and the strength of the charter record shapes whether public school choice can answer families’ demand without private vouchers.
- Charters do better: Those who favor charters argue that the national evidence has strengthened over three studies, that gains are largest for Black, Hispanic and low-income students, and that charters do it with public accountability: they take all applicants by lottery, give state tests and can be closed for poor results. Charter enrollment grew by more than half a million in six years while districts shrank (stat 13), which, they argue, shows families value them. They add that a charter that fails can be closed, something rarely done to a failing district school.
- No real advantage: Those who doubt the charter record argue that the average effect is small, that “days of learning” conversions exaggerate it, and that students who apply to charter lotteries have motivated families even when test scores are matched. Charter growth drains district budgets in the same way vouchers do, and some charters, especially online charters, have performed very poorly. Unequal results across states and the closures and scandals that recur in the sector suggest, in this view, that charters are not a reliable improvement over well-funded district schools.

## Positions

### 1. Fund every student wherever they learn
- Adherents: American Federation for Children (institution; national school choice advocacy group founded by Betsy DeVos), https://www.federationforchildren.org/about/ , which says “When funding for education follows students to the school of their choice, families win”; Corey DeAngelis (school choice advocate and researcher, then at the American Federation for Children), written testimony to the House Ways and Means Committee, October 25, 2023, https://gop-waysandmeans.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Corey-DeAngelis-Written-Testimony-Final.pdf , which closes “We should fund students, not systems.” Also EdChoice, the Heritage Foundation, the Cato Institute, the Institute for Justice, the Trump administration, most Republican governors and legislators, and many Catholic and evangelical school leaders.
- Case in the camp’s voice: Public education money is raised for children, not for buildings or bureaucracies, and every child should get their share to spend at the school or on the services that fit them: a district school, a charter, a religious or secular private school, a microschool, tutoring or homeschooling. Assigning children to schools by address gives real choice only to families who can buy a house in a good district or pay tuition on top of their taxes; universal accounts extend that freedom to everyone. Parents are the best judges of their own children, and the ability to leave is the strongest accountability there is. Demand proves the point: more than 274,000 Texans applied for 100,000 accounts in the first year, and more than 1.5 million students now use private choice nationally. Universal eligibility matters too. Programs limited to the poor have been small, politically fragile and easy to cut, while universal programs build a broad constituency and treat every taxpaying family alike. Competition helps the children who stay: the large majority of studies find public school students do better when choice arrives. And the cost per student is lower than in the public system.
- Variation inside the camp: Instruments differ. Some prefer education savings accounts because they let families buy services beyond tuition; some, including many at the Cato Institute, prefer tax-credit scholarships because private donations keep government further away from the schools; many back the new federal credit as a floor every state can build on. Most oppose state testing mandates as a threat to private schools’ independence, though some accept a requirement to give a nationally normed test of the school’s choosing. Some champion charter schools as part of the same agenda; others focus on private choice.
- Best evidence: Attainment studies, such as Chingos, Figlio and Karbownik’s finding that Ohio’s targeted voucher raised college enrollment and bachelor’s completion, and the federal finding that the D.C. program raised high school graduation; the Florida competition study (Figlio, Hart and Karbownik) and EdChoice’s count of 26 of 29 competition studies finding benefits; demand in Texas and Arizona; per-student costs well below public per-pupil spending (stat 10); and public support of 69 to 78 percent for choice policies in EdChoice’s 2026 survey.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that most money goes to families already in private school, with no proof of better learning): Those families pay school taxes too, and treating them equally is fairness, not waste; the share of new participants leaving public schools rises each year as families learn about the option. Test scores in state exams measure alignment with the public curriculum, not the outcomes that matter most to parents, and the strongest evidence on graduation and college is positive. The answer to a weak school is a parent’s right to leave it, which public school systems rarely offer.

### 2. Keep public money in public schools
- Adherents: National Education Association (institution; the largest US teachers union), https://www.nea.org/advocating-for-change/action-center/our-issues/vouchers , which says “Vouchers take scarce funding from students in public schools and give those resources to unaccountable private schools”; Randi Weingarten (president, American Federation of Teachers), quoted by Education Week on November 5, 2026, https://www.edweek.org/policy-politics/after-elections-democrats-will-run-school-choice-programs-theyve-blasted/2026/11 : “In race after race, those who supported public schools and rejected private school vouchers triumphed.” Also the Network for Public Education (https://networkforpubliceducation.org/new-report-gives-17-states-failing-grades-for-abandoning-public-schools/), Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, most Democratic officeholders, many school boards and superintendents’ associations, and rural Republicans in several states.
- Case in the camp’s voice: Public schools educate about nine in ten American children, take every child who walks in the door, and answer to voters. Vouchers and education savings accounts move public money to private schools that can turn children away, need not give state tests, and are not covered by the same protections for students with disabilities. The results do not justify the cost. The most rigorous studies of vouchers found test-score declines, sometimes among the largest ever measured, and universal programs mostly pay families whose children were already in private school, which is new spending, not choice: 71 percent of Arizona’s universal users had been privately educated. The money comes out of budgets that fund the children who remain, in districts whose fixed costs do not shrink when a few students leave, and rural communities with no private schools pay without gaining options. Voters have rejected statewide voucher measures again and again, including all three on the ballot in 2024. Public money should go to public schools, and the way to give families better options is to fund and improve the schools most children will always attend.
- Variation inside the camp: Some want existing programs repealed; others, including many new Democratic governors, accept that large programs cannot be quickly undone and seek caps, audits and testing as a step toward winding them down. On charter schools the camp divides: the Network for Public Education and many union members oppose charter expansion as another drain on districts, while others accept well-regulated charters as public schools. Most oppose the federal scholarship credit, though Democratic governors in Colorado, New York and New Mexico opted in while saying public school students must benefit.
- Best evidence: The Louisiana lottery study (math down 0.4 standard deviations after one year) and the four-year follow-up; the federal D.C. evaluation finding no achievement effect after three years; RAND’s Arizona figures (71 percent previously privately educated; spending about 10 percent of the education department budget); the Ohio appeals court’s finding that EdChoice creates “a downward spiral” for public schools; and the 2024 ballot results in Kentucky, Nebraska and Colorado.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that this traps poor children in failing schools while richer families already choose): The choice programs now expanding do not mainly serve poor children in failing schools; they serve families already in private school and, by RAND’s finding in Arizona, come disproportionately from more affluent districts. Families who want options within public education have them in many places through magnet schools, open enrollment and career academies. Helping a few children leave while cutting the budgets of the schools the rest attend is not a remedy for failing schools; funding and improving them is.

### 3. Target choice to need and hold schools accountable
- Adherents: Democrats for Education Reform (institution; Jorge Elorza, chief executive), “Democrats, It’s Time to Embrace School Choice,” February 17, 2026, https://dfer.org/2026/02/17/democrats-its-time-to-embrace-school-choice/ , which argues that “Public schools, charter schools, private schools and hybrid models should all be on the table” so long as choice is “accessible and accountable”; Rob Sand (Governor-elect of Iowa), who has proposed regular audits of education savings account recipients and income limits to keep the most affluent families out (The 74, November 6, 2026, https://www.the74million.org/article/after-midterm-wave-democrats-inherit-a-school-choice-dilemma/). Also Arizona Governor Katie Hobbs and the incoming Democratic legislative leadership, who plan to start from a $150,000 income cap and background checks (KJZZ, November 12, 2026), and, on testing, the Thomas B. Fordham Institute, which has long backed results-based accountability for private choice programs (https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/olive-branch-voucher-accountability).
- Case in the camp’s voice: Public money following children to private schools can do real good for families without options, especially low-income children and children with disabilities whose public schools do not serve them well. But a blank check is not the way to do it. Programs should put the money where it changes what children can do, through income limits or priority for low-income students and students with disabilities, rather than subsidizing tuition families were already paying. And schools and vendors that take public money should show what they do with it: annual audits, background checks, published results on a common test, and basic admission and disability protections. Iowa already requires state tests of account users, and Texas reserves its money first for low-income students and students with disabilities, which shows the design is workable. As Elorza put it to Education Week after the 2026 elections, “So, what does a progressive version of an education savings account look like?” Large programs in Arizona, Texas and Florida are established and serve many children; repealing them would hurt families, but leaving them unchecked invites waste and undermines public trust.
- Variation inside the camp: Some come to this position as supporters of choice who want it to be sustainable, such as DFER and some at the Fordham Institute; others are opponents of universal programs who see reform as the achievable path, such as several new Democratic governors. They differ on how strict to be: some want state tests, others accept any nationally normed test; some want income caps near $150,000, others near $250,000, the cap Hobbs proposed earlier in 2026.
- Best evidence: The pattern that targeted programs show stronger long-run results than universal ones have yet shown, with the Ohio EdChoice attainment study drawn from the program’s income-targeted years; RAND’s finding that Arizona’s universal users are disproportionately privately educated and from more affluent districts, and its recommendation of targeted outreach and spending rules; Texas’s experience showing a capped, prioritized program can reach mostly low-income families (about four in five below 200 percent of the poverty line); and Education Week’s and The 74’s reporting that voters elected governors in 2026 who promised oversight rather than repeal.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that means tests and mandates shrink the supply of schools and the political coalition, or, from the other side, that reform legitimizes a drain on public schools): Accountability that protects public money is what sustains a program politically; scandals and runaway costs, not rules, are what the winning candidates in Arizona and Texas campaigned against. Tests and audits need not dictate curriculum if schools can choose among sound tests. And to those who want repeal: tens of thousands of families now depend on these programs, and Josh Cowen of Michigan State, a leading critic of vouchers, has argued programs of that size should not be dismantled (The 74); designing them well serves children better than an unwinnable fight.

### 4. Keep choice within public schools
- Adherents: Third Way (institution; center-left policy group), Ellie Cash, “Democrats: Reject Vouchers, Embrace Family Agency in Public Education,” October 16, 2026, https://www.thirdway.org/report/democrats-reject-vouchers-embrace-family-agency-in-public-education , which concludes that “Democrats can continue to reject voucher policies while emphasizing the importance of parental agency”; Progressive Policy Institute (institution), David Osborne, “Public School Choice – Charters – Boosts Equal Educational Opportunity. Private School Choice – Vouchers – Destroys It,” January 22, 2019, https://www.progressivepolicy.org/osborne-for-the-74-public-school-choice-charters-boosts-equal-educational-opportunity-private-school-choice-vouchers-destroys-it-which-do-we-want . Also former US Education Secretary John B. King Jr., who said in 2017 that “The evidence is quite clear that voucher programs that exist around the country have not delivered the results” while calling for accountable charter schools (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/former-education-secretary-says-trumps-budget-assault-american-dream), and many charter school leaders and urban Democrats.
- Case in the camp’s voice: Families deserve real choices, and the public system can offer them: charter schools, magnet schools, career academies, and open enrollment across and within districts. Choice inside the public system keeps what makes public schools public. Charter schools are tuition-free, take students by lottery, give state tests, and can be closed when they fail, so the public can see and act on results. The evidence favors this path: CREDO’s national study found charter students gained more than matched district peers, with the biggest gains for Black, Hispanic and low-income students, while the best studies of private vouchers found test-score losses. Vouchers, by contrast, mostly subsidize families who could already pay; as Third Way puts it, “vouchers don’t actually make private schools affordable.” And private schools can select their students. The right answer to families’ demand for choice is to expand it within public education, with universal enrollment systems so every family can use it, not to divert public money to schools the public cannot hold accountable.
- Variation inside the camp: Some put charters first; others emphasize open enrollment and district-run options and are wary of charter growth in particular places. Some accept narrow private-school programs for students with disabilities. Members of this camp split over the federal tax credit: some urge Democratic governors to refuse it, others to opt in and steer scholarships to public school expenses.
- Best evidence: CREDO’s National Charter School Study III (16 more days of learning in reading and 6 in math, 27 and 23 for charter network schools); charter growth of more than half a million students in six years (stat 13); the Louisiana and D.C. voucher evaluations; and evidence that most universal voucher money goes to students already in private school.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that charters also drain district budgets and that limiting choice to public schools excludes families who want religious education): Charters are public schools: money that follows a student to a charter stays in public education, under public oversight, open to all. Families remain free to choose religious schooling, as they always have, but the public is not obliged to pay for schools that can turn its children away. A strong public charter sector answers the demand for alternatives without the selectivity and opacity of private choice.

### 5. Keep private and home education independent of public money
- Adherents: Mises Institute (institution; libertarian research center), Oscar Grau, essay on the economics and ethics of vouchers, Power & Market, April 4, 2025, https://mises.org/power-market/economics-and-ethics-vouchers-and-free-market-education , arguing that “vouchers are as much tax-funded as education provided by the government”; Christian Home Educators of Colorado (institution; Carolyn Martin) and Texans for Homeschool Freedom (Faith Howe, president), quoted in The 74, March 17, 2025, https://www.the74million.org/article/gops-push-for-school-choice-sees-pushback-from-unlikely-crowd-homeschoolers/ : Martin said “Government money comes with government control,” and Howe said of homeschoolers, “We specifically opted out of this system.” Also Tennessee Stands and some state homeschool networks.
- Case in the camp’s voice: The question of how public money should follow children assumes the money should exist in the first place. Private schools and homeschooling are valuable precisely because they are independent of the state: free to teach their own faith, curriculum and values and answerable to the families who pay. Once they depend on public money, rules follow, because legislators must answer for how taxpayers’ dollars are spent: testing mandates, approved-vendor lists, reapplication, curriculum standards, admissions rules. It is already happening. Utah tightened its universal program in 2026 with preapproval, audits and reporting requirements, Arizona’s new majority plans background checks and income caps, and every scandal brings demands for more control. Over time, a tax-funded private sector becomes a second public system. Families who left the public system to escape its control should not be drawn back into it, and the real answer is to lower the taxes that fund government schooling so families keep their own money to spend as they choose.
- Variation inside the camp: Libertarians in the tradition of Murray Rothbard oppose all public funding of education and favor separating school and state; many homeschool families object mainly to programs that include homeschoolers and could lead to new rules for those who do not participate, and some accept tax credits for one’s own tuition spending as letting families keep their money rather than receive government money. Some religious school leaders share the worry about strings while still taking part.
- Best evidence: The steady addition of rules to programs once they grow, recorded in FutureEd’s 2026 legislative tracker (Utah H.B. 467 and proposals in Arizona to require state tests); the Louisiana program, about which Frederick Hess of the American Enterprise Institute asked “whether well-meaning regulations deter quality schools from accepting voucher students” (Education Next, 2019, https://www.educationnext.org/making-sense-lousy-test-results-louisianas-voucher-program/); Supreme Court cases over whether religious schools receiving public funds must follow state nondiscrimination rules (the Colorado preschool case accepted in April 2026); and homeschool groups’ reports of members’ concerns about testing and reapplication requirements.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that refusing public money leaves families who cannot afford tuition without options while government already dominates education): Government money cannot buy independence; it buys dependence, and the families it helps today will face the rules it brings tomorrow. Families can be helped without state funding through private scholarships, lower taxes and cheaper models such as homeschool co-ops and microschools, which have grown on their own. A free education sector protected from political control serves poor families better in the long run than a subsidized one that politicians can reshape after every election.

## Crux

The question under the others: does putting each child’s share of public education money under the family’s control, including at private and religious schools, give children a better education overall than concentrating that money in public schools governed in common, and how much should a family’s freedom to choose weigh against the public’s interest in a shared, accountable school system?

The part evidence can settle is what the new programs actually do, now that they are large. The older research covers small, targeted programs and is mixed: test-score effects of vouchers ranged from neutral to sharply negative (Louisiana, Washington, D.C.), while effects on high school graduation and college enrollment were more often positive (Ohio, D.C.), and studies of competition found small benefits for public school students (Florida). None of it measures universal programs serving a tenth of a state’s students. Over the next several years, evidence can show whether students who switch into private schools under universal programs learn more or less, and graduate and enroll in college at higher or lower rates, than similar students who stay; what share of participants would otherwise have been in public schools, and so what the programs truly cost; whether districts that lose many students improve or decline; and whether private school quality and supply rise to meet the money. That evidence depends on data many programs do not yet collect, such as common test results and records of where participants came from, which is itself part of the argument over rules.

The part no data can close is the weighing. People who agree on every finding can still disagree about whether education is first a family’s responsibility that the public funds or a civic institution that the public runs; whether families already paying tuition deserve the same support as those who are not; whether taxpayers should fund schools that teach a faith or can turn some children away; whether the right check on a school is a parent’s ability to leave or a public test and an elected board; and how much risk to the schools most children attend is acceptable for the sake of more options for some. A person persuaded that vouchers lower test scores might still favor them on grounds of parental freedom or religious liberty, and a person persuaded that choice helps participants might still oppose universal programs to protect a common public system.
