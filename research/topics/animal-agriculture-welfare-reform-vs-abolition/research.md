# Animal Agriculture — research

## Overview

Most of the meat, milk and eggs eaten in rich countries, and a fast-growing share elsewhere, comes from industrial animal farming: large operations that keep thousands to hundreds of thousands of animals indoors at high density, often in cages or crates, using breeds selected for fast growth or high output. FAO data processed by Our World in Data count about 86 billion land animals slaughtered for meat in 2024, nearly 79 billion of them chickens, and the Sentience Institute estimates that about three-quarters of farmed land animals worldwide, and 99% in the United States, live on factory farms. This topic covers the argument over what to do about that system. The main answers are to raise welfare standards and phase out the harshest confinement, by law or by corporate pledge; to keep intensive farming and improve it through veterinary science and markets; to replace large factory farms with smaller, pasture-based ones; to replace animal products with plant-based, fermented or cultivated alternatives; or to end the use of animals for food altogether. It includes the laws and ballot measures that set housing standards (cage and crate bans, California’s Proposition 12, the EU’s End the Cage Age commitment), corporate cage-free and broiler pledges, the economics of higher-welfare production, alternative-protein investment and regulation, and the strategic dispute inside the animal movement over whether welfare reforms help animals or entrench their use. It deliberately leaves to their own topics the philosophical question of how much animals matter morally (moral status of animals), the climate and diet arguments about livestock (meat and alternatives in climate strategy), and the land-use argument over intensive versus extensive farming (land sparing versus sharing); those appear here only where a welfare or reform argument turns on them. Small-scale subsistence herding and pastoralism, which raise different questions, are outside the scope except as a contrast.

Why now: on 7 July 2026 the European Commission’s first Livestock Strategy committed it to propose a targeted revision of EU welfare rules for laying hens and broilers by the end of 2026 and for pigs, including a move from crates to pens, by mid-2027, five years after it promised in 2021 to answer the End the Cage Age citizens’ initiative with a cage ban (vegconomist, 5 November 2026, https://vegconomist.com/politics-law/end-the-cage-age-proposals-left-out-european-commissions-2027-work-programme/). The Commission’s 2027 work programme, published on 4 November 2026, left the proposals out; days later a leaked draft was reported to phase out cages for hens with a 12-year transition and apply similar rules to imports, and the Commission scheduled a discussion of the proposals for 9 December (just-food, 11 November 2026, https://www.just-food.com/newsletters/eu-pushes-ahead-with-plan-to-ban-caged-hen-egg-farming). In the United States the fight runs the other way: on 30 April 2026 the House passed a farm bill, 224 to 200, that would override Proposition 12 and similar confinement laws in at least 15 states (vegconomist, https://vegconomist.com/politics-law/us-house-passes-farm-bill-provisions-would-override-state-animal-welfare-laws/), while the Senate Agriculture Committee’s draft, released on 23 June 2026, left that provision out (The New Lede, https://www.thenewlede.org/2026/06/senate-farm-bill-draft-save-our-bacon-act/). England published an Animal Welfare Strategy on 22 December 2025 that commits to move away from colony cages for hens and farrowing crates for sows (GOV.UK, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/defra-announces-biggest-animal-welfare-reforms-in-a-generation), and on 3 November 2026 voters in Denver and Washington, D.C. approved bans on force-feeding birds for foie gras, with about 67% and 73% voting yes, two years after Denver voters rejected a slaughterhouse ban (Sentient via Stacker, 12 November 2026, https://stacker.com/stories/government-politics/denver-and-dc-have-voted-ban-foie-gras).

The debate is live because broad public sympathy for farm animals has not turned into a change in what people buy, and because each side can point to evidence the others find hard to answer. In a 2023 Eurobarometer, 84% of EU respondents said farmed animal welfare should be better protected, and 62.7% of California voters approved Proposition 12 in 2018; yet 4% of Americans call themselves vegetarian, global meat output has risen fivefold since 1961, US plant-based meat sales fell for another year in 2025, and voters in Switzerland, Denver and Sonoma County rejected measures to ban factory farming or slaughterhouses. Welfare organizations such as the RSPCA, Humane World for Animals and The Humane League press for cage, crate and fast-growth bans and point to cage-free pledges that companies have largely kept, while Compassion in World Farming, Food & Water Watch and Senator Cory Booker want the largest operations phased out in favor of smaller, pasture-based farming. Pork producers and the American Farm Bureau Federation, joined by the American Veterinary Medical Association, argue that state housing mandates raise food prices without proven welfare gains and that standards should rest on veterinary science. Alternative-protein advocates such as the Good Food Institute argue that only cheaper, tastier substitutes will move billions of eaters. Animal rights groups such as PETA and Animal Rising want animal farming to end, and among them a strand associated with the legal scholar Gary Francione holds that welfare reforms make people more comfortable using animals and should not be pursued at all.

## Questions

### 1. Do welfare reforms reduce suffering, or entrench the use of animals?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real relief / Entrenches use
Why contested: Almost everyone agrees that a cage-free barn or a crate ban changes conditions for the animals inside. The disagreement is over the net effect over time: whether the relief is large enough to matter for animals that are still confined and slaughtered young, and whether reforms make the public, and the industry, more comfortable with farming animals at all. The evidence is indirect. Reforms have been enacted and largely kept, while the number of animals farmed has kept rising, and no study has isolated what the reform era did to consumption.
Real relief: Those who back reforms argue they have already changed the lives of hundreds of millions of animals. The cage-free share of US hens rose from about 11% in 2014 to 45% in 2025 (Our World in Data), and the Open Wing Alliance reports that 92% of corporate cage-free pledges due by 2024 were fulfilled. Welfare science supports the direction: the European Food Safety Authority recommended in 2023 that hens be kept out of cages and that broiler growth be capped, and a 2021 meta-analysis of 6,040 commercial flocks holding 176 million hens in 16 countries found that mortality in cage-free systems is not inherently higher than in cages once producers gain experience with them (Schuck-Paim et al., Scientific Reports, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7862694/). Reformers add that the alternative to reform is not abolition but decades more of the status quo for tens of billions of animals a year, that every reform makes the cheapest and cruelest methods more expensive, and that the farm lobby’s own effort to overturn Proposition 12 shows reforms bite.
Entrenches use: Those who reject reform as a strategy argue that it treats the symptom and protects the cause. Gary Francione writes that “as a practical matter, animal welfare regulation simply does not work,” because while animals remain property such laws mainly make society more comfortable exploiting them (Rutgers University, 2010, https://www.rutgers.edu/news/rutgers-newark-law-school-professor-gary-francione-argues-abolitionist-position-new-book-about). PETA opposed California’s 2018 initiative on the ground that it would “ingrain the farming practice of giving hens a minuscule amount of space for years to come” (PETA, https://www.peta.org/blog/why-we-oppose-californias-farmed-animal-initiative-and-you-should-too/). On this view the numbers tell the story: the world slaughtered about 40 billion chickens in 2000 and 78.5 billion in 2024, through the whole era of corporate campaigns, and a “humanely raised” label invites people who were uneasy to keep eating animal products with a clear conscience.

### 2. Can animals raised at industrial scale have lives worth living?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Worth living / Net suffering
Why contested: The standard most welfare science and policy use, from the former UK Farm Animal Welfare Council, asks whether an animal has a life worth living, which means weighing its pain, frustration and fear against its comfort and positive experiences. Those states have to be inferred from health, behavior and physiology. Scientists broadly agree on some indicators, such as lameness, injuries and the inability to perform strongly motivated behaviors, but weigh them differently, and conditions vary enormously between species, systems and individual farms.
Worth living: Producers and many farm veterinarians argue that modern indoor housing protects animals from predators, weather, parasites and many diseases, that veterinary oversight and breeding have cut mortality and disease, and that good stockmanship matters more to welfare than the type of building. The American Veterinary Medical Association holds that “no one husbandry style is appropriate for all circumstances” (AVMA letter, 20 April 2026, https://nppc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/AVMA-House-Ag.-Farm-Bill-Prop-12-LTC-.pdf), and the RSPCA’s own certification scheme states that “animals can be extremely well cared for on larger farms” (RSPCA Assured, https://www.rspcaassured.org.uk/blog/large-scale-farming). Temple Grandin, whose livestock-handling designs are widely used in North American meat plants, argues that the duty owed to farm animals is “a decent life,” and that welfare problems can be corrected rather than requiring the end of the system.
Net suffering: Critics argue that for the most numerous animals, the answer is plainly no. Broilers have been bred to grow more than 400% faster than in 1957 (Zuidhof et al., 2014), and in a survey of 51,000 UK birds at about 40 days old, 27.6% walked poorly and 3.3% could barely walk (Knowles et al., 2008); they are slaughtered at a few weeks of age. Caged hens cannot dust-bathe, forage or nest properly, and sows in crates cannot turn around for weeks at a time. On this view a life of chronic pain and frustration followed by slaughter is a net harm, which is why some advocates argue that the most valuable welfare change is for fewer such animals to be born at all, and why the RSPCA, which certifies higher-welfare farms, says “we need to farm fewer animals and farm them better” (RSPCA, https://www.rspca.org.uk/whatwedo/food-and-farming/problems).

### 3. Will alternative proteins displace a large share of animal products?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Will displace / Stays niche
Why contested: The answer depends on whether plant-based, fermented and cultivated products can match animal products on price, taste and convenience, on how fast production costs fall at scale, and on whether governments help, hinder or ban them. The recent market data are poor, but the technologies are young, and both sides can read the same numbers as a trough or a ceiling.
Will displace: Alternative-protein advocates argue that diet persuasion has failed and price competition will not. Bruce Friedrich of the Good Food Institute says “the vast majority of people eat meat because they like meat, not because of how it’s produced,” and that “decades of diet change advocacy have not led to decreased meat consumption” (Green Queen interview, February 2026, https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/bruce-friedrich-meat-book-gfi-alternative-protein-policy-interview/); if alternatives deliver the same experience at the same or lower price, most consumers will be open to them. They point out that global retail sales of plant-based meat and seafood have tripled since 2015, to an estimated $6.6 billion in 2025 (GFI, https://gfi.org/pb-summary-2026), that fermentation and cultivated meat are at the stage of cost decline that solar power once was, and that conventional meat benefits from decades of public research and subsidy. Bill Gates has said “all rich countries should move to 100% synthetic beef” (MIT Technology Review, 2021, https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/02/14/1018296/bill-gates-climate-change-beef-trees-microsoft/).
Stays niche: Skeptics point to the market’s verdict. US retail sales of plant-based meat fell 10% in 2025 to about $1.0 billion, or 1.4% of packaged meat sales, and household penetration has fallen since 2021 (GFI); investment fell from a record $5 billion in 2021 to $881 million in 2025, with only $74 million for cultivated meat (GFI); and the National Agricultural Law Center lists seven US states that have banned cultivated meat. They argue that animal products are nutrient-dense, culturally central and cheap at scale, that many consumers distrust highly processed substitutes, and that the OECD and FAO still project meat, dairy and egg output to rise 17% by 2034. On this view alternatives will be a useful option, not a replacement, and reform of animal farming cannot wait for them.

### 4. Is it wrong to raise and kill animals for food if they live well?
Tag: value
Poles: Humane use acceptable / Killing is wrong
Why contested: This is a question about what we owe animals, not about conditions on farms. It turns on whether death is itself a harm to an animal that does not anticipate it, whether a good life that would not otherwise have existed is a benefit to the animal, and whether using a sentient being as a resource is wrong regardless of treatment. The broader question of animals’ moral status has its own page; here it matters because it divides the reform and abolition camps even if every factual dispute were settled.
Humane use acceptable: Those who hold this view argue that the wrong in factory farming lies in the suffering, not in the use. Temple Grandin writes that killing animals for food is ethical if they have what the Farm Animal Welfare Council calls “a life worth living” (Grandin, “Eating Meat is Ethical,” https://u.osu.edu/beef/?p=933). Farm animals exist only because people raise them, and a well-lived life with a quick death is, on this view, a good exchange; mixed farming with animals also cycles nutrients, uses grassland that cannot grow crops, and sustains cultures and livelihoods. The RSPCA, which wants fewer animals farmed and all of them farmed better, starts from the principle that “every farmed animal deserves a good life” (RSPCA, https://www.rspca.org.uk/whatwedo/food-and-farming/problems).
Killing is wrong: Animal rights advocates argue that a being with interests of its own has a right not to be used as a resource, however kindly. Francione argues that “we cannot justify using animals as human resources” however “humane” the treatment (Rutgers, 2010), and PETA’s founding principle is that animals are “not ours to experiment on, eat, wear, use for entertainment, or abuse in any other way” (PETA, https://www.peta.org/about-peta/). Death, on this view, deprives an animal of every good its future would have held, and farm animals are killed young: broilers at a few weeks and pigs at a fraction of their natural lifespan. Even those who would accept humane use in principle note that it describes a small fraction of the animal products people actually eat.

### 5. Should welfare standards be set by law, or left to producers, veterinarians and buyers?
Tag: value
Poles: Binding law / Market and vets
Why contested: The choice weighs democratic and collective decisions against flexibility, expertise and consumer freedom. Surveys find strong stated support for better welfare, but people mostly buy on price, which each side reads as support for its own approach.
Binding law: Those who favor mandates argue that welfare is a collective-action problem. Shoppers cannot see how animals were raised, labels are confusing, and a producer who spends more on welfare is undercut by one who does not, so only a rule that applies to everyone works. Majorities want it: 84% of EU respondents say farmed animal welfare should be better protected (Eurobarometer 2023), and 62.7% of Californians voted for Proposition 12. Corporate pledges help, but they are voluntary and can slip; laws hold.
Market and vets: Those who favor flexibility argue that housing rules written into statutes or ballot measures freeze one design in place regardless of disease risk, climate or new evidence. The American Veterinary Medical Association wrote in April 2026 that “the requirements in Prop 12 are not scientifically based and have not been objectively demonstrated to reliably and consistently improve animal welfare,” and that welfare regulations “should be based upon scientific evidence and the professional judgement of veterinarians” (AVMA, https://nppc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/AVMA-House-Ag.-Farm-Bill-Prop-12-LTC-.pdf). Buyers who want higher welfare can choose certified products; in the same Eurobarometer, 60% said they would pay more for welfare-friendly food but only 26% would pay up to 5% more, which suggests mandates impose on everyone a premium most people would not choose.

### 6. Are the costs of higher welfare standards worth paying?
Tag: value
Poles: Worth the cost / Too costly
Why contested: Higher standards cost money: more space, slower-growing breeds and new barns. How much they raise retail prices is an empirical question that early evidence is starting to answer; whether the gain to animals justifies the cost, and who should bear it, is a value choice.
Worth the cost: Those who favor higher standards argue that the cost per serving is small relative to the suffering prevented, and that cheap meat already carries costs that its price does not show, from water pollution to drug-resistant infections linked to the roughly 100,000 tonnes of antimicrobials used in food animals each year (Mulchandani et al., 2023). The cost can be shared: Italy’s 2026 budget law created a public fund for farms converting to cage-free systems (Humane World for Animals, https://www.humaneworld.org/en/practical-support-higher-welfare-farming), and long transitions let producers replace barns at the end of their life. Some reformers, including the RSPCA, see lower consumption at higher prices as part of the goal rather than a side effect.
Too costly: Those who oppose mandates point to measured effects. Economists at North Dakota State University and USDA estimate that Proposition 12 raised California prices for covered pork by about 20% and cut consumption by about 15% (Lwin et al., 2025), and the National Pork Producers Council cites estimates that compliant barns cost 25% to 40% more per sow. Food costs weigh most on low-income households, smaller farms are least able to finance new housing, and higher domestic standards can shift production to exporters with weaker rules unless imports are covered too.

### 7. Should a state or trading bloc set welfare standards for food produced elsewhere?
Tag: value
Poles: Sales standards fair / Producers’ own rules
Why contested: Proposition 12 applies to pork, veal and eggs sold in California wherever the animals were raised, and the US Supreme Court let it stand in 2023 in National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-468_5if6.pdf). The House farm bill passed in April 2026 would override it, and the EU’s planned hen rules are reported to include equivalent requirements for imports. The question is whether buyers’ standards may reach producers who never voted for them.
Sales standards fair: Supporters argue that every market sets standards for what is sold in it, from food safety to product labeling, and that a rule applying only to local farms would simply hand the market to lower-welfare imports. Eurobarometer found 84% of EU respondents want EU welfare rules applied to imports or imports labeled by their standards. To them, a federal override would strip voters in at least 15 states of laws they passed.
Producers’ own rules: Opponents argue that one state should not dictate how farmers in another state raise animals. The National Pork Producers Council and American Farm Bureau Federation warn that Proposition 12 “sets the stage for an unworkable 50-state patchwork of laws” (NPPC, 27 April 2026, https://nppc.org/news/millions-strong-farmer-coalition-urges-prop-12-relief-in-farm-bill/), and the AVMA argues that “allowing one state to dictate husbandry requirements to other states” interferes with interstate commerce. Exporting countries make the same argument against EU import standards, calling them protectionism dressed as welfare.

### 8. Do higher welfare standards conflict with environmental goals?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real trade-off / Goals align
Why contested: Intensification has made each kilogram of chicken, pork and eggs cheaper in feed, land and emissions, and welfare reforms often undo some of that efficiency. Whether that matters depends on whether reforms also reduce how much is produced and eaten, which is the question the land sparing versus sharing debate takes up in general.
Real trade-off: Those who see a trade-off note that the modern broiler needs about half the feed per unit of gain of a 1957 bird (Zuidhof et al., 2014), so slower-growing breeds, lower stocking densities and outdoor access mean more feed, more cropland and often more emissions per kilogram. Producers and some environmental analysts argue that if demand holds steady, higher-welfare chicken and eggs push farming onto more land.
Goals align: Those who see the goals aligning argue that the efficiency comparison assumes the same amount of meat is eaten, while higher standards raise prices and lower consumption, as Proposition 12 did for pork in California. They add that concentrated operations create local pollution problems, from manure lagoons to water contamination, that per-kilogram averages hide; Food & Water Watch campaigns against factory farm waste on those grounds. Compassion in World Farming and the RSPCA call for fewer animals farmed better, which they argue serves animals, climate and nature together.

## Positions

### 1. Raise welfare standards within animal farming
Adherents:
- RSPCA (institution): “We believe we need to farm fewer animals and farm them better”; calls for an end to cages in UK farming and asks shoppers to choose higher-welfare products. https://www.rspca.org.uk/whatwedo/food-and-farming/problems
- The Humane League (institution): mission “to end the abuse of animals raised for food,” pursued by pressing companies to end their worst practices and lawmakers to make those standards apply industry-wide. https://impact.thehumaneleague.org/
- Humane World for Animals, formerly the Humane Society of the United States (institution): works with producers to move to cage-free housing for hens and group housing for sows, and backs laws ending gestation crates, battery cages and veal crates. https://www.humaneworld.org/en/practical-support-higher-welfare-farming
- Temple Grandin, animal scientist (person): “It is our duty to provide the animals that we raise for food with a decent life.” https://u.osu.edu/beef/?p=933
Case: The cruelty in modern animal farming is concentrated in a handful of practices that science has identified and that can be ended now: cages that stop hens from nesting, perching or dust-bathing; crates in which sows cannot turn around; and broiler breeds that grow so fast many can barely walk. Ending those practices does not require persuading the world to stop eating meat, which has never happened, or waiting for substitutes that may not arrive. It requires clear, enforceable standards, won company by company and law by law, and long enough transitions for farmers to rebuild. Shoppers alone cannot fix this, because they cannot see inside barns and the producer who does better is undercut by the one who does not; so the standard has to apply to everyone. Where the standards are high, farming continues and animals live better lives.
Best evidence: Reform has a record. The cage-free share of US hens rose from about 11% in 2014 to 45% in 2025, Germany, Austria, Luxembourg and Sweden have all but eliminated cages (Our World in Data), and the Open Wing Alliance reports that 92% of corporate cage-free pledges due by 2024 were kept. The science points the same way: the European Food Safety Authority recommended in 2023 that hens be housed out of cages and that broiler growth be capped at 50 grams a day. The public agrees: 84% of EU respondents want farmed animals better protected (Eurobarometer 2023), and California voters passed Proposition 12 with 62.7%. Governments are moving: England’s December 2025 strategy commits to move away from colony cages and farrowing crates, and the European Commission has committed to cage proposals.
Answer to objections: To producers who say mandates raise costs without proven benefit, reformers reply that the welfare science on cages, crates and fast growth is now as settled as such science gets, that transitions can be phased over a decade and supported with public money, as Italy did in 2026, and that a modest price rise is a fair price for animals that can turn around. To abolitionists who say reform entrenches use, they reply that the billions of animals alive now cannot wait for a vegan world, that every reform makes the cheapest cruelty more expensive, and that the industry’s fight against Proposition 12 shows how much reform bites. To alternative-protein advocates, they reply that the two are complementary: higher standards raise the price of the cheapest animal products, which helps substitutes compete.
Variation: The camp divides on the end state. For the RSPCA and Grandin, well-run animal farming is a legitimate goal; for many activists in groups like The Humane League, reform is a step toward fewer animals or none, and the RSPCA itself pairs better farming with fewer animals. It divides on instrument, between corporate campaigns (The Humane League, the Open Wing Alliance) and legislation (Humane World for Animals, the RSPCA), and on scope, as fish and shrimp welfare emerge as new campaigns. It also divides on scale: the RSPCA’s certification scheme holds that “animals can be extremely well cared for on larger farms” (https://www.rspcaassured.org.uk/blog/large-scale-farming), while others in the camp, closer to camp 5, see large operations themselves as the problem.

### 2. Keep intensive farming, guided by veterinary science and markets
Adherents:
- National Pork Producers Council (institution): opposes Proposition 12 as “one state’s arbitrary law – rather than actual animal care grounded in veterinary science” and seeks a federal override in the farm bill. https://fixprop12.com/
- American Veterinary Medical Association (institution): “The requirements in Prop 12 are not scientifically based and have not been objectively demonstrated to reliably and consistently improve animal welfare.” https://nppc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/AVMA-House-Ag.-Farm-Bill-Prop-12-LTC-.pdf
- American Farm Bureau Federation (institution): co-led, with NPPC, a coalition warning that Proposition 12 “sets the stage for an unworkable 50-state patchwork of laws.” https://nppc.org/news/millions-strong-farmer-coalition-urges-prop-12-relief-in-farm-bill/
Case: Modern animal agriculture is one of the great achievements of the last century: it turned meat, milk and eggs from luxuries into affordable everyday foods, and it did so while cutting the feed, land and emissions needed for each kilogram. Indoor housing, designed with veterinarians, protects animals from predators, weather and disease; good welfare depends on stockmanship, health and management, not on square footage written into a ballot measure by people who have never run a barn. Farmers care for their animals because their livelihoods depend on it, and they improve practices as science advances and as customers ask. When states impose rigid rules, and impose them on farmers in other states who never voted for them, prices rise, consumption falls, and smaller farms that cannot afford new barns close, which drives the consolidation critics say they oppose. Standards should be national, science-based and set with veterinarians, and shoppers who want something different should be free to buy it. The framing of “reform versus abolition” assumes the current system is a cruelty to be reduced or ended; this camp rejects that premise.
Best evidence: The measured effect of Proposition 12 is a roughly 20% rise in California prices for covered pork and a 15% fall in consumption (Lwin et al., North Dakota State University and USDA, 2025), and NPPC cites estimates that compliant barns cost 25% to 40% more per sow. The AVMA, speaking for more than 111,000 veterinarians, opposes the law. Efficiency is real: a modern broiler needs about half the feed per unit of gain of a 1957 bird (Zuidhof et al., 2014), which matters as the OECD and FAO project meat, dairy and egg output to grow 17% by 2034. Voters, when asked to end factory farming or slaughter outright, have said no: 63% in Switzerland in 2022, about 64% in Denver and 85% in Sonoma County in 2024. And stated preferences are not purchases: only 26% of EU respondents would pay as much as 5% more for welfare-friendly food (Eurobarometer 2023).
Answer to objections: To welfare reformers, the camp replies that it supports welfare and that producers have moved to cage-free housing where customers pay for it; what it opposes is mandates that lock in one housing design whatever the disease risk, and it notes that cage-free aviaries bring their own problems, from bone fractures to pecking injuries. To abolitionists and activists, it replies that the great majority of people choose to eat animal products, only 4% of Americans are vegetarian (Gallup 2023), and campaigns that present themselves as welfare reform are often, as industry groups see it, steps toward ending animal agriculture. To alternative-protein advocates, it replies that substitutes are welcome to compete, honestly labeled, but have not won customers on their own merits.
Variation: Members differ on how far to accommodate change: egg producers have largely accepted a voluntary shift to cage-free housing where buyers ask for it, while pork producers fight sow-housing mandates. They differ on alternative proteins: several farm-state legislatures have banned cultivated meat outright, while other members prefer labeling rules and open competition. And in developing countries the same argument takes the form of a case for intensifying poultry and pig production to supply affordable protein to fast-growing populations.

### 3. Replace animal products with alternative proteins
Adherents:
- Good Food Institute (institution): aims to make alternative proteins “as delicious and affordable as conventional meat,” so that they can “compete on taste and price.” https://gfi.org/about/
- Bruce Friedrich, president of the Good Food Institute (person): “The vast majority of people eat meat because they like meat, not because of how it’s produced.” https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/bruce-friedrich-meat-book-gfi-alternative-protein-policy-interview/
- Bill Gates, investor and philanthropist (person): “I do think all rich countries should move to 100% synthetic beef.” https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/02/14/1018296/bill-gates-climate-change-beef-trees-microsoft/
Case: People choose food for taste, price and convenience. Decades of moral and health appeals have not lowered meat consumption, and welfare reforms, however worthwhile, improve conditions for a fraction of animals while numbers keep rising. The way out is to change the product, not the eater: make meat from plants, from fermentation and from cultivated cells that tastes as good and costs the same or less, and most people will switch without being asked to sacrifice anything. That requires what every food technology has needed: public research funding, open science, fair regulatory approval and an end to bans and label restrictions designed to protect incumbents. It ends factory farming by making it obsolete rather than by winning a political fight against it that reformers and abolitionists keep losing at the ballot box.
Best evidence: The persuasion record is flat: 4% of Americans call themselves vegetarian, below the 6% of 1999 (Gallup), and global meat production has risen more than fivefold since 1961 (FAO). The scale of farming outruns any reform agenda: about 86 billion land animals and an estimated 130 billion farmed fish are killed each year. Technology can move fast once costs fall: global retail sales of plant-based meat and seafood have tripled since 2015, to an estimated $6.6 billion in 2025 (GFI, citing Euromonitor), and GFI reports that China, Denmark, Germany, Portugal and Spain now prioritize plant-based foods in their national food strategies, even as the EU has passed restrictions on meat-related names for plant-based products (GFI, 2026 State of the Industry, https://gfi.org/pb-summary-2026).
Answer to objections: To skeptics who cite falling US sales and investment, the camp replies that the first generation of products did not reach price or taste parity and that the slump reflects that, not a ceiling; the remedy is public research and scale, as with solar panels and batteries, and the spread of state bans on cultivated meat shows that incumbents fear the competition. To reformers, it replies that it supports better welfare but that reforms alone cannot keep pace with rising demand. To abolitionists, it replies that motive matters less than outcome: animals spared because a cheaper product won are spared all the same.
Variation: Advocates differ on which technology will matter most: plant-based products now, fermentation for proteins and fats, or cultivated meat later. Some, like Gates, are motivated chiefly by climate; others, like GFI, by animal welfare, climate and food security together. Some companies once set explicit deadlines for replacing animal agriculture; the camp now talks more often of diversifying the protein supply alongside conventional meat.

### 4. End the use of animals for food
Adherents:
- PETA (institution): founding principle that animals are “not ours to experiment on, eat, wear, use for entertainment, or abuse in any other way.” https://www.peta.org/about-peta/
- Animal Rising (institution): campaigns for a transition to “a sustainable and just plant-based food system,” supporting farming and fishing communities through it. https://www.animalrising.org/about
- Gary Francione, legal scholar (person): “we cannot justify using animals as human resources,” however “humane” the treatment, and proposes ethical veganism. https://www.rutgers.edu/news/rutgers-newark-law-school-professor-gary-francione-argues-abolitionist-position-new-book-about
Case: The wrong in animal agriculture is not only how animals are kept but that they are bred, used and killed for products people do not need. Pigs, cows and chickens are sentient beings with interests of their own, and a being with interests should not be treated as a resource, however large its cage. “Humane” farming is a contradiction at any scale and a fantasy at the scale of tens of billions of animals; even higher-welfare animals are killed young. The task is a transition to a plant-based food system, through veganism as a personal baseline, through campaigns that shift institutions and public opinion, and through a just transition for the farmers and workers whose livelihoods depend on the current system.
Best evidence: The scale of killing has grown through the whole era of welfare reform, from about 40 billion chickens in 2000 to 78.5 billion in 2024 (FAO), plus an estimated 86 to 180 billion farmed fish (Fishcount). A large share of people already feel the tension: in a 2017 survey, 69.6% of US adults agreed they had some discomfort with how animals are used in the food industry (Sentience Institute). Societies do end practices once thought normal: in November 2026, about two-thirds of Denver voters and 73% of Washington, D.C. voters banned the production and sale of foie gras outright.
Answer to objections: To those who say people will never give up animal products, the camp replies that moral progress has always looked impossible before it happened, and that plant-based options make the change easier each year. To welfare reformers, it replies that reducing suffering is good but presenting a better cage as humane misleads the public and delays the real goal. To farmers and farm communities, it replies that the camp’s argument is with the system, not with them, and that a transition should include support for farmers to move to plant crops and other work.
Variation: The camp is divided on welfare reform itself. Francione’s abolitionist approach holds that “as a practical matter, animal welfare regulation simply does not work” and that advocates should promote veganism rather than reforms. PETA supports some reforms but opposed California’s 2018 initiative as too weak, arguing it would “ingrain” small cage-free spaces for hens (PETA, https://www.peta.org/blog/why-we-oppose-californias-farmed-animal-initiative-and-you-should-too/). Others back practice bans and ballot measures as steps toward abolition, and groups like Animal Rising use civil disobedience and campaigns for fully plant-based catering in public institutions.

### 5. Replace factory farms with smaller, pasture-based farms
Adherents:
- Compassion in World Farming (institution): mission to end factory farming, with a target of 2040, through a shift to regenerative, nature-friendly farming and lower consumption of animal products (strategic plan 2026–2030). https://ciwf.org.uk/about-us/strategic-plan
- Food & Water Watch (institution): plans in 2026 to “push for statewide halts on all new factory farms” in New York and to rein in factory farm pollution in other states. https://www.foodandwaterwatch.org/news/2026/01/20/top-priorities-food-water-climate-2026/
- Cory Booker, US senator (person): sponsor of the Farm System Reform Act, which would place “an immediate moratorium on new and expanding large CAFOs” and phase out the largest by 2040; “We must immediately begin to transition to a more sustainable and humane system.” https://www.booker.senate.gov/news/press/booker-reintroduces-bill-to-reform-farm-system-with-expanded-support-from-farm-labor-environment-public-health-faith-based-and-animal-welfare-groups
- Family Farm Action (institution): its president, Joe Maxwell, said the Farm System Reform Act “tackles the root of our food system issues: consolidated corporate power” (same release).
Case: The problem is not a few bad practices but the industrial model itself: animals concentrated by the tens of thousands in buildings owned or controlled by a handful of corporations, bred for maximum output, fed imported grain, and producing more manure than nearby land can absorb. That model harms the animals, the workers, the water and the independent farmers it has driven out of business, and adjusting cage sizes inside it leaves its incentives untouched. The answer is to stop new large operations, phase out the largest, buy out the farmers who want to leave, and rebuild a farm economy of smaller, mixed and pasture-based farms where animals can behave naturally and help restore soil. People would eat less meat and pay more for it, and the meat would be better for animals, for farmers and for the land.
Best evidence: Industrial concentration is near total in the United States: the Sentience Institute estimates that 99% of US farmed animals live on factory farms. Intensive systems depend on antibiotics, roughly 100,000 tonnes a year in food animals worldwide (Mulchandani et al., 2023). There is real public support for this direction, short of a majority: 37% of Swiss voters backed writing an end to factory farming into the constitution in 2022 (swissinfo), and the Farm System Reform Act drew support from farm, labor, environmental, public-health and animal welfare groups (Booker, 2021).
Answer to objections: To producers who say intensive systems use less land and feed per kilogram, the camp replies that efficiency measured per kilogram ignores pollution, public-health risks and the farmers driven out, and that the goal is to produce and eat less. To welfare reformers who say good care does not depend on farm size, it replies that the economics of scale, not the intentions of individual farmers, set the conditions. To abolitionists, it replies that well-managed grazing animals can have good lives and benefit the land. To alternative-protein advocates, it replies that replacing livestock with products from a few technology companies would repeat the concentration problem in another form.
Variation: In Europe the camp is led by animal welfare groups under the banner of “less and better” meat; in the United States it is a coalition of environmental, anti-monopoly and family-farm groups, for whom animal welfare is one concern among several. Members differ on whether the goal is a legal phase-out of large operations (Booker, Food & Water Watch) or a shift driven by policy incentives, public procurement and diet change (Compassion in World Farming). The Compassion in World Farming page above was confirmed through search; direct fetches were rate-limited on 23 November 2026.

## Crux

Can animals be farmed at anything like today’s scale in a way that gives them lives worth living, at a price people will pay, and if so, is that enough?

Evidence could settle much of the first half. Welfare science can measure, with growing precision, how animals fare in cages, crates, barns, aviaries and pastures: lameness, injury, disease, mortality, and the behaviors animals are strongly motivated to perform. Economics can measure what higher standards cost and how prices and consumption respond, as the first studies of Proposition 12 have begun to do. Markets will show whether alternative proteins reach price and taste parity, and how many people switch when they do. If higher-welfare systems turn out to give most animals good lives at a modest cost, the case for reform and for keeping farming strengthens; if good welfare proves possible only at a scale and price far from today’s, the cases for replacement, for smaller pasture-based farming and for ending animal farming strengthen; if alternatives win on price, much of the argument becomes moot.

No data can close the second half. Whether a good life followed by an early death is a fair exchange or a wrong done to the animal; how much weight the suffering of a chicken or a pig should carry against food prices, farm livelihoods and the freedom to eat as one chooses; and whether voters in one place may set the terms for producers elsewhere are choices about what matters. People who agree on every fact can still divide here, between those who hold that animal farming done well is legitimate, those who hold it should be left to producers and buyers, and those who hold that using animals for food is wrong however it is done.
