# Treating Aging as a Disease — research

Slug: treating-aging-as-disease
Scope: Classifying aging as a treatable condition, and whether radically extended life would be good.
Researched: 2026-11-26

## Overview

This topic is about two linked questions. The first is whether biological aging should be treated as a medical condition in its own right: named in disease classifications, targeted directly by drugs, and approved by regulators as something a medicine can treat, rather than treated only through the separate diseases that become common with age, such as heart disease, cancer, dementia and frailty. The second is whether it would be good, for people and for societies, if such medicine worked well enough to extend human life far beyond today’s limits, to 120, 150 or indefinitely. It covers the science of slowing aging (the “geroscience” idea that a few shared biological processes drive most age-related disease), the regulatory question of whether the US Food and Drug Administration and its counterparts should approve drugs for aging itself, the 2019 to 2022 fight over the World Health Organization’s disease classification, the ethics of radical life extension, and who would get such treatments. It deliberately leaves aside obesity drugs as a disease category (the obesity-drugs-medicalization topic) and the general trade-off between drug prices and innovation (drug-pricing-vs-innovation), touching each only where it bears on aging; it also leaves aside pensions and the fiscal effects of population aging, end-of-life choices such as assisted dying, and the consumer wellness and supplement market except where it bears on the case for regulation. The debate is global, but most of the money, the regulatory fight and the survey data are American.

The why-now event: on October 1, 2026, at the Aging Research and Drug Discovery meeting at Harvard, senior FDA officials said that aging and longevity will be one of the topics in the agency’s next Focus Areas of Regulatory Science, the document that sets its regulatory research priorities; the agency’s chief scientist, Steven Kozlowski, gave a tentative release target of fiscal year 2027. Jeffrey Siegel, who directs the FDA’s Office of Drug Evaluation Sciences, outlined two ways a sponsor might show a drug affects aging broadly: by accumulating evidence that it benefits several age-related conditions, or by showing that it slows the decline of intrinsic capacity, a composite of physical and mental capacities, in a defined population (Lifespan.io, October 2, 2026, https://lifespan.io/fda-leaders-name-longevity-a-priority-at-ardd/). No FDA official said aging is a disease, and no approval pathway for an aging indication exists; trade coverage described the announcement as a signal to staff, funders and investors rather than a change to review requirements (In Vivo, October 21, 2026, https://insights.citeline.com/in-vivo/innovation/the-fda-says-aging-is-on-its-agenda-now-it-wants-the-data-ZKADWCEASBDZ7A2GY5KQXRKQFI/). The announcement capped a year in which the question moved from advocacy toward government: on February 24, 2026, the federal Advanced Research Projects Agency for Health (ARPA-H) awarded up to $144 million over five years to seven teams in its PROSPR program, which aims to “target the biology of aging directly” (ARPA-H, https://arpa-h.gov/news-and-events/research-teams-add-more-healthy-years-americans-lives-they-age); on May 27, 2026, the Reagan-Udall Foundation for the FDA, with ARPA-H and XPRIZE, held a public meeting on regulatory paths for drugs that target aging, whose summary states that “there is no FDA-approved indication for aging itself and no validated aging biomarker or accepted regulatory endpoint” (Reagan-Udall Foundation, July 2026, https://reaganudall.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/073126_Affecting%20the%20Aging%20Trajectory%20Public%20Meeting%20Summary.pdf); and on August 11, 2026, XPRIZE named 20 finalists in its $101 million Healthspan prize, who must run trials from 2026 to 2029 aiming to restore muscle, cognitive and immune function by at least 10 years in adults ages 50 to 90 (XPRIZE, https://www.xprize.org/news/20-healthspan-finalist-teams-advance-in-the-race-to-extend-healthy-aging). In January 2026 the FDA accepted the safety section of the company Loyal’s application for LOY-002, a pill meant to extend the healthy lifespan of senior dogs, which the company says would be “the first FDA-approved drug for lifespan extension itself in any species” if approved; it expects to file the last section in 2027 (Loyal, January 13, 2026, https://loyal.com/posts/loy-002-tas-announcement).

The debate is live because the science, the money and the institutions have all moved at once, while the public has not. Drugs such as rapamycin extend lifespan in mice, a two-year human trial of calorie restriction slowed a blood-based measure of the pace of aging, and investors put $8.49 billion into longevity companies in 2024 by one industry count, yet no drug has shown that it slows aging in people, and an August 2026 survey found only 9 percent of Americans see aging as a medical condition that science should actively try to treat. The people in the debate are geroscientists and their funders, such as the American Federation for Aging Research, ARPA-H and the Saudi-funded Hevolution Foundation, who want regulators to accept slowing aging as a medical goal measured by healthy years; geriatricians, gerontologists and advocates for older people, such as the International Longevity Centre Brazil, who led the campaign that made the WHO withdraw “old age” as a diagnosis and who argue that calling aging a disease feeds ageism; life-extension advocates, from the philosopher Nick Bostrom and the gerontologist Aubrey de Grey to the entrepreneur Bryan Johnson’s Don’t Die movement, who regard aging as the largest cause of death to be defeated; and bioethicists in the tradition of the President’s Council on Bioethics and Leon Kass, who argue that a finite life is part of what makes a human life good. Regulators, notably the FDA, are the audience for much of the argument rather than a party to it.

### 1. Global life expectancy at birth

- Value: 73.3
- Unit: years
- Comparison rows (Our World in Data long-run series, period life expectancy at birth, world, years; built from Riley 2005, Zijdeman et al. 2015, the Human Mortality Database and UN WPP 2024): 1900 32.0; 1950 46.4; 2000 66.4; 2019 72.6; 2021 70.9; 2023 73.2. - Data year: 2024
- Source label: UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs, World Population Prospects 2024: Summary of Results
- Source URL: https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Files/WPP2024_Summary-of-Results.pdf
- Note: Globally, life expectancy at birth reached 73.3 years in 2024, up 8.4 years since 1995, and the UN projects about 77.4 years in 2054.
- Context: Life expectancy more than doubled in the twentieth century, from about 32 years worldwide in 1900 (Our World in Data, https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/life-expectancy). Period life expectancy is a snapshot of one year’s death rates, not a forecast for anyone born that year. Country gaps remain wide: in Our World in Data’s series, Japan was at 84.7 years in 2023, the United States at 79.3 and Nigeria at 54.5.

### 3. Share of adult disease burden from age-related diseases
- Label: Share of the global disease burden among adults caused by age-related diseases
- Value: 51.3
- Unit: percent of disability-adjusted life years among adults ages 25 and older
- Comparison rows: none
- Data year: 2017
- Source label: Chang, Skirbekk, Tyrovolas, Kassebaum and Dieleman, “Measuring population ageing: an analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2017,” The Lancet Public Health, 2019
- Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6472541/
- Note: The study classed 92 diseases as age-related and found they accounted for 51.3 percent of all the disease burden among adults worldwide in 2017.
- Context: The authors defined a disease as age-related when its incidence among adults rises quadratically with age, and measured burden in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), which add years lost to early death and years lived with disability; the figure is a share of burden, not of deaths. The age-related diseases with the most deaths and DALYs were ischemic heart disease, intracerebral hemorrhage and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Age-standardized rates of age-related disease fell over time across regions, and the onset varies widely: the study found that 76-year-olds in Japan and 46-year-olds in Papua New Guinea carry the same age-related burden as the average 65-year-old worldwide. Geroscientists cite figures like this to argue that aging is the common root of most chronic disease; the same variation by country is cited by those who stress that social conditions shape how people age.

### 8. Oldest verified human age
- Label: Longest verified human lifespan
- Value: 122
- Unit: years (122 years, 164 days)
- Comparison rows (Guinness World Records, years): Jeanne Calment of France, died 1997, 122; Ethel Caterham of England, oldest living person, turned 117 on August 21, 2026 (Guinness World Records news, August 2026)
- Data year: 1997 (record set); 2026 (oldest living person)
- Source label: Guinness World Records, Oldest person ever
- Source URL: https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/oldest-person
- Note: The oldest verified person, Jeanne Calment, was born on February 21, 1875 and died on August 4, 1997, aged 122 years and 164 days.
- Context: No verified person has come within several years of Calment’s age since her death. Demographers disagree about what this means. Either way, without slowing aging itself very few people reach these ages. ### 10. Lifespan extension from rapamycin in mice
- Label: Increase in mouse lifespan from rapamycin started late in life
- Value: 14
- Unit: percent increase in age at 90 percent mortality, female mice (9 percent in males)
- Comparison rows (Harrison et al. 2009, percent increase in age at 90 percent mortality): females 14; males 9
- Data year: 2009
- Source label: Harrison et al., “Rapamycin fed late in life extends lifespan in genetically heterogeneous mice,” Nature, 2009 (National Institute on Aging Interventions Testing Program)
- Source URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19587680/
- Note: Rapamycin given to mice starting at 600 days of age extended the age at which 90 percent had died by 14 percent in females and 9 percent in males.
- Context: The study, run at three independent sites in genetically varied mice to avoid results that hold only for one strain, was the first to show a drug extending lifespan in a mammal of both sexes; rapamycin also increased survival when started at 270 days. The authors noted it might work by postponing cancer, by slowing aging, or both. Rapamycin is an approved immunosuppressant in people, with side effects that make long-term use in healthy people uncertain. It is now being tested in companion dogs in the Dog Aging Project’s TRIAD trial (https://dogagingproject.org/). No drug has yet been shown to extend lifespan in humans.
- Visualization: ranked (females, males)

### 11. Human trial evidence on the pace of aging
- Label: Slowing in the pace of biological aging from two years of calorie restriction
- Value: 2–3
- Unit: percent slower pace of aging, by the DunedinPACE blood test
- Comparison rows: none
- Data year: 2023 (trial run over two years)
- Source label: Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health, “Calorie Restriction Slows Pace of Aging in Healthy Adults,” February 9, 2023, on Waziry et al., Nature Aging (CALERIE trial)
- Source URL: https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/news/calorie-restriction-slows-pace-aging-healthy-adults
- Note: In the CALERIE trial, two years of calorie restriction slowed the pace of aging by 2 to 3 percent as measured by the DunedinPACE DNA-methylation test.
- Context: The CALERIE trial randomized 220 healthy adults without obesity to a 25 percent calorie-restriction target or their normal diet for two years; the analysis was a post hoc study of stored blood samples (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37118425/). The effect appeared on DunedinPACE but not on other DNA-methylation “clocks” such as PhenoAge and GrimAge, and the authors described the effect sizes as small. Columbia’s release says a 2 to 3 percent slowing corresponds in other studies to a 10 to 15 percent lower mortality risk; that is an extrapolation, since the trial did not measure deaths. It is among the strongest randomized evidence that human aging biology can be changed at all, and it shows the regulatory problem: as of 2026 no aging clock is accepted by the FDA as a surrogate endpoint (Reagan-Udall Foundation, stat 13).
- Visualization: bar (one number)

### 12. Estimated economic value of slowing aging
- Label: Estimated value to the United States of slowing aging enough to add one year of life expectancy
- Value: 38
- Unit: trillion US dollars
- Comparison rows (Scott, Ellison and Sinclair 2021, trillion US dollars): slowdown in aging adding 1 year of life expectancy 38; adding 10 years 367
- Data year: 2021
- Source label: Scott, Ellison and Sinclair, “The economic value of targeting aging,” Nature Aging, 2021
- Source URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37117804/
- Note: The study estimated that slowing aging enough to raise US life expectancy by one year would be worth $38 trillion, and by ten years $367 trillion.
- Context: The estimate uses the economists’ “value of a statistical life” approach, which prices extra years of life and health by what people are willing to pay for them; it is a measure of welfare, not of money that would flow into budgets. The authors found that improving health (compressing the years of illness) is worth more than further gains in life expectancy alone, and that targeting aging is worth more than eliminating any single disease, because gains in one disease raise the value of gains in the others. One of the authors, the Harvard geneticist David Sinclair, is among the best-known advocates of treating aging as a disease. The figure says nothing about whether such a slowdown is achievable or what it would cost.

## Questions

### 1. Is aging itself a disease, or the normal process within which diseases arise?
- Tag: value
- Poles: a disease to treat / a normal life stage
- Why contested: Whether something counts as a disease is partly a definition, and the definition decides what medicine may target, what regulators may approve and what insurers pay for. Aging has the features many definitions use (a mechanism, a progressive course, harm and, in animals, interventions that change it), but it happens to everyone, which most definitions of disease exclude. The question was fought out in the WHO’s eleventh International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11): a draft category labeled “old age” and an extension code for conditions “ageing-related” were added, then after a campaign by advocates for older people the label was changed to “ageing associated decline in intrinsic capacity,” and WHO now states that “ICD is not classifying ‘old age’ as a disease” (WHO, https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/old-age).
- A disease to treat: Those who hold that biological aging is a disease, including the researchers whose proposal produced the ICD-11 “ageing-related” code, argue that aging meets the same criteria as any other disease: it can be measured with frailty indices and biomarkers, it has known causal mechanisms such as cellular senescence and chronic inflammation, it responds to interventions in animals, and it causes most of the suffering and death in rich countries. Khaltourina and colleagues concluded that “aging fits the ICD-11 criteria and can be considered a disease” (Mechanisms of Ageing and Development, 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32251691/). That everyone gets it is no argument for leaving it alone, they say; before vaccines nearly everyone got measles. Calling aging normal has, in their view, kept funding low and treatments unapproved.
- A normal life stage: Those who hold that aging is not a disease, including geriatricians and advocates for older people who campaigned against the ICD-11 label, argue that the word “disease” should mark departures from normal human functioning, and that aging is the normal course of every human life. Kiran Rabheru, Julie Byles and Alexandre Kalache wrote that “Ageing is not a pathological process and is globally accepted as a normal human attribute, with longevity being a privilege that we all hope to enjoy,” and that “Old age is not a disease, but ageism is” (The Lancet Healthy Longevity, 2022, https://health.uconn.edu/pepper-center/wp-content/uploads/sites/272/2024/01/PIIS2666756822001027.pdf). People age at very different rates, so chronological age says little about any individual, and the label invites doctors to write off treatable problems as “just old age.” Many in this group support research into the biology of aging; they object to the disease label, not to the science.

### 2. Can drugs slow human aging enough to matter within the next few decades?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: yes, within decades / not yet shown
- Why contested: Lifespan can be extended in yeast, worms, flies and mice by genetic changes, calorie restriction and drugs such as rapamycin, but no intervention has been shown to slow aging in people, and the experiments that could show it take years and large numbers of participants. The best human evidence so far is indirect: a two-year calorie-restriction trial that slowed one blood measure of the pace of aging by 2 to 3 percent, observational studies of people taking metformin or newer diabetes drugs, and biomarker results from small trials.
- Yes, within decades: Those who expect success, including geroscientists such as Nir Barzilai and funders such as ARPA-H and the Hevolution Foundation, argue that the biology is now well mapped (researchers list twelve “hallmarks of aging,” from genomic instability to chronic inflammation, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36599349/), that the same pathways extend lifespan across very different species, and that several drugs already on the market for other uses appear to delay multiple diseases at once. The obstacle, in their view, is less the science than the absence of trials large enough to test it, which they attribute to the lack of a regulatory indication and of commercial incentive for cheap generic drugs.
- Not yet shown: Those who are skeptical, including many clinicians and the demographer S. Jay Olshansky in his reading of current trends, point out that most results come from short-lived laboratory animals whose aging differs from ours, that drugs which extend mouse lifespan often do so by delaying cancer, and that early human trials of senolytics and other anti-aging drugs have produced mixed or negative results. Biomarker “clocks” can move without any change in health. Until a randomized trial shows fewer diseases, less disability or longer survival, they argue, claims that aging can be slowed in people remain hypotheses, and a market selling them to consumers is running ahead of the evidence.

### 3. Should regulators approve drugs to treat aging itself?
- Tag: value
- Poles: create an aging indication / approve disease by disease
- Why contested: The FDA approves drugs for specific diseases or conditions. No approval pathway exists for an aging indication and no aging biomarker is accepted as a surrogate endpoint (Reagan-Udall Foundation, July 2026). In October 2026 FDA officials said aging will appear in the agency’s next regulatory science priorities, and outlined two possible routes: evidence across several age-related conditions, or slowing of measured decline in intrinsic capacity. Advocates want a dedicated pathway; others want the existing disease-based system kept.
- Create an aging indication: Those who favor an aging indication, including the Alliance for Longevity Initiatives, which calls for “clear FDA standards to streamline approval of safe, effective longevity medicines” (https://a4li.org/), and ARPA-H, whose PROSPR program funds work on endpoints regulators could accept, argue that the disease-by-disease system forces companies to wait until people are already ill, rewards drugs that treat one condition late rather than many conditions early, and leaves cheap drugs untested. A drug that delayed heart disease, dementia and cancer together would be the most valuable medicine ever made, yet there is no label under which it could be approved and sold as such.
- Approve disease by disease: Those who favor keeping the current system argue that drugs taken for decades by healthy people need a higher safety bar than drugs for the sick, and that an aging indication defined by biomarkers or composite scores would invite approvals on numbers that do not reflect real benefit. At the May 2026 Reagan-Udall meeting, the former FDA official Sandra Kweder argued that “well-tolerated” may be acceptable for disease-targeting drugs but is not enough for gerotherapeutics used chronically in generally healthy people, and the nutrition scientist David Allison argued that lifespan remains the most credible endpoint and warned against composite measures. The existing system, they note, already lets a drug prove benefit across several age-related conditions one indication at a time.

### 4. Would a much longer life be good for the person who lives it?
- Tag: value
- Poles: more life is better / finitude gives meaning
- Why contested: This is the oldest part of the argument, and no data can settle it. Surveys show most Americans would not want to live to 120 even with treatments (56 percent in 2013), but most also want to live past 80, and many say they would pause their biological age if they could.
- More life is better: Those who hold that longer life is good, including the philosopher Nick Bostrom and the life-extension movement, argue that people almost never want to die tomorrow, and that if each additional healthy year is worth having, there is no point at which the next one stops being worth it. Bostrom’s “Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant” concludes that “we have compelling moral reasons to get rid of human senescence” (Journal of Medical Ethics, 2005, https://nickbostrom.com/papers/the-fable-of-the-dragon-tyrant/). The fear of boredom assumes people would live the same life over and over, when longer lives would allow new careers, relationships and learning; the claim that death gives life meaning is, in this view, a consolation invented because death was unavoidable.
- Finitude gives meaning: Those who hold that a bounded life is better, including the President’s Council on Bioethics in its 2003 report Beyond Therapy and its chair Leon Kass, argue that the awareness of limited time is what makes people commit, aspire and act with urgency. The Council warned that a much longer life could become “a life of lesser engagements and weakened commitments” (https://sitearchives.georgetown.edu/kie-pcbe/reports/beyondtherapy/chapter4.html), and Kass wrote that “To number our days is the condition for making them count” (First Things, 2001, https://firstthings.com/lchaim-and-its-limits-why-not-immortality/). A life with a natural shape, from youth to old age, gives each stage its meaning; removing the end does not simply add more of the good parts.

### 5. Would much longer lives be good for society?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: society would adapt / society would stagnate
- Why contested: If aging were slowed for most people, populations would grow older and larger for a time, careers and pensions would have to change, and older generations would hold power and jobs for longer. How large these effects would be depends on how many people get treatments, on fertility, and on whether extra years are healthy, which is unknown. In 2013, 51 percent of Americans said radical life extension would be bad for society, and about two-thirds agreed longer lives would strain natural resources (Pew).
- Society would adapt: Those who expect adaptation, including geroscientists and economists such as Andrew Scott, argue that healthier older people work, care for others and pay taxes for longer, which is the main fiscal benefit; that population growth from slower aging would be modest because fertility, not mortality, drives it in rich countries; and that societies have absorbed a doubling of life expectancy since 1900 without the predicted catastrophes. Aubrey de Grey argues that fears about overpopulation, pensions or old people blocking progress each have a policy answer and are no reason to let people die.
- Society would stagnate: Those who expect harm, including the President’s Council on Bioethics, argue that social renewal depends on generations giving way: the Council warned that “the succession of generations could be obstructed by a glut of the able” and that people who feel less mortal may be less welcoming of children. Others point to resource use and to the concentration of wealth and power in long-lived elites. Even if the effects can be managed, they argue, the burden of proof should be on those who would change the basic rhythm of human generations.

### 6. Is there a fixed upper limit to the human lifespan?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: a fixed limit / no fixed limit
- Why contested: The verified record has stood at 122 years since 1997. Demographers disagree about whether death rates keep rising at the oldest ages, which would imply a ceiling near 115 to 125, or level off, which would imply that the record could be broken given enough very old people.
- A fixed limit: Those who see a limit, including the geneticist Jan Vijg and colleagues, found that survival gains decline after 100 and that the age at death of the world’s oldest person stopped rising in the 1990s, concluding that the maximum lifespan “is fixed and subject to natural constraints” (Nature, 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27706136/). Olshansky and colleagues found that life expectancy gains in the longest-lived countries have slowed sharply and that survival to 100 is unlikely to exceed 15 percent for women and 5 percent for men this century without a breakthrough in slowing aging.
- No fixed limit: Those who see no fixed limit, including the demographers Elisabetta Barbi, James Vaupel and Kenneth Wachter, found that the risk of death among Italians aged 105 and older stopped rising and stayed level, which they called “the best evidence to date for the existence of extreme-age mortality plateaus in humans” (Science, 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29954979/). Life-extension advocates add that any limit observed so far reflects unmodified human biology; a drug that slowed aging would move it, as calorie restriction and genetic changes move the maximum lifespan of animals.

### 7. Would treatments for aging widen inequality or spread to everyone?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: spread to all / deepen inequality
- Why contested: In the United States the richest 1 percent of men already live 14.6 years longer than the poorest 1 percent (Chetty et al., 2016). Early longevity products are expensive and sold privately, and about two-thirds of Americans said in 2013 that in practice only wealthy people would have access to life-extending treatments (Pew); in 2026 about two-thirds worried that such treatments may be available only to the extremely wealthy (National Geographic).
- Spread to all: Those who expect broad access argue that the history of medicine is one of expensive treatments becoming cheap: statins, antiretrovirals and now GLP-1 drugs fell sharply in price as patents expired and volumes grew. Several candidate drugs for aging, such as metformin and rapamycin, are already generic. A treatment that delayed many diseases at once would also save health systems money, giving governments a reason to provide it widely, and advocates such as the Alliance for Longevity Initiatives say they want next-generation treatments made available fairly.
- Deepen inequality: Those who expect inequality argue that the first effective treatments will be patented biologics or gene therapies priced for the rich, that the wealthy already gain years faster than the poor, and that extending the lives of the advantaged would compound their wealth and power across a longer span. Sixty-two percent of Americans in the 2026 National Geographic survey said governments should not fund longevity research until basic health care is universally guaranteed. In this view, the cheapest healthy years come from closing existing gaps, not from new drugs.

### 8. Does calling aging a disease help older people or feed ageism?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: helps older people / feeds ageism
- Why contested: Both sides claim to speak for older people. WHO estimated in 2021 that every second person in the world holds ageist attitudes, and campaigners cited this in persuading WHO to drop “old age” as a diagnosis. Advocates of the disease frame argue the opposite: that accepting decline as normal is itself the ageist position.
- Helps older people: Those who think the label helps argue that calling decline “normal aging” is what leads doctors to dismiss frailty, pain and memory loss as untreatable, and that a disease label would bring research money, clinical attention and insurance coverage, as it did for osteoporosis and sarcopenia once they were defined as conditions. Seeing aging as treatable, they say, treats older people as patients worth helping rather than as people past help.
- Feeds ageism: Those who think it harms, including the International Longevity Centre Brazil, whose campaign #VelhiceNãoÉDoença (“Old age is not a disease”) helped change the ICD-11 label, argue that a code for “old age” would let clinicians and death certificates record age itself as the problem, hiding the specific diseases that could be treated, and would reinforce the view of older people as defective. Rabheru, Byles and Kalache reported that the “old age” label “led to very serious real-world challenges for being used inappropriately and erroneously.” A frame built on function, such as intrinsic capacity, can guide care without labeling a life stage as pathology.

### 9. Should research funding shift from single diseases toward the biology of aging?
- Tag: value
- Poles: fund aging biology / fund specific diseases
- Why contested: Public research money is organized by disease. In the United States, Congress has directed much of the National Institute on Aging’s growth to Alzheimer’s disease, while research on the basic biology of aging receives a small share; the Alliance for Longevity Initiatives says less than 1 percent of the NIH budget goes to aging biology. Moving money would take it from patient communities that fought for it.
- Fund aging biology: Those who favor the shift, including the American Federation for Aging Research and the Alliance for Longevity Initiatives, argue that aging is the largest risk factor for nearly every major chronic disease, so curing one disease at a time yields little: a person saved from cancer at 75 soon faces heart disease or dementia. Scott, Ellison and Sinclair estimated that targeting aging is worth more than eliminating any single disease (Nature Aging, 2021). Eli Dourado and Joanne Peng, writing for the Federation of American Scientists, argued that NIA’s Alzheimer’s funds would do more good if they could be spent on aging biology (https://fas.org/publication/curing-alzheimer-s-by-investing-in-aging-research/).
- Fund specific diseases: Those who favor disease-targeted funding argue that specific diseases have measurable endpoints, proven treatments and patients waiting now, while aging biology has yet to produce a single approved therapy. Patient groups for Alzheimer’s, cancer and heart disease argue that their gains, from survival rates to new drugs, came from focused research. Others in this group, including many geriatricians, would put more money into care, prevention and the social conditions that make people age badly, which the evidence on income and life expectancy suggests can add years at lower cost.

## Positions

### 1. Slow aging to add healthy years
- Adherents: American Federation for Aging Research (institution; funder of geroscience and sponsor of the TAME trial, whose stated aims include “Delaying Disease by Targeting Aging”), https://www.afar.org/about-afar ; S. Jay Olshansky (demographer, University of Illinois Chicago), https://today.uic.edu/despite-medical-advances-life-expectancy-gains-are-slowing/ . Also ARPA-H, whose PROSPR program aims to “target the biology of aging directly” (https://arpa-h.gov/news-and-events/research-teams-add-more-healthy-years-americans-lives-they-age); Nir Barzilai of Albert Einstein College of Medicine, who leads TAME (NPR, https://www.nhpr.org/2024-04-22/a-cheap-drug-may-slow-down-aging-a-study-will-determine-if-it-works); the Hevolution Foundation; and the Alliance for Longevity Initiatives, which lobbies Congress for “clear FDA standards to streamline approval of safe, effective longevity medicines” (https://a4li.org/).
- Case in the camp’s voice: Aging is the single largest risk factor for heart disease, cancer, stroke, dementia and frailty, and the biology behind it, from cellular senescence to chronic inflammation, is now understood well enough to target. Medicine still fights these diseases one at a time, late, after damage is done; a person cured of one at 75 soon meets the next. If instead we slow the shared process underneath, we can delay many of them together and shrink the decade or more that people now spend sick at the end of life. The goal is health, not immortality: as Olshansky puts it, “We should now shift our focus to efforts that slow aging and extend healthspan.” Whether one calls aging a disease matters less than whether regulators will accept evidence that a drug delays several age-related conditions at once, or slows measured decline in function, as grounds for approval. That is what TAME was designed to test with metformin, a cheap generic no company will pay to study, and what ARPA-H and XPRIZE are now funding. Governments should put far more into the biology of aging, which the Alliance for Longevity Initiatives says receives less than 1 percent of the NIH budget, and the FDA should define endpoints that a healthspan drug could meet.
- Variation inside the camp: Members differ on the label. Some, such as the Alliance for Longevity Initiatives, prefer to call aging a “condition” or a risk factor and focus on a regulatory pathway; others in the field are comfortable calling it a disease. They also differ on the route to approval: through clusters of existing diseases (the TAME and FDA “several conditions” model), through intrinsic capacity and other functional measures, or through validated biomarkers. Olshansky is a leading skeptic of radical life extension; others in the camp are more open to large lifespan gains if they come with health.
- Best evidence: Rapamycin’s 9 to 14 percent extension of mouse lifespan started late in life; the CALERIE trial’s 2 to 3 percent slowing of the pace of aging in humans; the finding that age-related diseases make up 51.3 percent of adult disease burden; the exponential rise of death risk with age; WHO’s estimate of about 10.5 years lived in less than full health; Olshansky’s finding that life expectancy gains have slowed without a change in aging itself; and the estimate that slowing aging by one year of life expectancy is worth $38 trillion to the United States.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that no drug has been shown to slow human aging, and the field is running on hype): That is exactly the argument for doing the trials. The camp is not asking regulators to approve anything on faith; it is asking for endpoints that real trials can meet, at the same safety and efficacy standards as other medicines, and for public money to test cheap drugs no company will. Without those, consumers will keep buying unproven products from clinics and supplement sellers, and the question will never be settled.

### 2. Treat aging as a life stage, not a disease
- Adherents: International Longevity Centre Brazil (institution; its president Alexandre Kalache helped lead the #VelhiceNãoÉDoença campaign and co-wrote the account of the ICD-11 change), https://health.uconn.edu/pepper-center/wp-content/uploads/sites/272/2024/01/PIIS2666756822001027.pdf ; Kiran Rabheru and Julie Byles (co-authors of the same Lancet Healthy Longevity comment, 2022), same URL; James Appleby (chief executive, Gerontological Society of America), who at the May 2026 Reagan-Udall meeting described aging itself as a blessing, not a disease, https://reaganudall.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/073126_Affecting%20the%20Aging%20Trajectory%20Public%20Meeting%20Summary.pdf . Also the authors of the 2021 Lancet Healthy Longevity call “Not a disease: a global call for action urging revision of the ICD-11 classification of old age” (Banerjee, Mukhopadhyay, Rabheru, Ivbijaro and de Mendonça Lima, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36098014/), and the WHO, which states that “ICD is not classifying ‘old age’ as a disease” and runs the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing (https://www.who.int/initiatives/decade-of-healthy-ageing).
- Case in the camp’s voice: Growing old is not a malfunction. It is the normal course of every human life, and reaching old age is the great success of modern public health. People age at very different rates, so a label tied to age tells a doctor little about the person in front of them, and in practice a diagnosis of “old age” lets clinicians and death certificates name age as the problem instead of the heart failure, depression or infection that could be treated. In a world where WHO estimates half of people hold ageist attitudes, calling a life stage a disease tells older people that they themselves are the pathology. As Rabheru, Byles and Kalache wrote, “Old age is not a disease, but ageism is.” The right target is function: helping people keep their intrinsic capacity, their ability to move, think, see, hear and live independently, through prevention, good geriatric care, healthy environments and inclusion. Research into the biology of aging is welcome, but it should be measured by whether people live and function better, not by whether aging has been relabeled, and the largest gains in healthy years are still to be had by closing the gaps between rich and poor.
- Variation inside the camp: Some members, including researchers who developed the intrinsic capacity framework, work closely with geroscientists and accept drugs that slow functional decline, provided they are judged by function; they differ from camp 1 mainly on the label and on emphasis. Others put the priority on social policy, long-term care and anti-ageism and are wary of a pharmaceutical frame for later life altogether.
- Best evidence: The successful 2021 campaign that led WHO to replace “old age” in ICD-11 with “ageing associated decline in intrinsic capacity”; WHO’s estimate that every second person holds ageist attitudes; the wide variation in how people age, such as the Global Burden of Disease finding that 76-year-olds in Japan carry the same age-related burden as 46-year-olds in Papua New Guinea; the decline in age-standardized age-related disease over recent decades without any anti-aging drug; the 14.6-year gap in life expectancy between the richest and poorest American men; and the 2026 survey in which only 9 percent of Americans saw aging as a medical condition to be treated.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that calling decline “normal” is fatalism that leaves treatable suffering untreated): The camp does not accept decline; it refuses to label a stage of life as illness. Frailty, sarcopenia, dementia and loss of capacity are all diagnosed and treated, and ICD-11 now codes decline in intrinsic capacity precisely so that it can be tracked and addressed. What the camp rejects is the step from “older people have more disease” to “being old is a disease,” which history suggests leads to less care, not more.

### 3. Accept a natural human lifespan
- Adherents: President’s Council on Bioethics (institution; its 2003 report Beyond Therapy, chapter “Ageless Bodies”), https://sitearchives.georgetown.edu/kie-pcbe/reports/beyondtherapy/chapter4.html ; Ezekiel Emanuel (oncologist and bioethicist, University of Pennsylvania), who plans to forgo life-prolonging interventions after 75 and calls wellness and longevity “means to the larger end” (WBUR, June 30, 2026), https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/06/30/longevity-wellness-dementia-zeke-emanuel . Also Leon Kass, the Council’s chair, in “L’Chaim and Its Limits: Why Not Immortality?” (First Things, 2001, https://firstthings.com/lchaim-and-its-limits-why-not-immortality/).
- Case in the camp’s voice: Medicine should help people live well through a full human life and die well at its end; it should not aim to abolish that end. A life has a shape, from childhood through maturity to old age, and that shape gives each stage its meaning and its tasks. Knowing our time is limited is what moves us to commit, to love, to raise children and to make our days count: in Kass’s words, “To number our days is the condition for making them count.” The President’s Council warned that greatly extended lives could become “a life of lesser engagements and weakened commitments,” that “the succession of generations could be obstructed by a glut of the able,” and that “Children are one answer to mortality.” The wish to live a little longer at every moment does not show that an endless life would be good; it shows how hard it is to accept death, which is a reason for wisdom, not for a technology. Treating the diseases of old age is good medicine; making the defeat of aging a social goal, and spending public money and attention on it, mistakes what a good life is and feeds a culture of anxious self-optimization around diets, supplements and longevity plans, much of whose advice Emanuel dismisses outright.
- Variation inside the camp: Kass and the Council argue from the meaning of a finite life and the good of generational renewal; Emanuel argues from his own reading of what a good old age is and from the costs of prolonging decline, and his stance is a personal choice he does not propose to impose. Some religious thinkers in the camp ground it in the acceptance of mortality; others are secular. Most would welcome drugs that compress illness at the end of life without lengthening it; the disagreement is with the goal of radical extension.
- Best evidence: Pew’s 2013 finding that 56 percent of Americans would not want treatments to live to 120 and 51 percent thought they would be bad for society; the median ideal lifespan of 90 in 2013 and the average preferred age of 91 in 2025; the 2026 survey in which 64 percent would accept a shorter life free of decline; and the gap of about 10.5 years between total and healthy life expectancy, which they read as a warning that longer lives have so far meant longer illness as well as longer health.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that accepting death is a rationalization, and no one asks to die at any given moment): The camp distinguishes between the choices individuals make about their own care, which it does not propose to forbid, and the goals a society adopts and funds. Not wanting to die tomorrow is compatible with thinking a bounded life better than an unbounded one, just as wanting another piece of cake is compatible with thinking a meal should end. And the record of prolonging life so far, with more years spent in illness, gives reason to doubt that radically longer lives would be the healthy, engaged ones advocates imagine.

### 4. End aging and radically extend life
- Adherents: Don’t Die (institution; the movement founded by the entrepreneur Bryan Johnson, which says “We are fighting for the freedom to exist as long as one chooses”), https://dontdie.com/ ; Nick Bostrom (philosopher), “The Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant,” Journal of Medical Ethics, 2005, https://nickbostrom.com/papers/the-fable-of-the-dragon-tyrant/ . Also the LEV Foundation, led by Aubrey de Grey, whose work is “conducting and inspiring research to comprehensively cure and prevent human age-related disease” (https://www.levf.org/); and the Harvard geneticist David Sinclair, who said in 2019 that “it’s quite possible that aging is a reversible disease” (Harvard Gazette, https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2019/03/anti-aging-research-prime-time-for-an-impact-on-the-globe/).
- Case in the camp’s voice: Aging kills more people than everything else combined, and it does so slowly, through years of decline. If any other cause took that toll it would be called the world’s greatest emergency. Bostrom’s fable makes the point: a dragon that devoured thousands every day would not be accepted as natural, and “we have compelling moral reasons to get rid of human senescence.” Aging is a set of damage processes in cells and tissues, and damage can in principle be repaired; animal studies show lifespan is not fixed, and the oldest human ages show no hard wall. The aim is not to stretch out frailty but to keep people biologically young for as long as they choose to live, and if repair therapies improve faster than damage accumulates, there is no set limit. Classifying aging as a disease would unlock the research money, the regulatory pathway and the medical seriousness the problem deserves. The objections, from boredom to overpopulation, would never be accepted as reasons to withhold a cure for cancer; they are no better as reasons to let people age and die.
- Variation inside the camp: Members differ on method and on timing. De Grey emphasizes repairing several kinds of accumulated damage and has spoken of reaching “longevity escape velocity,” the point where therapies add years faster than people age; Sinclair emphasizes epigenetic reprogramming and reversal; Johnson promotes a personal regimen and a philosophy that makes not dying the organizing goal of life and society; Bostrom argues the moral case. Some, including de Grey, say they work on health rather than immortality, and expect long lives as a side effect of staying young.
- Best evidence: The rise of global life expectancy from 32 years in 1900 to 73 today, which shows that what is “natural” has always moved; lifespan extension in mice from rapamycin and other interventions; the plateau of death rates among Italians over 105, which counts against a fixed maximum; the estimate that slowing aging is worth tens of trillions of dollars; and Pew’s finding that 68 percent of Americans think most people would want radical life extension.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that radically longer lives would cause overpopulation, stagnation and inequality): Each has a policy answer: fertility is already falling below replacement in most rich countries, longer healthy lives mean longer working lives and later pensions, and the answer to unequal access is to make treatments cheap, as with every other medicine, not to deny them to everyone. Societies absorbed a doubling of life expectancy in the twentieth century without the disasters that were predicted. And the price of waiting is counted in lives: every year the work is delayed is another year in which age-related disease remains the leading cause of death.

## Crux

The question under the others: is aging a process that medicine should try to slow or stop, or the course of a human life that medicine should help people live well, treating the diseases that come with it but not the passing of time itself?

The part evidence can settle is what slowing aging would actually deliver. Over the next one to two decades, trials such as those funded by ARPA-H’s PROSPR program and the XPRIZE Healthspan prize, and any trial like TAME that is run to completion, can show whether a drug can delay several age-related diseases at once in people, whether measures such as intrinsic capacity or biological “clocks” predict real benefit, and whether the extra years gained are healthy ones or added years of illness. Demographic data can show whether there is a ceiling on lifespan. Studies of clinical practice can show whether diagnosing aging, or decline in capacity, leads older patients to better care or to less, and studies of prices and uptake can show whether treatments reach poorer people or widen the gap of more than 14 years in life expectancy between the richest and poorest American men.

The part no data can close is what a good human life is, and what a society should aim at. Even with complete evidence, people will disagree about whether a process that happens to everyone should be called a disease; whether a life without a natural end would be richer or emptier; whether the duty to prevent suffering extends to defeating aging itself or stops at helping people through a full lifespan; how to weigh the wishes of people alive now against the renewal that comes with generations; and whether public money should go first to the biology of aging or to closing the gaps in healthy years that already exist. A person who expects geroscience to work might still think radical life extension unwise, and a person who doubts it will work might still think aging is the greatest harm medicine could address.
