# Loneliness & Community — research

## Overview

Loneliness is the painful feeling that one’s relationships fall short of what one wants; social isolation is the objective condition of having few relationships or little contact with others; social capital is the wider web of friendships, families, congregations, clubs, neighbors and trust that lets people rely on one another. This topic covers all three and the argument that ties them together: whether rich societies, and the United States above all, are living through an “epidemic” of loneliness caused by the decline of community, how much harm loneliness and isolation do to health, what is driving the changes that are measurable (less time with friends, more time alone, more people living alone, fewer people in congregations), and who, if anyone, should act: governments and health systems, families, churches and voluntary associations, or technology companies offering artificial companions. It deliberately leaves to their own pages the narrower debates over smartphones and teenage mental health, falling birth rates and pronatalism, religion’s place in public life, and the specific problems of boys and men, drawing on them only where they bear on connection and community.

Why now: on 17 June 2026 Senator Chris Murphy of Connecticut reintroduced the National Strategy for Social Connection Act (S. 4818), which would create a permanent Office of Social Connection Policy in the White House, require a government-wide strategy across transportation, housing, health, education and labor, and fund research on loneliness at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention; Murphy said “Americans are feeling more disconnected than ever and it’s a full-blown crisis for our democracy” (press release, https://www.murphy.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/murphy-introduces-bill-to-tackle-loneliness-and-social-disconnection-improve-the-spiritual-health-of-our-democracy; bill text, https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119s4818is). The bill follows a year in which the question went global. In May 2025 the World Health Assembly adopted its first resolution on social connection (WHA78.9), and on 30 June 2025 the WHO Commission on Social Connection, co-chaired by former US Surgeon General Vivek Murthy, estimated that about one in six people worldwide is lonely and that loneliness accounts for about 871,000 deaths a year (WHO, https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death). Gallup reported in September 2025 that the share of Americans lonely “a lot of the day yesterday” had climbed back to 21 percent, its highest since March 2021 (https://news.gallup.com/poll/694199/u.s.-depression-rate-remains-historically-high.aspx).

The debate is live because the alarm and the evidence do not line up neatly. Measures of contact have clearly fallen: Americans spend much less time with friends and more time alone than twenty years ago, more live alone, and fewer belong to congregations. But measures of the feeling itself are patchy, vary several-fold depending on how the question is asked, and, outside some groups of young people, do not show a clear long-run rise; the WHO’s own report says earlier data “are too limited to determine whether the rates of social isolation and loneliness have risen or fallen.” The health case is also under challenge, as genetic studies suggest loneliness may mark poor health more than cause it. The participants are public-health bodies (the WHO, the former Surgeon General, the UK and Japanese governments, which both have ministers for loneliness), legislators such as Murphy, social scientists who doubt the epidemic (the Berkeley sociologist Claude Fischer) or document the decline (Robert Putnam, the Survey Center on American Life), conservative and religious institutions that see the collapse of family, faith and civic life as the root, free-market writers who reject the claim that markets make people lonely, and technology companies, led by Meta, that pitch AI companions as part of the answer.

For scale, the WHO estimates that tobacco kills more than 7 million people a year, so the loneliness estimate is large but well short of the comparisons with smoking that circulate in coverage (WHO tobacco fact sheet, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/tobacco).

Note: Across 90 studies of about 2.2 million people, social isolation was linked to a 32 percent higher risk of early death and loneliness to a 14 percent higher risk.
Context: This 2023 meta-analysis pooled 90 prospective cohort studies with 2,205,199 participants and found pooled hazard ratios of 1.32 for social isolation and 1.14 for loneliness on all-cause mortality. Isolation, the objective lack of contact, carries a larger association than the feeling of loneliness. The best-known earlier figure comes from Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues, whose 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies (308,849 participants) found a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival among people with stronger social relationships, an odds ratio of 1.50 (PLoS Medicine, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20668659/). That study is the source of the widely repeated comparison, used in the 2023 Surgeon General’s advisory, that lacking social connection is as dangerous as smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day. The 50 percent figure measures survival with strong ties rather than risk from loneliness, so it is not directly comparable to the two figures above. All three rest on observational studies, which adjust for health and income but cannot rule out that illness causes isolation rather than the reverse.

## Questions

Question: Is loneliness actually rising, or is it the contact between people that has fallen?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Rising, especially among the young / Roughly stable over decades
Why contested: The measures that show a clear decline (time with friends, time alone, living alone, congregation membership) are measures of contact, not of the feeling. Direct measures of loneliness are short, inconsistent and sensitive to wording, and the WHO’s own 2025 report concludes that earlier data are too limited to say whether loneliness has risen or fallen.
Case for rising: Every long series that tracks young people points the same way. Loneliness scores among 18 to 29 year olds rose by about half a standard deviation from 1976 to 2019, teenagers’ loneliness at school rose in 36 of 37 countries between 2012 and 2018, Japanese studies show a rise since 1983, and England’s chronic loneliness rate stepped up after the pandemic. Time with friends fell by almost half in the United States even before COVID-19. When contact collapses this much and young people report more loneliness wherever they are asked consistently, waiting for perfect data is a choice to do nothing.
Case for roughly stable: The best-designed studies of adults and older people find stable or falling loneliness: no increase among older Americans between 2005 and 2016, stable or declining trends in Northern Europe, and a decline in high-income countries between 2023 and 2024 in Gallup’s world data. The young-adult rise appears in North America but not in Europe or Asia. The headline “half of Americans are lonely” figures come from multi-item scales that have no long baseline, and Americans have repeatedly been told they are newly lonely since the 1920s. People are spending more time alone, but that has not produced a matching rise in the feeling.

Question: Does loneliness itself damage health, or does it mostly mark other problems?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Direct health threat / Marker of other ills
Why contested: Lonely and isolated people die sooner in almost every cohort study, but those studies are observational, and loneliness travels with depression, poverty, illness and bereavement. Genetic and experimental evidence points in different directions.
Case for a direct threat: Meta-analyses of millions of people find isolation linked to a 32 percent and loneliness to a 14 percent higher risk of death after adjusting for health and income, and associations with heart disease, stroke and dementia are consistent across countries, ages and follow-up lengths. Laboratory and animal studies show plausible pathways through chronic stress, inflammation, sleep and health behavior. The same standard of evidence was enough to act on many other risk factors before randomized trials were possible.
Case for a marker: When researchers use genetic variants as a natural experiment, most of the links between loneliness and disease disappear: in a 2024 UK Biobank study, 20 of 26 associations did not look causal, and socioeconomic factors, depression and existing illness explained most of the rest. People who are already ill withdraw from social life, so illness causes isolation as much as the reverse. Comparisons such as “as deadly as 15 cigarettes a day” rest on survival odds from observational studies and overstate what is known.

Question: Who should take the lead in rebuilding connection: government or civil society?
Tag: value
Poles: Public strategy and funding / Family, faith and voluntary groups
Why contested: Both sides agree that social ties matter. They disagree about whether a problem of relationships is something governments can or should fix, and about whether the growth of the state is part of the cause.
Case for public strategy: Loneliness is shaped by things only governments control: transport, zoning, housing, public spaces, working hours, health systems and data. England and Japan have ministers and national strategies, the World Health Assembly has asked all countries to act, and a coordinated strategy can fund what works, such as social prescribing through doctors, and measure progress. Leaving it to private initiative leaves out exactly the people civil society misses: the poor, the disabled, the bereaved and the very old.
Case for civil society: Friendship cannot be delivered by an agency. The institutions that used to supply belonging, marriage, congregations, unions, lodges and neighborhood associations, grew from below, and their decline is cultural. Studies by the congressional Social Capital Project find weaker social capital where reliance on government programs is higher, suggesting the state can crowd out the voluntary bonds it hopes to rebuild. Public effort is better spent removing obstacles and supporting families and local institutions than building new bureaucracies.

Question: What is mainly driving the loss of contact: technology, economics, or the decline of family and faith?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Screens and platforms / Older structural shifts
Why contested: The drop in time with friends, the fall in congregation membership, later marriage and the rise of living alone all overlap in time with the spread of the internet and smartphones, with economic changes such as longer commutes and less secure work, and with secularization. Disentangling them is hard with observational data.
Case for screens and platforms: The sharpest changes among the young began around 2012, when smartphones and social media became universal, and they appear across dozens of countries at once, which purely national economic or cultural explanations struggle to explain. School loneliness rose most where smartphone access and internet use were high. Time once spent with friends is now spent alone at home with screens.
Case for older structural shifts: Membership in congregations and associations was falling for decades before smartphones, and marriage and solo living trends go back to the 1960s. Longer working hours constrain time with others, low-income countries show the highest loneliness, and lonely people in Japan most often cite bereavement, living alone and job changes, not technology. Treating phones as the main cause risks neglecting family formation, work, housing and the decline of shared institutions.

Question: Can AI companions relieve loneliness, or will they deepen it?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Useful supplement / Hollow substitute
Why contested: AI companions are new and spreading fast, especially among teenagers, and the early studies are short and conflicting. The answer may differ for an isolated 85 year old and a 14 year old.
Case for a useful supplement: In controlled experiments, a week of conversations with an AI companion reduced loneliness about as much as talking with another person, mostly by making users feel heard. New York State’s program giving companion robots to isolated older adults reports a 95 percent reduction in loneliness among participants and very heavy daily use, though without a control group. For people who are housebound, grieving or socially anxious, something that listens is better than nothing, and it can be a bridge to human contact.
Case for a hollow substitute: In a four-week study of 981 people, heavier voluntary chatbot use went with more loneliness, more emotional dependence and less real-world socializing. A third of teen users already find AI conversations as satisfying as time with friends, which suggests substitution rather than support. Companies that profit from engagement have reason to make companions compelling rather than to send users back to people, and child-safety groups recommend that minors not use them at all.

Question: Is spending more time alone a problem if people do not feel lonely?
Tag: value
Poles: Solitude is a choice / Isolation is a risk
Why contested: The clearest measured change is that people spend more time alone, at home, and live alone more often, but many say they prefer it. Whether that counts as a social problem depends on what one thinks a good life and a healthy society require.
Case for treating it as a choice: Living alone and spending evenings at home are often the fruit of prosperity, independence and longer lives; the Nordic countries, where solo living is most common, report some of the lowest loneliness in Europe. Treating a preference as a pathology is paternalistic, and it risks stigmatizing single people, introverts and older people living independently.
Case for treating it as a risk: Objective isolation is linked to early death more strongly than loneliness is, and its harms do not depend on whether people feel them. Preferences adapt to what is available: people who have lost the habit of seeing friends may stop missing them. A society with fewer shared activities also loses the trust and cooperation that democracy and mutual aid rely on, a cost that falls on everyone, not only on the people who stay home.

Question: Does calling it an “epidemic” help or harm?
Tag: value
Poles: Mobilizes needed action / Overstates and medicalizes
Why contested: The epidemic language has driven attention, money and legislation, but it borrows the authority of medicine for a claim whose trend data are disputed, and it reframes an ordinary human experience as a disease.
Case for the framing: Public health has always used the language of epidemics to make invisible, diffuse harms visible. The Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory and the WHO commission put social connection on the agenda of governments that had ignored it, and the scale of the problem is large whether or not it is new. Without strong language, a problem that falls on the isolated, who by definition have no one to speak for them, stays neglected.
Case against: Overstated alarms erode trust in public health when the data do not bear them out, and they can make lonely people feel their condition is a disease rather than a common, often temporary state. Each generation since the 1920s has been told it is the loneliest, and the result can be panic about the wrong causes and the wrong cures. Precise language would focus help on the groups where loneliness is clearly high and rising.

Question: Should efforts focus first on the young or on the old?
Tag: value
Poles: Young adults first / Older adults first
Why contested: Loneliness is highest and, where measured, rising among adolescents and young adults, while objective isolation and its health consequences are greatest among older people. Programs, budgets and laws have historically targeted the old.
Case for young adults first: Young people report the most loneliness in almost every survey, from the WHO’s global estimates to Gallup and England’s official statistics, and the trend for them is the clearest. Patterns of connection set in youth last a lifetime, and loneliness among the young is closely tied to depression and anxiety, which have risen sharply. Most existing programs were designed for the elderly and miss them.
Case for older adults first: Older people are most likely to be objectively isolated, through widowhood, disability and living alone, and isolation carries the strongest link to death, dementia and need for care. Their numbers are growing fast as populations age. Young people’s loneliness often eases as they form partnerships and careers, while isolation in old age tends to deepen and is harder to escape without help.

## Positions

Camp: Make social connection a public health priority
Adherents: World Health Organization, Commission on Social Connection (institution), report From loneliness to social connection, June 2025, https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/978240112360 ; Vivek Murthy, 19th and 21st US Surgeon General and co-chair of the WHO commission, advisory Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation, May 2023, https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf (the HHS server blocks automated requests; an archived copy is at https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) ; Senator Chris Murphy, National Strategy for Social Connection Act, June 2026, https://www.murphy.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/murphy-introduces-bill-to-tackle-loneliness-and-social-disconnection-improve-the-spiritual-health-of-our-democracy
Case: Social connection is a basic human need and a determinant of health on the scale of smoking, obesity and physical inactivity, yet it is almost entirely missing from health policy. One person in six worldwide is lonely, loneliness is linked to hundreds of thousands of deaths a year, and the forces pulling people apart, from car-dependent suburbs and long working hours to platforms designed to hold attention, are built into how societies are organized. Problems built into systems need systemic answers: national strategies with a lead office, routine measurement, social prescribing that lets doctors refer lonely patients to community activities, design standards for housing, transport and public spaces, and accountability for technology companies. Individuals and voluntary groups matter, but they cannot by themselves reach the isolated, who are by definition the people with no one to bring them in. England and Japan already treat this as a government responsibility; the WHO has asked every country to do the same.
Variation: Within this camp, some put the economy at the root. The economist Noreena Hertz, author of The Lonely Century (2020), blames market competition, insecure work and the decline of unions, and wants stronger labor protections and public investment in shared spaces; others, including Murthy, put more weight on regulating social media and protecting children’s time. They agree that government must lead and differ on which levers matter most.
Best evidence: The 2023 meta-analysis of 90 cohort studies (2.2 million people) linking isolation to a 32 percent and loneliness to a 14 percent higher risk of death; the American Time Use Survey showing time with friends falling from 60 minutes a day in 2003 to 34 in 2019; the WHO’s global estimates of prevalence and attributable deaths; and the consistency of higher loneliness among young people across surveys and countries.
Answer to objections: To those who say the trend is unproven, this camp answers that the case for action rests on the size of the burden, not on whether it is new: high blood pressure did not need to be rising to deserve a public health response. To those who say loneliness only marks other illnesses, it answers that isolation is linked to death even after adjusting for health, that social ties shape behavior and stress in well-understood ways, and that the uncertainty argues for better measurement, which a national strategy would fund. To those who fear bureaucracy, it answers that a strategy coordinates and funds local groups rather than replacing them.

Camp: Rebuild family, faith and civic life
Adherents: Joint Economic Committee Social Capital Project, Republican staff under Senator Mike Lee (institution), “Does Government Crowd Out Social Capital?” December 2022, https://www.jec.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/republicans/2022/12/new-report-does-government-crowd-out-social-capital ; Institute for Family Studies (institution), Erica Komisar, “Solving the Loneliness Epidemic Requires Family, Faith, and Friendship,” May 2023, https://ifstudies.org/blog/solving-the-loneliness-epidemic-requires-family-faith-and-friendship ; Timothy P. Carney, American Enterprise Institute, Alienated America (2019), https://www.aei.org/research-products/book/alienated-america-why-some-places-thrive-while-others-collapse/
Case: The loss of connection is real, but it is not mainly a health problem and it will not be solved by a health program. It is the visible result of the long decline of the institutions that used to give people belonging without anyone having to plan it: marriage and the extended family, the congregation, the lodge, the union hall, the parish school and the neighborhood. Where those institutions remain strong, people are less alienated, more mobile and more trusting; where they have collapsed, government programs have moved in and the voluntary bonds have withered further. The remedy is to make family formation easier, protect and encourage religious and civic life, give local communities room to run their own affairs, and stop treating as private and optional the commitments that sustain people through illness, grief and old age. As Carney puts it, it is not the factory closings that have torn communities apart, but the church closings.
Best evidence: Gallup’s finding that membership in a house of worship fell from 70 percent of Americans in 1999 to 47 percent in 2020; Census data showing married-couple households falling to 47 percent and one-person households rising to 29 percent; the Survey Center on American Life’s finding that the share of Americans with no close friends rose from 3 percent in 1990 to 12 percent in 2021; and the Social Capital Project’s finding that counties more reliant on government programs tend to have weaker social capital.
Answer to objections: To those who say this ignores people without families or faith, this camp answers that it wants institutions open to newcomers, and that congregations and voluntary groups already do most of the visiting, feeding and befriending of the isolated. To those who say the decline of religion and marriage is a free choice that policy should not reverse, it answers that policy already shapes those choices through taxes, benefits and housing, and should stop penalizing them. To the public-health camp it answers that a White House office cannot supply friendship, and that the evidence it cites shows the decline of communities, which is this camp’s point.

Camp: Drop the epidemic framing
Adherents: Claude S. Fischer, sociologist at the University of California, Berkeley, “The Myth of the Loneliness Epidemic,” Asterisk, November 2024, https://asteriskmag.com/issues/08/the-myth-of-the-loneliness-epidemic ; Cato Institute (institution), Johan Norberg, “Does Capitalism Really Make Us Lonely?” September 2023, https://www.cato.org/commentary/does-capitalism-really-make-us-lonely (the Cato server blocks automated requests; a syndicated copy is at https://www.yahoo.com/news/does-capitalism-really-us-lonely-100022185.html)
Case: There is no good evidence of an epidemic of loneliness, and the alarm does harm. Long-run data on how lonely people feel show no clear rise among adults, the numbers that make headlines come from new survey instruments with no baseline, and similar warnings that Americans had become a nation of strangers appeared in 1929, 1950 and 1972. What has changed is how people live: more live alone, marry later and spend more time at home, often by choice and as a result of prosperity and longer lives. Treating that as a disease medicalizes ordinary experience, invites panic about the wrong causes, from capitalism to secularism, and diverts attention from the specific groups for whom loneliness really is high, such as the widowed, the poor, the sick and some young people. Real people are lonely and deserve help; the claim that society as a whole is getting lonelier is what this camp rejects.
Best evidence: The WHO commission’s own conclusion that earlier data are too limited to say whether loneliness has risen; the University of Chicago finding of no increase in loneliness among older Americans between 2005 and 2016; England’s official chronic loneliness rate holding at 5 to 6 percent from 2013/14 to 2021/22; Gallup world data showing loneliness lower in high-income countries and falling there between 2023 and 2024; Fischer’s finding that loneliness depends mainly on whether someone has a spouse or partner rather than on friends; and Our World in Data’s finding that living alone and feeling lonely are only weakly related.
Answer to objections: To the evidence that time with friends has fallen, this camp answers that aloneness is not loneliness, and that the falls in contact have not produced matching rises in the feeling. To the evidence that young people are lonelier, it answers that the rise is real in some places and deserves study, but that it is a targeted problem, not a society-wide epidemic, and that young people’s loneliness often eases as they form partnerships. To those who say strong language is needed to get action, it answers that overstated claims make for badly aimed programs and erode trust when the data catch up.

Camp: Use AI companions to ease loneliness
Adherents: New York State Office for the Aging (institution), ElliQ companion robot program, August 2023, https://aging.ny.gov/news/nysofas-rollout-ai-companion-robot-elliq-shows-95-reduction-loneliness ; Mark Zuckerberg, chief executive of Meta, interview on the Dwarkesh Podcast, April 2025, https://dwarkesh.com/p/mark-zuckerberg-2
Case: People want more connection than they have, and human help cannot reach everyone who needs it. There are not enough visitors, therapists or neighbors for every housebound older person, night-shift worker or anxious teenager, and loneliness peaks at hours when no one else is awake. AI companions are available at any moment, never tire, remember what matters to their users and can prompt them to call family, take medicine or join an activity. They do not replace human relationships, and their advocates say so; they fill gaps between them, and for many isolated people the alternative is not a friend but silence. As the technology improves and the stigma fades, society will find the vocabulary to recognize why people who use these tools are acting rationally.
Best evidence: New York State reports a 95 percent reduction in loneliness among more than 800 older adults given ElliQ devices, with users interacting dozens of times a day; experiments by Julian De Freitas and colleagues, published in the Journal of Consumer Research in 2025, found AI companions reduced loneliness about as much as talking with another person and more than watching videos; and Common Sense Media’s survey shows that 72 percent of teenagers have already used AI companions, so the question is how they are designed rather than whether they will be used.
Answer to objections: To evidence that heavy chatbot use goes with more loneliness, this camp answers that the lonely are the people most likely to seek out companions, so correlation does not show harm, and that design choices, such as nudging users toward people, matter more than the technology itself. To the charge that companies profit from dependence, it answers that this is a case for safety rules, which states such as California and New York have begun to write, not for denying the tool to people it helps. To those who say only human contact counts, it answers that this is a luxury many isolated people do not have.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others: is the measurable retreat from shared life (less time with friends, more time alone, fewer families and congregations) producing a widespread, growing harm that societies must deliberately reverse, or is it mostly a change in how people choose to live, with real but concentrated suffering among particular groups?

What evidence could settle: Whether loneliness, as distinct from time alone, is rising, and for whom. That needs long, consistent series using the same questions across ages and countries, which mostly do not exist yet but are now being built (England’s Community Life Survey, Japan’s annual national survey, Gallup’s world data). And whether loneliness and isolation cause illness and early death, or mainly accompany poverty, depression and existing illness: genetic studies, natural experiments and randomized trials of interventions can narrow this. If loneliness turns out to be rising broadly and to cause harm in its own right, the case for treating it like other public health threats becomes much stronger; if it proves stable outside some young groups and mostly a marker of other problems, the case shifts toward targeted help and toward treating the underlying conditions.

What no data can close: Whether a good society should steer people toward togetherness even when they choose solitude, and who should do the steering. People who value family, faith and local belonging see the decline of those institutions as a loss in itself, whatever the loneliness surveys say; people who value individual freedom see more solo living as a gain that should not be pathologized. Even with the facts settled, the choice between a government strategy, a revival of civil society, a lighter touch, or technology that fills gaps turns on how much one trusts the state, markets and tradition to shape private life, and on whether a relationship with a machine counts as connection at all.
