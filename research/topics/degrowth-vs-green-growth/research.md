# Degrowth vs. Green Growth — research

## Overview

This topic asks whether an economy can keep growing while its greenhouse gas emissions fall, and fall fast enough to meet the climate goals governments have signed up to. “Green growth” is the view, held by most governments and international bodies, that economic output (measured as gross domestic product, GDP) can be “decoupled” from emissions through clean energy, efficiency and policy, so that rich and poor countries alike can keep growing while emissions fall to net zero. “Degrowth” is the view that in rich countries this decoupling cannot happen fast enough, and that those countries should instead plan a deliberate, fair reduction in their energy and material use, accepting that GDP will probably shrink. Between and around these poles sit two further positions: “agrowth” or post-growth, which says policy should stop treating GDP growth as a goal at all and pursue climate and wellbeing targets directly, whatever happens to GDP; and a growth-first view, which says growth is what lifts people out of poverty and pays for adaptation and innovation, so emissions should be cut only where the benefits clearly exceed the costs. The topic is about growth itself: whether it can be made compatible with climate goals, whether it should remain a goal, and what rich countries owe poorer ones. It deliberately excludes the pace of the energy transition and the timing of net zero (covered under net-zero pace), the choice among energy sources, the supply of critical minerals, inequality as such, population and birth rates, and civilizational collapse, each of which has its own page and appears here only where it bears on growth.

Why now: figures published in 2026 gave both sides fresh evidence from the same facts. The International Energy Agency’s Global Energy Review 2026 found that global energy-related CO2 emissions rose by about 0.4 percent in 2025 to a record of nearly 38.4 billion tonnes, while the world economy grew by about 3.1 percent, which the agency called “a continued decoupling between emissions and economic activity” (IEA, https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/df903e1c-49c6-4757-8cbf-6fbcfe7611a0/GlobalEnergyReview2026.pdf). On 2 September 2026, Carbon Brief published an analysis by Lauri Myllyvirta of the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air finding that China’s CO2 emissions from fossil fuels and cement had plateaued for more than two years since peaking in March 2024, and fell by 1 percent in the second quarter of 2026 as oil use dropped 9 percent amid the Strait of Hormuz supply crisis (https://www.carbonbrief.org/analysis-chinas-co2-emissions-fall-in-q2-2026-due-to-plummeting-oil-use), even though China’s economy grew 5.0 percent in 2025 according to its National Bureau of Statistics (https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202601/t20260119_1962328.html). Supporters of green growth read these as evidence that growth and falling emissions can go together even in the world’s largest emitter; supporters of degrowth read the same year as one in which global emissions still set a record while the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget shrank to about four years of current emissions (Global Carbon Project, https://globalcarbonbudget.org/fossil-fuel-co2-emissions-hit-record-high-in-2025/). In the same months the question of growth itself moved onto the United Nations agenda: on 7 May 2026 a UN High-Level Expert Group co-chaired by Nora Lustig and Kaushik Basu presented “Counting What Counts,” a dashboard of indicators meant to complement GDP, with Basu stressing that moving beyond GDP “does not mean eschewing economic growth” (UN DESA, https://www.un.org/en/desa/united-nations-proposes-new-global-dashboard-to-measure-progress-beyond-gdp).

The debate is live because both the evidence and the politics are moving. The Global Carbon Project counts 35 countries whose emissions fell over 2015 to 2024 while their GDP grew, twice as many as in the previous decade, yet global fossil CO2 emissions are still rising and the remaining carbon budget for 1.5°C is, in its words, “virtually exhausted.” Among academics, a 2023 survey of climate policy researchers found 73 percent preferring agrowth or degrowth positions to green growth, while a 2026 survey of 16,781 citizens in 13 European Union countries found nearly 60 percent holding pro-growth views and fewer than 10 percent skeptical of growth. The participants are governments and the European Commission, which describe climate policy as a growth strategy; international bodies such as the OECD, the World Bank, the IEA and UN agencies; ecological economists and degrowth scholars such as Jason Hickel, Giorgos Kallis and Julia Steinberger, who won a 9.9 million euro European Research Council grant in 2022 for post-growth research; post-growth advocates such as Kate Raworth and the Wellbeing Economy Alliance; ecomodernists and data writers such as the Breakthrough Institute and Hannah Ritchie; growth-first voices such as Bjørn Lomborg’s Copenhagen Consensus Center and the current United States administration; and the governments of developing countries, which insist on their right to grow.

Green growth supporters point to the widening gap between the two lines and to its recent acceleration as clean energy gets cheaper. Degrowth supporters point out that global emissions are still rising in absolute terms, and that the IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report estimated that the carbon intensity of energy would have to fall by about 7.7 percent a year from 2020 to 2050 in pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C, against 0.3 percent a year achieved in 2010 to 2019 (IPCC AR6 Working Group III, Summary for Policymakers, 2022, B.2.4: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGIII_SPM.pdf). Emissions here are territorial (counted where they occur) and exclude land use.

Context: The Global Carbon Project, the scientific consortium that publishes the annual global carbon budget, reports that 35 countries had declining emissions over 2015 to 2024 while their GDP grew, “twice as big as the previous decade” (2005 to 2014). The IPCC’s 2022 assessment found that at least 18 countries had sustained reductions in both production-based greenhouse gas emissions and consumption-based CO2 emissions (which count the emissions in imported goods) for longer than ten years, with some achieving several years of reductions around 4 percent a year, but that “these reductions have only partly offset global emissions growth” (IPCC AR6 WGIII SPM, B.3.5: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGIII_SPM.pdf). Zeke Hausfather, writing for the Breakthrough Institute in 2021, counted 32 countries with more than a million people that had decoupled on both territorial and consumption bases since 2005. The count is the green growth camp’s headline evidence that absolute decoupling is real and spreading; the degrowth camp accepts the count and disputes its sufficiency, as the next figures show.

Note: The world can emit about 170 billion more tonnes of CO2 for an even chance of holding warming to 1.5°C, about four years of current emissions.
Context: The Global Carbon Project projected fossil CO2 emissions of 38.1 billion tonnes in 2025, up 1.1 percent, with emissions from land-use change adding about 4.1 billion tonnes. At that rate the 1.5°C budget “will be gone before 2030”; the project calls it “virtually exhausted.” The budgets for 1.7°C and 2°C are 525 and 1,055 billion tonnes, about 12 and 25 years of 2025 emissions (FAQ: https://globalcarbonbudget.org/gcb-2025/the-global-carbon-budget-faqs-2025/). The budget is what turns a dispute about rates into a dispute about time: decoupling that is real but slow uses up a fixed budget. Degrowth supporters argue that with budgets this small, rich countries cannot wait for technology alone; green growth supporters argue that because 1.5°C is effectively lost, the relevant question is how quickly clean technology can bend emissions toward net zero under any target, and that shrinking economies would not change the arithmetic much given that most emissions growth now comes from middle-income countries.

Social scientists other than economists were the most skeptical; economists were the most likely to hold green growth views. The paper is at https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-023-01198-2.

## Questions

Question: Can rich economies cut their emissions fast enough to meet the Paris goals while they keep growing?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Fast enough / Too slow
Why contested: Everyone agrees that some rich countries have cut emissions while growing; the Global Carbon Project counts 35 countries that did so over 2015 to 2024. The disagreement is about speed. The rates achieved so far, typically 1 to 4 percent a year, are far below the rates that the carbon budget implies, and the two sides disagree about whether the past is a guide to what clean technology and policy can now deliver.
Case for fast enough: The rates of the past decade were achieved before solar, wind and batteries became the cheapest sources of new power almost everywhere and before most climate policy took effect. The UK cut its territorial emissions by more than half since 1990 while its economy grew, the EU by 37 percent while growing 71 percent, and in 2025 clean electricity covered all the growth in world power demand while China’s emissions stopped rising despite 5 percent growth. Decarbonization is a set of engineering substitutions, from coal to solar, from petrol cars to electric ones, from gas boilers to heat pumps, none of which requires people to consume less. The IPCC’s assessed pathways reach 1.5°C or 2°C with world GDP still doubling by 2050 at a cost of a few percent of GDP. Speed is a matter of policy and investment, and both are accelerating.
Case for too slow: The question is not whether decoupling happens but whether it happens at the rate the carbon budget requires, and it does not. Vogel and Hickel found that the 11 rich countries that decoupled in 2013 to 2019 cut consumption-based emissions by about 1.6 percent a year, against roughly 30 percent a year needed for a fair share of the 1.5°C budget; at their pace a 95 percent cut would take more than two centuries. Every percent of extra GDP raises energy demand that clean supply must then also cover, so growth makes the target harder, not easier. The 1.5°C budget is nearly gone and the models that reconcile it with growth lean on carbon removal at a scale that does not exist. Reducing energy and material demand in rich countries is the only lever that works immediately.

Question: Should GDP growth remain a central goal of economic policy in rich countries?
Tag: value
Poles: Keep pursuing growth / Stop targeting growth
Why contested: GDP measures the market value of output, not welfare, and almost no one defends it as a full measure of progress. The disagreement is whether rich societies should still aim to raise it, given what growth pays for and what it may cost, or whether they should target wellbeing, equality and environmental limits directly and accept whatever GDP results.
Case for keep pursuing growth: Growth is what funds pensions, health care, defense and the energy transition itself without forcing a fight over a fixed pie. It is the reason today’s workers live better than their grandparents, and it is closely tied to the innovation that makes clean technology cheaper for everyone, including poorer countries. Societies that stop growing tend to become more zero-sum and politically bitter, as stagnant periods have shown. Measuring progress with a broader dashboard is welcome, as the UN’s 2026 Beyond GDP report proposes, but its own co-chair Kaushik Basu stressed that this “does not mean eschewing economic growth.” Abandoning growth as a goal would remove the main source of rising living standards without any guarantee of better climate outcomes.
Case for stop targeting growth: GDP counts pollution cleanup and longer commutes as gains and ignores unpaid care, leisure and the depletion of nature, so steering a rich economy by it steers toward the wrong things. Past a certain level of income, more GDP buys little extra health, longevity or life satisfaction, while environmental damage keeps rising. Kate Raworth argues that rich countries need “economies that make us thrive, whether or not they grow,” and Jeroen van den Bergh that if GDP does not capture societal progress in rich nations, “the time has come to ignore it.” Freed from the need to protect growth at all costs, governments could adopt stricter climate policies that are now blocked because they might slow GDP.

Question: Does more economic growth still make people in rich countries better off?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Still raises wellbeing / Little added wellbeing
Why contested: Evidence on income and wellbeing points two ways. Within countries, richer people report higher life satisfaction at every income level. Across time, some rich countries have grown for decades with little change in average reported happiness, and indicators such as life expectancy level off at high incomes.
Case for still raises wellbeing: Large studies using global surveys, such as those by Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers (2008) and Matthew Killingsworth (2021), find that life satisfaction keeps rising with income without a clear ceiling. Growth also delivers things that wellbeing surveys miss: new medicines, better technology, the fiscal room to care for aging populations, and resilience against shocks. Many people in rich countries still struggle with housing, child care and health costs, and growth is the most reliable way to ease those pressures without taking from someone else.
Case for little added wellbeing: Richard Easterlin’s finding that average happiness in rich countries has barely moved as incomes multiplied has held up in many long-run series, and research on “wellbeing thresholds” finds that most social outcomes, from life expectancy to education, can be met at income and energy levels well below those of today’s richest countries. What matters past that point is how income is distributed and what public services provide, not total output. If extra growth adds little wellbeing in rich countries but adds environmental pressure that falls on everyone, its net value there is low.

Question: Can a rich economy stay stable without growth?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Stable without growth / Growth-dependent
Why contested: Modern economies are built around growth: unemployment rises when output growth falls below productivity growth, public debts are sustainable when nominal growth exceeds interest rates, and pension systems rely on rising wages. Whether these dependencies can be redesigned, and at what cost, has been modeled but never tested at national scale in peacetime.
Case for stable without growth: The dependencies are institutional, not natural laws. Peter Victor and Tim Jackson’s macroeconomic models of Canada find scenarios in which output stabilizes while employment, public finances and emissions all improve, through shorter working hours, a shift toward labor-intensive services such as care and education, and redistribution. Japan has had near-zero growth for long stretches without social collapse. Rich societies already produce enough to meet everyone’s needs; the problem is distribution and the design of welfare systems, which can be changed.
Case for growth-dependent: Every recession of the past century has brought unemployment, falling tax revenue and political backlash, and there is no historical example of a deliberately non-growing modern economy that stayed prosperous and democratic. With aging populations, health and pension costs are rising regardless, and debt dynamics turn hostile without growth. Models of stable no-growth economies depend on assumptions, such as smooth reductions in working hours and productivity, that real labor markets and capital markets may not follow. A 2024 review of 561 degrowth studies by Ivan Savin and Jeroen van den Bergh found that almost 90 percent were opinion pieces rather than analysis and that few addressed economy-wide effects.

Question: Is the decoupling seen so far genuine, or largely shifted abroad and confined to carbon?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Genuine decoupling / Shifted or partial
Why contested: Territorial emissions, counted where they occur, flatter countries that import manufactured goods; consumption-based emissions, which follow the goods, show smaller cuts. Carbon is also only one environmental pressure; material use, land and biodiversity follow different trends.
Case for genuine decoupling: The strongest test has been passed. The IPCC found that at least 18 countries cut both production-based greenhouse gases and consumption-based CO2 for more than ten years, and the UK’s consumption-based footprint is 29 percent below its 2007 peak even as GDP grew. Emissions are falling because coal plants closed and were replaced by gas and renewables, not because factories moved. Material decoupling is weaker, but material use in high-income countries has been roughly flat since 2008, and the materials that matter most for climate, fossil fuels, are exactly what the transition replaces.
Case for shifted or partial: Even on the consumption basis the UK footprint has fallen only 15 percent since 1996 while territorial emissions halved, and over half of the UK footprint is now emitted abroad. Globally, the International Resource Panel finds “no evidence of widespread absolute decoupling” of material use, which has tripled since 1970 and drives most biodiversity loss. A systematic review by Helmut Haberl and colleagues (2020) of 835 studies found absolute decoupling rare and concluded that “large rapid absolute reductions of resource use and GHG emissions cannot be achieved through observed decoupling rates.” Solving carbon while overshooting other planetary boundaries is not green growth.

Question: Will cheaper clean technology make growth and decarbonization reinforce each other, or will rebound effects and limits eat the gains?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Clean tech pays / Rebound eats gains
Why contested: Solar electricity is about 89 percent cheaper than in 2010 and clean energy now draws nearly twice as much investment as fossil fuels. But efficiency gains tend to lower costs and raise consumption, and historically new energy sources have added to old ones rather than replaced them.
Case for clean tech pays: Clean technologies follow learning curves: every doubling of deployment cuts costs further, so growth in demand for them speeds the transition. Once electricity from the sun and wind is cheaper than from fossil fuels, the market does the decoupling and the poorest countries can develop on clean power. Renewables overtook coal in global electricity in 2025 and met all new demand. The rebound effect matters when the extra consumption is fossil-powered; when energy itself is clean, using more of it is not a climate problem.
Case for rebound eats gains: Efficiency savings are partly or wholly spent on more consumption elsewhere; a 2021 review led by Paul Brockway estimated that economy-wide rebound may erode more than half of expected energy savings. Global clean energy has grown for two decades while fossil energy use also kept rising. Building the new energy system requires large amounts of metals, land and construction, whose extraction carries its own emissions and ecological damage. As long as total demand keeps growing, clean supply is chasing a moving target.

Question: Should rich countries reduce their consumption to leave room for poorer countries to grow?
Tag: value
Poles: Rich must shrink / Everyone can grow
Why contested: The average person in a high-income country emits about 35 times as much CO2 as the average person in a low-income country, and the remaining carbon budget is small. Nearly every camp agrees poor countries need to grow; the disagreement is whether fairness requires the rich to cut back to make that room, or whether technology can let everyone grow at once.
Case for rich must shrink: The carbon budget is a shared and nearly exhausted resource, and rich countries have already used far more than their share. Every tonne they emit to keep growing is a tonne unavailable to countries that still need energy for basic needs. Degrowth scholars such as Jason Hickel argue for “convergence”: rich countries reducing their energy and material use while poorer ones increase theirs toward a common sustainable level. Asking the poor to wait for clean technology while the rich keep expanding repeats the historical pattern in which the global North’s prosperity was built on the South’s resources.
Case for everyone can grow: The poor gain nothing from rich-country recessions, which cut demand for their exports, aid budgets and investment. What lets poorer countries grow cleanly is cheap clean technology, and that has come from rich-country markets, research and scale; Chinese solar panels became cheap because global markets bought them at enormous scale. Most future emissions growth will come from middle-income countries, so even zero emissions in the rich world would leave the problem unsolved without cheap clean energy everywhere. Fairness is better served by finance and technology transfer than by shrinking.

Question: Could a planned reduction in rich countries’ output win and keep democratic support?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Can win support / Politically impossible
Why contested: Climate policy already provokes backlash when it raises costs. Surveys find substantial public interest in wellbeing over growth when posed abstractly, but much less support for an economy that deliberately shrinks.
Case for can win support: Around 60 percent of respondents in European and global surveys prioritize environmental protection over growth when given a binary choice, as a 2026 Nature Communications paper summarizes, and many specific degrowth policies, such as shorter working weeks, better public transport, free public services and limits on luxury consumption such as private jets, poll well individually. Degrowth advocates argue that the program is about security and free time, not deprivation, and that framed that way it can build a coalition, as citizens’ assemblies on climate have tended to back ambitious demand-side measures.
Case for politically impossible: A 2026 survey of 16,781 citizens in 13 EU countries found nearly 60 percent holding pro-growth views and only 8 percent skeptical of growth, even in the region where academic skepticism is highest. The review by Savin and van den Bergh found that most degrowth studies of public support concluded the strategy was socially and politically infeasible. Voters punish governments for recessions, and a program that promises lower GDP hands the field to parties that reject climate policy altogether, as recent elections in Europe and the United States suggest.

Question: How much growth is it worth giving up to reduce the risk of climate damage?
Tag: value
Poles: Weigh costs and benefits / Avoid the risk
Why contested: Economic models that weigh mitigation costs against climate damages often recommend slower emissions cuts than the Paris goals, especially if future damages are discounted at market rates. [Correction 2026-10-05: not “often”; some cost-benefit models, such as William Nordhaus’s, favor slower cuts than the Paris goals, while the IPCC finds the benefits of limiting warming to 2°C exceed the costs in most of the literature.] Others hold that catastrophic and irreversible risks, and harms to future generations and the poor, cannot be traded against GDP in that way.
Case for weigh costs and benefits: Resources are limited and every dollar spent on expensive emissions cuts is not spent on health, education or adaptation, which often do more good per dollar, especially for the poor. Bjørn Lomborg argues that even after climate damage, people at the end of this century will be far better off than people today, and that those most vulnerable to climate change are best protected by getting richer. Climate policy should be judged like any other policy: cut emissions where it is cheap, invest in clean-energy research, and avoid policies whose costs exceed their benefits.
Case for avoid the risk: Cost-benefit models leave out the outcomes that matter most, such as tipping points, mass displacement and species extinction, and their answers hinge on a discount rate that is an ethical choice about how much future people count. The IPCC reports that the benefits of limiting warming to 2°C exceed the costs in most of the literature unless damages are at the low end or the future is heavily discounted. The damage falls mainly on poor people and future generations who did not share in the growth that caused it. Where losses are irreversible, precaution, not a GDP calculation, should set the limit.

## Positions

Order note: the first camp is the official position of most governments and international bodies and the largest group in public surveys. The second is the opposite pole named in the topic’s title. The third is the hybrid, and in effect rejects the question as posed: it holds that whether GDP rises or falls is the wrong thing to argue about. It is the most common position among climate policy researchers and about a third of EU citizens fall in the matching cluster, so it sits above the fourth by weight within this debate. The fourth also rejects the question as posed, from the other side: it holds that emissions cuts should be weighed against growth rather than growth against emissions, and it is the governing view of the current United States administration. Developing-country governments’ insistence on their right to grow is shared by all four camps in some form and is noted inside them rather than as a separate camp. Ecomodernism, which holds that growth and innovation are needed to decarbonize, differs from the first camp mainly in emphasis and instrument and is noted inside it. Eco-socialist degrowth is noted inside the second camp.

Camp: Keep growing and cut emissions with clean technology and policy
Adherents: European Commission, “The European Green Deal” (COM(2019) 640, 11 December 2019), https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=COM:2019:640:FIN, describing “a new growth strategy that aims to transform the EU into a fair and prosperous society, with a modern, resource-efficient and competitive economy where there are no net emissions of greenhouse gases in 2050 and where economic growth is decoupled from resource use”; its 2025 progress report reports emissions down over 37 percent since 1990 “all while growing its economy by 71%” (https://climate.ec.europa.eu/eu-action/climate-strategies-targets/progress-climate-action_en). Zeke Hausfather, climate scientist, “Absolute Decoupling of Economic Growth and Emissions in 32 Countries,” Breakthrough Institute (6 April 2021), https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/energy/absolute-decoupling-of-economic-growth-and-emissions-in-32-countries, arguing that “there is no physical law requiring economic growth” to be linked to CO2 emissions. Also: the OECD, which launched its green growth strategy in 2011; the World Bank; Hannah Ritchie, author of Not the End of the World (2024); Nicholas Stern and the Grantham Research Institute at the London School of Economics; the Breakthrough Institute and other ecomodernists.
Case: The problem is carbon, not prosperity. Emissions come from burning coal, oil and gas, and every major use of them now has a clean substitute that is already cheap or getting cheaper: solar and wind for power, electric vehicles for transport, heat pumps for buildings, and electrified or hydrogen-based processes for industry. Replacing these does not require anyone to consume less; it requires building a great deal, which is itself economic activity. Growth is also what pays for the transition and what brought down the cost of clean technology in the first place, through the learning that comes with mass deployment. Most of humanity still needs much more energy and income, and the only way they can get it without wrecking the climate is cheap clean energy, which a growing world economy is delivering. Asking rich countries to shrink would not help them; it would cut the markets, investment and innovation that make clean technology cheap, and it would destroy public support for climate policy. The task is to decarbonize faster, with carbon pricing, standards, public investment and grids, not to give up on growth.
Best evidence: Thirty-five countries cut their emissions over 2015 to 2024 while their economies grew, twice as many as a decade earlier (Global Carbon Project). The EU cut emissions 37 percent since 1990 while its economy grew 71 percent, and the UK’s consumption-based footprint is 29 percent below its 2007 peak (European Commission; Defra). In 2025 clean power met all growth in global electricity demand, China’s emissions fell while its GDP grew 5 percent, and clean energy now attracts nearly twice the investment of fossil fuels (Ember; IEA; China National Bureau of Statistics). The IPCC’s assessed pathways limit warming with world GDP still at least doubling between 2020 and 2050, at a cost of a few percent of GDP in 2050 before counting avoided damage. Solar electricity is about 89 percent cheaper than in 2010 (IRENA via Our World in Data).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that decoupling has been far too slow: about 1.6 percent a year in the rich countries that achieved it in 2013 to 2019, against cuts of 30 percent a year that a fair share of the 1.5°C budget would require. The camp’s answer is that those rates describe the decade before clean technology became the cheapest option, and that the relevant comparison is with the S-curve of adoption now under way in power and cars, which is accelerating. Shrinking GDP is a weak lever by comparison: the 2020 pandemic cut world output about 3 percent and emissions about 5 percent at enormous human cost, while the structural changes in the energy system cut emissions permanently. Where the 1.5°C fair-share arithmetic is unachievable, it is unachievable under degrowth too, and a strategy that requires voters to accept falling incomes will not be sustained long enough to work.
Variation inside the camp: Mainstream economists and the OECD emphasize carbon pricing and market instruments. Industrial-policy supporters, including the architects of the US Inflation Reduction Act and the EU’s Clean Industrial Deal, emphasize public investment and subsidies for clean industry. Ecomodernists such as the Breakthrough Institute stress innovation, nuclear power and abundance, and argue that faster growth makes decarbonization easier. Some in the camp accept a role for demand reduction, such as in aviation or meat, as long as it does not mean targeting lower GDP. Developing-country governments in the camp stress that rich countries must finance clean growth in poorer ones.

Camp: Shrink rich countries’ energy and material use, even if GDP falls
Adherents: Research & Degrowth International, a Barcelona-based research and action group, https://degrowth.org/, which describes its aim as a society that can “thrive without perpetually expanding its economic footprint” and opposes reliance on “green growth”; the movement’s portal degrowth.info defines degrowth as “a reduction in the material size of the global economy, of production and consumption, and redistributing these equitably,” and states that “economic growth” cannot “be decoupled from resource use” (https://degrowth.info/en/degrowth). Jason Hickel, economic anthropologist at the Autonomous University of Barcelona, with Jefim Vogel, “Is green growth happening?” Lancet Planetary Health (September 2023), https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/120169/, concluding that high-income countries “have not achieved green growth, and are very unlikely to be able to achieve it in the future” and “will need to pursue post-growth demand-reduction strategies.” Also: Giorgos Kallis and Julia Steinberger, who with Hickel lead a 9.9 million euro European Research Council project on post-growth pathways; Timothée Parrique; Kohei Saito, author of Slow Down (2024).
Case: Decoupling is real but nowhere near fast enough, and the carbon budget does not wait. Rich countries that have cut emissions while growing have done so by about 1 to 4 percent a year, while their fair share of the remaining budget requires cuts of well over 10 percent a year starting now. Every extra unit of output in a rich economy needs energy that clean supply must also cover, so growth makes the task harder; global material extraction has tripled since 1970 with no sign of absolute decoupling, and carbon is only one of several planetary boundaries being crossed. Rich countries already produce more than enough to give everyone a good life; what they lack is fair distribution. The answer is to scale down the production that does the least for human wellbeing and the most damage, such as SUVs, private jets, fast fashion, advertising-driven consumption and planned obsolescence, while guaranteeing secure livelihoods through shorter working hours, universal public services and job guarantees. This is a planned, democratic and selective reduction in energy and material throughput in rich countries, not a recession, and it is paired with continued growth in poor countries so that the world converges toward a sustainable level of use.
Best evidence: The 11 high-income countries that achieved absolute decoupling in 2013 to 2019 cut consumption-based emissions by an average 1.6 percent a year; at those rates they would take more than 220 years to cut emissions by 95 percent and emit 27 times their fair share of the 1.5°C budget (Vogel and Hickel, 2023). The UK’s territorial emissions halved since 1996 but its consumption-based footprint fell only 15 percent (Defra). Global material extraction rose from 30 to 106.6 billion tonnes between 1970 and 2024 with “no evidence of widespread absolute decoupling at the global level” (International Resource Panel). A systematic review of 835 studies found that observed decoupling rates cannot deliver “large rapid absolute reductions” and must be complemented by “sufficiency-oriented strategies” (Haberl and colleagues, 2020). The IPCC found demand-side measures could cut end-use emissions 40 to 70 percent by 2050.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that deliberately shrinking the economy would mean unemployment, falling public revenue and political backlash, and that no democracy will vote for it. The camp’s answer is that this describes an unplanned recession in an economy built to need growth, not a planned transition. Unemployment can be avoided by sharing work through shorter hours, and essential needs can be secured through public provision rather than rising incomes, which also makes welfare systems less dependent on growth. The objection also assumes that green growth is the safe option, when its own record shows it is not delivering the required cuts; the real risk comparison is between a managed reduction now and an unmanaged one forced by climate breakdown later. Degrowth applies to rich countries and to their most harmful consumption, and its policies, such as shorter working weeks, free public transport and taxes on luxury emissions, are individually popular.
Variation inside the camp: Many degrowth scholars, including Hickel, Kallis and Steinberger, increasingly use “post-growth” for the same program, so labels overlap with the third camp; what distinguishes this camp is that it calls for deliberate reductions in rich countries’ production and consumption. Eco-socialists such as Kohei Saito argue that the reduction requires moving beyond capitalism itself; others see it as compatible with a reformed market economy. Some emphasize local and community economies; others, national planning of provisioning systems.

Camp: Stop targeting growth and pursue climate and wellbeing goals directly
Adherents: Wellbeing Economy Alliance, a global coalition of organizations, governments and individuals, https://weall.org/what-is-wellbeing-economy, which says that “rather than treating economic growth as an end in and of itself and pursuing it at all costs, a Wellbeing Economy puts our human and planetary needs at the centre” and that success should shift “Beyond GDP growth to delivering shared wellbeing.” Jeroen van den Bergh, environmental economist at ICREA and the Autonomous University of Barcelona, “A third option for climate policy within potential limits to growth,” Nature Climate Change (2017), https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate3113, which argues that both green growth and degrowth jeopardize environmental or social goals and proposes “agrowth” to “depolarize the debate and reduce resistance to climate policies.” Also: Kate Raworth and the Doughnut Economics Action Lab, whose principle is to be “agnostic about growth”; Tim Jackson, author of Prosperity without Growth, and the Centre for the Understanding of Sustainable Prosperity; the European Environmental Bureau, whose 2019 report Decoupling Debunked called for complementing efficiency “with sufficiency”. The Wellbeing Economy Governments partnership of Scotland, Iceland, New Zealand, Wales and Finland shares the camp’s wellbeing framing, though its members have not dropped growth as a goal.
Case: The argument over whether GDP can or should grow is a distraction. GDP was never designed to measure welfare, and in rich countries more of it adds little to health, longevity or life satisfaction. Making it the master goal means every climate policy is judged by whether it might slow growth, which is why strong policies get diluted. Making degrowth the goal makes the same mistake in reverse, targeting an indicator instead of outcomes, and it alarms voters needlessly. The sensible course is to set binding targets for what actually matters, emissions, ecological limits, health, security, equality, and pursue them with the best policies available, including strong carbon pricing and regulation, while making the economy resilient to whatever happens to GDP as a result. If GDP keeps growing within those limits, fine; if it stagnates or falls, institutions such as pensions, employment and public finance should be redesigned so that this is not a crisis.
Best evidence: Among 764 climate policy researchers surveyed in 2023, 73 percent preferred agrowth or degrowth to green growth, with agrowth the most common position (King, Savin and Drews). About a third of EU citizens surveyed in 2026 fell in a “growth-indifferent” cluster (Savin, King, van den Bergh and others, Nature Communications). The UN’s 2026 Beyond GDP report, commissioned by member states in the 2024 Pact for the Future, proposes a dashboard to complement GDP because, in its co-chair Nora Lustig’s words, “GDP ignores inequality and poverty. It does not capture environmental degradation.” Growth attitudes among EU citizens are not associated with climate concern or policy support, suggesting climate action does not need to be sold as a growth strategy.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from both sides, is that indifference is not a policy: if the binding constraint is the carbon budget, someone still has to say whether the economy will grow or shrink as a result, and governments facing voters cannot be agnostic about recessions. The camp’s answer is that the outcome for GDP is genuinely uncertain and depends on technology, so committing in advance to growth or to degrowth is a bet the evidence cannot support. What can be decided now is that environmental limits come first and that social systems should be made robust to low or negative growth. Being agnostic is also a political strategy: it lets climate policy be judged on its own results, which the EU survey suggests is how most citizens judge it, rather than tying it to a fight over growth that neither side can win.
Variation inside the camp: Van den Bergh’s agrowth emphasizes standard environmental policy, especially carbon pricing, and is relatively close to green growth in instruments. Raworth’s doughnut economics and the wellbeing economy movement emphasize redesigning economic institutions and measures of progress. Tim Jackson and much of the post-growth literature lean closer to degrowth in expecting that rich economies will in fact have to stop growing. The boundary with the second camp is blurred by shared use of “post-growth.”

Camp: Put growth first and cut emissions only where the benefits exceed the costs
Adherents: US Department of Energy, Secretary Chris Wright, keynote at CERAWeek (10 March 2025), https://www.energy.gov/articles/secretary-energy-chris-wright-delivers-keynote-remarks-ceraweek-2025, stating that the administration “will treat climate change for what it is, a global physical phenomenon that is a side effect of building the modern world,” that “everything in life involves trade-offs,” and that previous climate policies were “impoverishing to our citizens” and that “the cure was far more destructive than the disease.” Bjørn Lomborg, president of the Copenhagen Consensus Center, “Welfare in the 21st century: Increasing development, reducing inequality, the impact of climate change, and the cost of climate policies,” Technological Forecasting and Social Change (July 2020), https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/tefoso/v156y2020ics0040162520304157.html, which states that “common portrayals of devastation are unfounded” and that while IPCC scenarios show human welfare likely rising to 450 percent of today’s level over the century, climate damages “will reduce this welfare increase to 434%,” and which weighs those damages against the costs of climate policies. Also: Alex Epstein, author of Fossil Future (2022); the Copenhagen Consensus Center (https://copenhagenconsensus.com/); many in the US Republican Party; and the argument, made by several developing-country governments, that their right to development comes before emissions constraints.
Case: Growth is the greatest force for human welfare in history: it more than doubled life expectancy, ended mass hunger in most of the world and lifted billions out of poverty, and cheap, reliable energy, still mostly fossil, is what powered it. Climate change is a real cost of that progress, but a manageable one: mainstream estimates put the damage from substantial warming at a few percent of a much larger future world income, and richer societies are far better able to adapt to heat, floods and storms. The question is therefore not whether growth can be made emission-free but what each tonne of emissions avoided is worth compared with what it costs. Some climate policies pass that test, especially research into cheaper clean energy, adaptation and removing methane, and should be pursued. Many do not, including mandates that raise energy prices, and degrowth fails it spectacularly. The poorest people are hurt most by expensive energy, so climate policy should follow growth, not the other way around.
Best evidence: World GDP is projected to at least double between 2020 and 2050 in every pathway the IPCC assessed, and the IPCC itself finds that whether the benefits of limiting warming to 2°C exceed the costs depends on assuming damages are not at the low end and on not discounting the future heavily. Lomborg’s review of the IPCC’s scenarios finds human welfare rising strongly over the century even after climate damage. The 2020 pandemic showed that cutting output by about 3 percent cut emissions by only about 5 percent, at a cost of large job losses. Emissions per person in low-income countries are about 0.3 tonnes a year, a level that reflects energy poverty, not virtue.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that cost-benefit analysis cannot price catastrophic and irreversible risks, such as tipping points, and that it puts low weight on future generations and on the poor countries least responsible for emissions. The camp’s answer is that unbounded precaution is also a choice with victims: every dollar spent on costly emissions cuts is a dollar not spent on health, education, nutrition or adaptation, which save more lives sooner, often in the same poor countries. Catastrophic risks are best insured against by being rich, technologically advanced and adaptable, and by investing in research that makes clean energy cheaper than fossil fuels so that the world switches because it wants to. Growth and innovation are the insurance policy.
Variation inside the camp: Lomborg and the Copenhagen Consensus accept mainstream climate science and favor green energy research and adaptation over mandates and subsidies. The current US administration goes further, withdrawing from the Paris Agreement, promoting fossil fuel production as “energy dominance” and rolling back emissions regulation. Some developing-country governments share the priority on growth without rejecting climate goals, arguing that rich countries should bear the cost of mitigation.

## Crux

The question under the others: can rich economies cut their emissions, and their wider environmental footprint, at the speed the climate goals require while their output keeps growing, and if the two cannot both be had, which should give way: growth, the pace of emissions cuts, or neither, by changing what societies aim for?

What evidence could settle: How fast absolute decoupling can go once clean technology is the cheapest option, which the next decade of data from the power sector, electric transport and heat will show far better than the 2013 to 2019 record both sides now argue over. Whether decoupling holds on consumption-based accounting and extends beyond carbon to materials, land and biodiversity. How large economy-wide rebound effects are when energy itself is clean. How much extra wellbeing additional GDP still buys in rich countries, and whether pensions, employment and public finances can be redesigned to work at low or zero growth without the harms of a recession. Whether policies that reduce energy and material demand can win and keep democratic majorities. Each of these is measurable, and progress on them would move people: sustained annual cuts several times faster than today’s in rich economies that keep growing would strengthen the green growth case, while a decade in which clean energy expands but total emissions barely fall would strengthen the case for reducing demand.

What no data can close: How to weigh the risk of overshooting the carbon budget against the certain costs of slower growth, and so how much precaution is owed to future generations and to the poorest countries, who did least to cause warming and are hit hardest by it. Whether a society should measure its progress by output, by wellbeing, or by staying within ecological limits, and whether fairness requires rich countries to consume less so that poorer ones can consume more. And how much any of these is worth trading against the freedom, opportunity and political stability that growth has historically provided. Evidence can tell each camp what its strategy would achieve and cost; it cannot tell a society which risks to accept and whose welfare to put first.
