# Aging Societies & Pensions — research

## Overview

This topic is about how countries where people live longer and have fewer children pay for old age. It covers three linked questions: the age at which people can claim a full public pension; the finances of the big public pension systems, which are mostly pay-as-you-go (today’s workers’ contributions pay today’s retirees), above all US Social Security and the state pensions of Europe and East Asia; and what working-age adults, retirees and generations not yet working owe one another. The main options in the debate are raising retirement ages, raising payroll taxes or other revenue, slowing the growth of benefits, building invested funds that prefund part of future pensions, and shrinking the public pension toward a basic anti-poverty floor with more personal saving on top. The topic deliberately excludes health spending on older people (Medicare, long-term care) except where it shares the same budget arithmetic, the regulation of private employer pensions, and the funding of US state and local public-employee pension plans. Birth-rate policy, government debt as a whole, and medicine that aims to slow aging have their own pages and appear here only where they bear on who pays for pensions.

Why now: on 9 June 2026 the Social Security trustees reported that the retirement trust fund (Old-Age and Survivors Insurance) will run out in the fourth quarter of 2032, one quarter earlier than the year before, after which incoming taxes would cover only 78 percent of scheduled benefits; the 75-year shortfall grew from 3.82 to 4.42 percent of taxable payroll, mostly because the trustees cut their long-run fertility assumption from 1.90 to 1.75 children per woman, lowered immigration assumptions, and counted lower revenue from the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (2026 Trustees Report, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CDOC-119hdoc163/pdf/CDOC-119hdoc163.pdf; release date per CRFB, https://www.crfb.org/blogs/social-security-and-medicare-trustees-release-2026-reports). On 3 November 2026 Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress for the last two years of Donald Trump’s term (NPR via OPB, https://www.opb.org/article/2026/11/04/2026-midterms-democrats-win-the-senate/), which means any fix before the 2032 deadline will need a Democratic Congress and a Republican president to agree. Two weeks later, on 18 November, the Senate Budget Committee chairman, Ron Johnson, told Fox Business that Social Security is a Ponzi scheme that cannot be fixed and should be shut down, then said at that day’s committee hearing that his wording was imprecise and that he wants a law preventing benefit cuts; Democrats accused him of scaring seniors, and Tim Kaine, expected to chair the committee from January, called for a focus on fixing the program’s finances (Roll Call, https://rollcall.com/2026/11/19/tempers-flare-as-future-of-social-security-debated). Europe is in the same argument. On 23 June 2026 Germany’s pension commission recommended linking the statutory retirement age to life expectancy after 2031 (about 67.5 by 2041), ending penalty-free retirement after 45 contribution years, and adding a mandatory invested pension; Chancellor Friedrich Merz called it one of the “schwierigsten Reformprojekte unserer Zeit” (the most difficult reform projects of our time) and his coalition pledged to implement it (German federal government, https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-de/aktuelles/faq-rentenreform-2444534). In France, the 2023 reform that is raising the legal retirement age from 62 to 64 has been suspended until 1 January 2028 (Service Public, https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/actualites/A18825?lang=en), and on 17 November 2026 Marine Le Pen, who leads polls for the April 2027 presidential election, unveiled a plan to repeal it, let people retire between 60 and 62 depending on when they started work, and add a mandatory funded savings pillar (Euronews, https://fr.euronews.com/2026/11/17/dose-de-capitalisation-depart-de-60-a-62-ans-marine-le-pen-devoile-sa-reforme-des-retraite; Brussels Signal, https://brusselssignal.eu/2026/11/le-pen-vows-to-undo-macrons-pension-reform-and-create-mandatory-retirement-fund/).

The debate is live because the arithmetic is no longer distant. In the United States there were 5.1 covered workers per Social Security beneficiary in 1960, 2.6 in 2025, and the trustees project 2.3 by 2035; across rich countries the number of people aged 65 and over is rising much faster than the number of working age, and governments from Denmark (retirement at 70 from 2040) to China (raising its retirement ages for the first time since the 1950s) are legislating later retirement, in France’s case after months of mass protest in 2023. Every option shifts a cost onto someone: workers through higher taxes, retirees through smaller or later benefits, the young through debt, or everyone through market risk in invested funds. The participants are pension trustees and actuaries, finance and labor ministries, independent bodies such as France’s Conseil d’orientation des retraites and Germany’s pension commission, the OECD and IMF, labor unions such as the AFL-CIO, Germany’s DGB and France’s CGT, retiree advocates such as AARP and Social Security Works, budget-focused groups such as the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, free-market think tanks such as the Cato Institute and the American Enterprise Institute, progressive ones such as the Roosevelt Institute and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, academic centers such as the Center for Retirement Research at Boston College and the Penn Wharton Budget Model, and parties of every stripe, from populists on the left and right who promise no cuts to centrists who argue for gradual increases in the retirement age.

Context: Running out of reserves does not mean Social Security stops paying. Under current law the program can pay only what its income covers, so benefits would be cut automatically, across the board, to about 78 percent of what the benefit formula promises. The trustees put the retirement fund’s depletion in the fourth quarter of 2032, one quarter earlier than in their 2025 report. The disability fund is projected to stay solvent for the full 75 years, so if Congress merged the two funds, as it has done in the past, the combined reserves would last to the third quarter of 2034 and then pay 83 percent. The Center for Retirement Research at Boston College notes that because merging requires a change in law, 2032 is the date that forces action, “six years from now,” and that the replacement rate for a typical retiree at 65 would fall from about 36 percent to about 29 percent of pre-retirement earnings (Alicia Munnell, June 2026, https://crr.bc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/IB_26-12-1.pdf). The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget describes the 2032 cut as 22 percent when today’s youngest retirees turn 68, growing to 38 percent by 2100 (https://www.crfb.org/blogs/social-security-and-medicare-trustees-release-2026-reports). In December 2025 Social Security paid benefits to 70 million people: 56 million retired workers and their dependents, 8 million disabled workers and their dependents, and 6 million survivors.

Note: Paying every scheduled benefit for the next 75 years would take extra money equal to 4.42 percent of all earnings subject to the Social Security tax, up from 3.82 percent a year earlier.
Context: The actuarial deficit is the average amount by which income would have to rise, or costs fall, over 75 years to pay every scheduled benefit and end with a one-year reserve. In present-value dollars the trustees put the 75-year unfunded obligation at $29.3 trillion, also equal to 1.5 percent of GDP over the period. The trustees say the cut in their fertility assumption, from 1.90 to 1.75 children per woman, is “the largest contributor to the significantly increased deficit”; lower assumed levels of temporary and unauthorized immigration and the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, whose lower income tax rates and extra deduction for people over 65 reduce the income tax paid on benefits that flows to the trust funds, also worsened it, while faster assumed near-term productivity and wage growth helped. The 1983 law, passed on the recommendations of the Greenspan Commission, raised payroll taxes, began taxing benefits and phased the full retirement age up from 65 to 67; the trustees then projected a small surplus. The Center for Retirement Research attributes more than half of the drift since then to the 75-year window moving forward a year at a time and picking up years of deficit, with worse economic and demographic assumptions accounting for most of the rest. The Penn Wharton Budget Model, using its own model rather than the trustees’, puts the 75-year shortfall at 4.78 percent of payroll (https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/p/2026-09-21-seven-social-security-reform-packages-effects-on-growth-benefits-and-generations/).

Context: The payroll tax is split evenly: 6.2 percent paid by the employee and 6.2 percent by the employer, or 12.4 percent by the self-employed, on earnings up to a cap, the contribution and benefit base, which is $184,500 in 2026 and $192,300 in 2027 (2027 figure from the Social Security Administration’s 14 October 2026 cost-of-living announcement, https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-10-14.html, read via search extract because ssa.gov blocks automated fetching). The trustees present these figures as an illustration of the gap’s size, not a recommendation; they note that “other equivalent combinations of increased revenue and/or reduced benefits” would also work. That trend is the basis of proposals to raise or remove the cap, such as the June 2026 plan by Senators Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Moreno. In 2025 Social Security took in $1,449 billion, 91.3 percent of it from payroll taxes, and spent $1,609 billion.

## Questions

Question: Should retirement ages rise as people live longer?
Tag: value
Poles: Rise with longevity / Hold current ages
Why contested: Life expectancy at 65 has risen by six to seven years in the United States since 1940 while the full retirement age has risen by two, and a growing number of countries, from Denmark to China, are pushing pension ages up. But the gains are uneven: the richest Americans live a decade or more longer than the poorest, and an older pension age is a benefit cut that weighs most on people with shorter lives and physically hard jobs. The disagreement is less over the averages than over what fairness requires when the averages hide so much.
Case for “Rise with longevity”: Those who favor raising the age argue that a pension age set when retirement lasted about 12 or 13 years cannot be held fixed when it lasts 18 to 21, and that linking the age to life expectancy keeps the share of adult life spent in retirement roughly stable without anyone having to cut the monthly benefit. They point to evidence that people are not just living longer but staying healthier: IMF economists found that “a person who was 70 in 2022 had the same cognitive health score as a 53-year-old in 2000” and estimate that better labor outcomes for people over 50 could add about 0.4 percentage point a year to global growth through 2050 (IMF Finance & Development, June 2025, https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2025/06/sustaining-growth-in-an-aging-world-bertrand-gruss). France’s pension council finds that raising the effective retirement age, unlike cutting pensions or raising contributions, increases employment and output in every model it commissioned (COR, June 2026). A later age, they add, is the one lever that improves both sides of the ledger at once: more years of contributions and fewer years of benefits. Hardship is better handled by targeted disability and long-career exceptions than by holding the age down for everyone.
Case for “Hold current ages”: Those who oppose raising the age argue that it is the most regressive way to cut pensions, because it takes the most, as a share of lifetime benefits, from people with the shortest lives, who are disproportionately lower earners; the Chetty study found the gap in life expectancy between the richest and poorest 1 percent at 14.6 years for men and widening (JAMA 2016). Many people do not choose when they stop working: a ProPublica and Urban Institute analysis of the Health and Retirement Study found that 56 percent of Americans who reach their 50s in steady jobs are laid off or pushed out at least once before they choose to retire (https://www.propublica.org/article/older-workers-united-states-pushed-out-of-work-forced-retirement). For a cleaner, a nurse or a roofer, a later pension age often means more years on disability or unemployment, not more years of work. Germany’s union federation states flatly that “Das Rentenalter darf nicht angehoben werden” (the retirement age must not be raised) and argues that every increase falls mainly on those who already cannot reach the current age in work (DGB, June 2026). They prefer to fund the extra years of retirement as a collective gain of a richer society.

Question: Should the funding gap be closed mainly with new revenue or mainly with benefit changes?
Tag: value
Poles: Mainly new revenue / Mainly benefit changes
Why contested: Social Security’s 75-year gap equals raising the payroll tax from 12.40 to 16.65 percent now, or cutting every benefit by 25.2 percent, or any mix of the two (2026 Trustees Report). The arithmetic does not choose; the answer depends on how large a public pension people want, who should pay, and disputed evidence on how each choice affects the economy. The same split runs through Europe, where the choice is between higher contributions and slower-growing or later pensions.
Case for “Mainly new revenue”: Those who favor revenue argue that Social Security’s promised benefits are modest, averaging about $2,163 a month for a retired worker in January 2027 (Social Security Administration figure, reported by NAPA, https://www.napa-net.org/news/2026/10/breaking-2027-social-security-cola-largest-increase-in-5-years/), and that cutting them would push more older people into poverty at a time when poverty among Americans over 65 is rising. The gap grew, they argue, partly because a rising share of earnings escaped the tax cap and partly because of tax laws such as the 2025 act; asking high earners to pay the same rate on all their wages, as Senators Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Moreno propose, would close much of the gap without touching benefits. They note that the overwhelming majority of Americans in both parties oppose cuts (Pew 2024), and that a tax increase of a few points phased in over years is a small price for keeping a promise people planned their lives around.
Case for “Mainly benefit changes”: Those who favor benefit changes argue that the gap comes from the program’s design meeting a new demography, so the design must change: benefits for each new cohort of retirees grow with wages, faster than prices, and people collect them for longer. Raising payroll taxes by a third, they say, would fall on young workers who already face higher housing and debt costs and would weaken growth. In the Penn Wharton Budget Model’s September 2026 comparison, packages that rely more on slowing benefit growth raise GDP much more over the long run than the all-tax package, which lowers GDP slightly through 2050. They also argue that lifting the cap alone does not close the gap, because the extra taxes would also raise the benefits of high earners unless the formula changed, and that trimming growth mainly for higher earners can protect the poorest retirees.

Question: Can most people keep working into their late sixties?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Most can work longer / Many cannot
Why contested: Whether raising the pension age is fair depends heavily on whether people are able to work to that age. Health in later life has improved on average, but averages are pulled up by educated, high-income workers in desk jobs, and employers often push older workers out regardless of their health. The evidence exists on both sides; the dispute is over which part of it describes the typical worker.
Case for “Most can work longer”: Those who expect longer working lives point to measured gains in cognitive and physical health, such as grip strength and lung capacity, documented by the IMF, and to the rising employment of people in their sixties across rich countries as pension ages have risen. Work has become less physically demanding over decades, and part-time and flexible work makes a gradual exit possible. Where pension ages have risen, from Germany’s move to 67 to Denmark’s indexation, employment among older people has risen with them, which suggests that many leave work at the pension age because of the rules rather than because they cannot continue.
Case for “Many cannot”: Those who doubt it argue that the averages mislead. Gains in life expectancy and health have been concentrated among higher earners (Chetty and colleagues, JAMA 2016), and many workers in manual and care jobs are worn out well before 67. The ProPublica and Urban Institute analysis found that more than half of older workers in long-held jobs are pushed out before they choose to leave, and that only about one in ten of them ever again earns as much. The IMF article itself notes that many workers leave the labor force after 50, well before the statutory age. For these workers, they argue, a later pension age means a longer gap between losing work and receiving a pension.

Question: Who should bear more of the adjustment, people near or in retirement or younger and future workers?
Tag: value
Poles: Older generations share more / Protect those near retirement
Why contested: Every package that closes the gap shifts costs across generations. The Penn Wharton Budget Model finds that “every package produces losses for older current workers and near-retirees and gains for future households”; a middle-income household aged 60 in 2027 loses between $2,460 and $80,645 depending on the package, while one born in 2052 gains between $27,725 and $306,866. Most reform plans protect people already retired, which makes the cut larger for everyone else: in the trustees’ illustration, a 25.2 percent cut for all beneficiaries becomes 30.3 percent if current beneficiaries are spared. Germany fought over exactly this in late 2025, when the Junge Gruppe of younger CDU and CSU members of parliament objected to holding the pension level at 48 percent to 2031 because of the cost to their generation.
Case for “Older generations share more”: Those who want older generations to carry more argue that the baby boom generation received generous returns on its contributions, benefited from decades of rising asset prices, and is better off on average than younger cohorts at the same age, while federal spending per older American is about 5.8 times spending per child (Peterson Foundation, citing the Urban Institute, 2026). Sparing everyone over a certain age, they argue, loads the whole adjustment onto people who had no say in the promises and who also face the larger national debt. Modest changes for current retirees, such as a slightly lower cost-of-living adjustment for those with high incomes or more income tax on high benefits, would spread the burden across more people.
Case for “Protect those near retirement”: Those who would protect people near retirement argue that a pension is a promise on which people have built their lives, and that someone at 60 cannot go back and save more or work in a different job; younger workers have decades to adjust. Cutting benefits for current retirees, many of whom are old, widowed and living mainly on Social Security, would be cruel and politically impossible. They also note that the comparison between generations misleads: today’s retirees paid the payroll taxes that financed their parents’ benefits, and each generation both pays and receives. The 1983 reform, often cited as a model, phased in its increase in the retirement age over decades precisely so that no one near retirement was affected.

Question: Should public pensions be partly prefunded by investing in markets?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Invest for higher returns / Keep pay-as-you-go
Why contested: Pay-as-you-go pensions earn, in effect, the growth rate of wages and the workforce, which aging is slowing. Investing a fund in stocks and other assets promises higher returns but brings market risk, transition costs and, when the fund is financed by borrowing, a bet that returns will beat the government’s interest rate. Countries have tried both: Canada, Sweden, Japan and Norway hold large public pension or reserve funds invested in markets, Chile and Australia built systems around individual accounts, while US Social Security’s reserves are held entirely in special-issue Treasury securities, which earned an effective 2.6 percent in 2025 (2026 Trustees Report).
Case for “Invest for higher returns”: Those who favor prefunding argue that a fund invested over decades can earn far more than Treasury bonds and so reduce the taxes or cuts later generations face. Senators Bill Cassidy and Tim Kaine propose borrowing $1.5 trillion for a separate fund invested for 75 years that they estimate could grow to about $30.6 trillion, while promising that no one on Social Security or nearing retirement would see any change in benefits; they say the approach “matches the strategy most nations use to fund their retirement programs” (Cassidy, July 2025, https://www.cassidy.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/icymi-cassidy-outlines-plan-to-save-social-security-in-op-ed). Germany’s pension commission recommended a mandatory funded pension modeled on Sweden’s, financed by an extra 2 percent of wages, and France’s Rassemblement National now proposes a mandatory funded pillar as well. Canada’s pension plan, reformed in the late 1990s to build an invested reserve, is their leading example of a public system that prefunding has stabilized.
Case for “Keep pay-as-you-go”: Those who doubt prefunding argue that it does not create money from nothing. Borrowing to invest in stocks only pays if returns beat interest costs over the whole period, which is a bet the government makes with retirees’ security; the Center for Retirement Research concluded that the Cassidy-Kaine plan would do “virtually nothing” to solve Social Security’s financing problems (https://crr.bc.edu/the-cassidy-kaine-proposal-does-virtually-nothing-to-solve-social-securitys-financing-problems/), and the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget argues that “a sovereign debt fund can’t save Social Security” (https://www.crfb.org/blogs/sovereign-debt-fund-cant-save-social-security). Building a fund while paying current retirees means a transition generation pays twice. A crash just before a cohort retires can cut its pensions sharply, as individual-account systems learned in 2008. And a large government fund invested in private companies raises questions about political control of corporate investment. Germany’s union federation argues that the debate over funded pensions distracts from the real need to raise the statutory pension level.

Question: Should a public pension replace a share of earnings or mainly prevent poverty?
Tag: value
Poles: Replace earnings / Prevent poverty
Why contested: Social Security and most European state pensions pay more to people who earned more, replacing a share of past wages; others, such as New Zealand’s, pay everyone a flat amount, and Australia means-tests its public pension and relies on mandatory private savings for the rest. Moving toward a flat or targeted benefit would close much of a funding gap, but it would change what the program is and who supports it.
Case for “Replace earnings”: Those who favor earnings-related benefits argue that a pension people earn by working, in proportion to what they paid in, is why social insurance is popular across classes and parties, and why it has survived for more than 90 years. A program only for the poor, they say, becomes a program with poor political support. Earnings replacement also protects the middle class from a collapse in living standards at retirement, which private saving has not reliably done: only about half of US private-sector workers participate in a workplace plan (BLS, March 2026). The Brookings authors Wendell Primus and colleagues warn that flat-benefit proposals would “shift Social Security from being a wage replacement program to a poverty reduction program,” with deep cuts for many future middle-income retirees (Brookings, January 2026).
Case for “Prevent poverty”: Those who favor a flatter benefit argue that public money should go first to the people who need it, and that paying the largest checks to the highest earners is an expensive way to prevent poverty. The Cato Institute cites a Congressional Budget Office estimate that replacing Social Security’s earnings-related formula with a flat benefit at 125 percent of the poverty line would eliminate the long-term shortfall, and argues that middle and upper earners can save for themselves through workplace and personal accounts, which give them ownership and the ability to leave wealth to their heirs (Cato, read via search extract, https://www.cato.org/blog/doge-recommendations-social-security). A smaller public promise would also free young workers from a payroll tax that they argue offers them poor returns.

Question: Can economic growth, immigration or higher birth rates close the gap?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Growth can close much of it / No substitute for reform
Why contested: Pension finances depend on how many people work and how much they earn, so faster productivity growth, more immigration or more births all help. The question is how much, and whether any of them can be counted on. The trustees’ own sensitivity tests give the size of each effect.
Case for “Growth can close much of it”: Those who look to growth argue that the trustees’ projections are not destiny: each extra 0.1 percentage point of real wage growth improves the 75-year balance by about 0.19 percent of payroll, each extra 0.1 in the fertility rate by about 0.22, and each additional 100,000 net immigrants a year by about 0.10 (2026 Trustees Report). Advances in artificial intelligence and automation, they argue, could raise productivity well above the trustees’ assumption of 1.63 percent a year, and immigration is the fastest lever of all, because immigrants arrive mostly at working age. The 2026 report’s deficit grew mainly because the trustees cut their fertility and immigration assumptions, showing how much these factors matter. Policies that raise employment among women and older people, as in Germany and Japan, can enlarge the workforce further. Some on the populist right in both the United States and Europe argue that growth and cutting other spending, not tax increases or benefit cuts, are the answer.
Case for “No substitute for reform”: Those who doubt it point to the same tables. Even under the trustees’ optimistic scenarios, real wage growth of 1.74 percent a year leaves a 75-year deficit of 3.27 percent of payroll, a fertility rate of 2.1 leaves 3.67, and net immigration averaging 1.62 million a year leaves 4.00 (2026 Trustees Report). Growth helps less than people expect because Social Security’s starting benefits are indexed to wages, so faster wage growth raises future benefits too. Higher fertility takes two decades to add workers and, in the short run, slightly raises costs. And immigrants themselves age and earn benefits. Every one of these levers, they argue, is worth pursuing, but none is under the control of pension policy or large enough alone, so waiting for them only shrinks the time left to phase in changes.

Question: Should pensions adjust automatically or only by new political decisions?
Tag: value
Poles: Automatic rules / Elected choices
Why contested: Several countries have built automatic adjustments into their pension systems: Sweden’s balancing mechanism trims indexation when liabilities outrun assets, Japan’s macroeconomic slide slows benefit growth as the workforce shrinks, Germany’s sustainability factor ties pension increases to the ratio of contributors to pensioners, and Denmark and other countries link pension ages to life expectancy. US Social Security has no such mechanism; when reserves run out, the only automatic rule is an abrupt across-the-board cut. Whether hard choices should be made in advance by formula or each time by elected politicians is itself contested.
Case for “Automatic rules”: Those who favor automatic adjustment argue that it depoliticizes the hardest decisions and spreads small changes over many years and many cohorts, instead of forcing a crisis every few decades; the trustees stress that early, gradual changes let more generations share the cost. A rule set in advance is easier to plan around than a deal struck at the last minute. Germany’s pension commission recommended restoring the sustainability factor after 2031 and linking the retirement age to life expectancy by formula, and the OECD reports that one in four of its member countries now links retirement ages to life expectancy.
Case for “Elected choices”: Those who prefer political decisions argue that a pension is a social bargain whose terms voters should set openly, not a formula that cuts benefits quietly every year. Automatic mechanisms can be suspended when they bite, as Germany did with its sustainability factor through the 48 percent floor to 2031, and Denmark’s prime minister Mette Frederiksen told Berlingske in August 2024, “We no longer believe that the retirement age should be increased automatically,” even as her government went on to legislate age 70 by 2040 (AFP via The Local Denmark, https://www.thelocal.dk/20250711/danes-reluctant-to-embrace-retirement-at-70). Formulas also build in assumptions, such as that longer average lives justify later retirement for everyone, that hide unequal effects. Better, they argue, that each generation decide openly how to share the cost.

## Positions

Camp: Keep promised benefits and retirement ages, and raise the revenue to pay for them
Adherents:
- Social Security Works (institution, US retiree advocacy group): its platform is “Expand, Don’t Cut,” stating that “Benefits should be strengthened to keep pace with the real cost of living, not slashed in backroom deals,” and it opposes privatization. https://socialsecurityworks.org/about/
- Deutscher Gewerkschaftsbund, DGB (institution, Germany’s union federation): in its June 2026 response to the pension commission it states “Das Rentenalter darf nicht angehoben werden” (the retirement age must not be raised) and calls for raising the pension level to at least 50 and later 53 percent, financed partly by taxing high incomes, large fortunes and capital income more. https://www.dgb.de/fileadmin/download_center/Uploads/260626_DGB-Rentenkommission-Empfehlungen.pdf
- Roosevelt Institute (institution, US progressive think tank): its president Elizabeth Wilkins responded to the 2026 Trustees Report that “the answer cannot be to cut benefits for the people who rely on them,” warned against raising the retirement age, and called for the wealthy to contribute more (9 June 2026). https://rooseveltinstitute.org/press-releases/trustees-report-strengthen-social-security-dont-cut-it/
- Senators Elizabeth Warren (Democrat) and Bernie Moreno (Republican): in a joint June 2026 New York Times op-ed they proposed removing the cap on Social Security taxes, arguing that the wealthiest Americans “should contribute the same percentage of their income as a factory worker in Chillicothe, Ohio.” https://www.moreno.senate.gov/press-releases/moreno-warren-nyt-op-ed-lift-the-social-security-cap/
- France’s CGT union (institution): demands repeal of the 2023 reform and a return to retirement at 60, funded by phasing out exemptions from social contributions (CGT congress documents, 2025 to 2026; read via search extract). https://54.cgt.fr/sites/54.cgt.fr/files/2026-04/54eCongres_DocOrientation_RVB.pdf
Variation inside the camp: The left wing of the camp wants to expand benefits and pay for it with higher taxes on high earners, capital income or employers (Social Security Works, the Roosevelt Institute, the DGB, the CGT, Representative John Larson’s Social Security 2100 Act). A populist-right wing promises to protect benefits and retirement ages but rejects tax increases, looking instead to growth, cutting other spending and fighting fraud: Donald Trump pledged in his 2024 campaign not to cut Social Security or raise the retirement age (PolitiFact’s promise tracker, https://politifact.com/truth-o-meter/promises/maga-meter-tracking-donald-trumps-2024-promises/promise/387502/leave-social-security-and-medicare-unchanged/article/387505/), and France’s Rassemblement National promises to undo the move to 64 (its newer funded pillar puts it partly in the third camp below). The two wings differ on who pays, not on whether benefits and ages should hold.
Case: Pensions are not a gift; they are earned insurance that workers paid for, and the promise of a secure retirement at a known age is what people plan their working lives around. The so-called crisis is a modest, predictable gap: in the United States it is about 1.5 percent of GDP over 75 years, in a country that spends far less on public pensions than France or Italy. Richer societies can afford to support people for the extra years they now live; that is what economic growth is for. The gap opened largely because a growing share of income escaped the payroll tax cap or was taxed lightly as capital income, and because of tax cuts for high earners, so that is where the money should come from. Raising the retirement age is the most regressive possible cut, because lower earners have gained least in life expectancy, and many older workers are pushed out of jobs long before the official age. Cutting benefits would push more older people into poverty at a time when poverty among Americans over 65 is already rising.
Best evidence: Social Security moved 28.8 million Americans out of poverty in 2025, more than any other program (Census Bureau, September 2026). The share of US covered earnings subject to the payroll tax fell from 88.6 percent in 1984 to about 83 percent, as earnings above the cap grew fastest (2026 Trustees Report). Life expectancy at 40 differs by 14.6 years between the richest and poorest 1 percent of American men, and the gap widened from 2001 to 2014 (Chetty and colleagues, JAMA 2016). 79 percent of Americans, including 77 percent of Republicans, say benefits should not be reduced in any way (Pew, 2024). Even after the 2026 revision, the full 75-year gap equals about 1.5 percent of GDP.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that funding everything with taxes would require raising the payroll tax by about a third and would load the cost onto younger workers and slow the economy. The camp answers that the increase need not fall on ordinary workers: removing or raising the cap, as Warren and Moreno propose, asks the highest earners to pay the same rate everyone else already pays, and taxes on capital income and large fortunes can supply the rest. They argue that most growth models overstate the harm of taxes and ignore the economic cost of retirees losing a fifth of their income at once; that young workers are the ones who most need Social Security to be there when they retire; and that the United States collects less in taxes than most rich democracies that pay comparable or larger pensions. Holding ages steady, they add, is not denial of aging but a choice to share its cost collectively rather than through later retirement that falls on those least able to bear it.

Camp: Raise retirement ages as lives lengthen and share the rest of the gap through benefits and taxes
Adherents:
- International Monetary Fund (institution): IMF economists Bertrand Gruss and Diaa Noureldin write that policies should “incentivize a gradual rise in the effective retirement age in line with improvements in life expectancy,” noting that “a person who was 70 in 2022 had the same cognitive health score as a 53-year-old in 2000” (Finance & Development, June 2025). https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2025/06/sustaining-growth-in-an-aging-world-bertrand-gruss
- Germany’s Alterssicherungskommission and the Merz government (institutions): the commission’s 23 June 2026 report recommends that the statutory retirement age “nach 2031 moderat an die steigende Lebenserwartung angepasst werden” (be adjusted moderately to rising life expectancy after 2031), with gains split 2:1 between working and retirement years, the end of penalty-free retirement after 45 contribution years and a restored sustainability factor; the governing coalition pledged to implement it. https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-de/aktuelles/faq-rentenreform-2444534
- Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (institution, US budget watchdog): urges “trust fund solutions sooner rather than later” combining cost reductions and revenue, and its leaders’ Pro-Growth Social Security Reform framework would raise the retirement age to reflect longer lifespans as part of a mix of revenue and benefit changes. https://www.crfb.org/blogs/social-security-and-medicare-trustees-release-2026-reports ; https://www.crfb.org/blogs/social-security-reform-can-boost-incomes-grow-economy
- Denmark’s parliament (institution): voted 81 to 21 on 22 May 2025 to raise the state pension age to 70 by 2040 under a system indexed to life expectancy since 2006. https://report.az/en/amp/other-countries/denmark-raises-retirement-age-to-70-by-2040
Variation inside the camp: Members differ on how much of the gap to close with revenue rather than benefits, from roughly half and half (plans cited by the Brookings authors Wendell Primus and colleagues) to mostly benefit changes, and on how to protect lower earners: indexing the age to life expectancy for everyone (Denmark), splitting gains between work and retirement (Germany’s 2:1 rule), raising the age only for higher earners (Primus and colleagues, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/insufficient-financing-should-not-provoke-dramatic-changes-to-social-security/), or pairing a later age with a stronger minimum benefit and hardship exits. France’s 2023 reform under President Emmanuel Macron, which raises the legal age from 62 to 64 and is now suspended until 2028, is the camp’s most contested recent example.
Case: When pensions began, most people did not live long past the retirement age; today a 65-year-old American can expect 18 to 21 more years, and Europeans more. A pension system that never adjusts its age to longer lives is quietly promising each generation a longer retirement paid for by fewer workers. Working somewhat longer is the fairest way to share the cost of longer life, because it splits the gain in years between work and rest rather than cutting monthly checks or raising taxes on the young. It is also the only lever that strengthens both sides of the ledger and the economy as a whole. The rest of the gap should be closed with a balanced mix of slower benefit growth for higher earners and new revenue, phased in early and gradually so that no one near retirement is caught by surprise and the burden is spread across many generations rather than dumped on the last ones in line.
Best evidence: US life expectancy at 65 rose from 11.9 years for men and 13.4 for women in 1940 to 18.2 and 20.7 in 2025, while the full retirement age rose only from 65 to 67 (2026 Trustees Report). Across the OECD there are 33 people aged 65 and over per 100 aged 20 to 64, projected to reach 52 by 2050 (OECD 2025). France’s pension council found in models from the Treasury, OFCE and Cepremap and the Paris School of Economics that raising the effective retirement age increases labor supply and output, while pension cuts and higher contributions slow activity in the short run (COR, June 2026). The trustees themselves recommend acting early so that “more generations” share the needed changes.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a higher retirement age is regressive, because the poor live shorter lives and many manual workers cannot work longer. The camp answers that the objection is about design, not principle. Disability insurance, long-career and hardship exits, and a higher minimum benefit can protect those who cannot work, as Germany’s commission proposes with its hardship rules; the age can be raised only partly in line with longevity, as the 2:1 rule does; and the alternative, higher payroll taxes, also falls on low earners, who pay the tax on every dollar they earn. They add that holding the age fixed while lives lengthen redistributes most to those who live longest, who are disproportionately well-off, so a fixed age is not as progressive as it looks.

Camp: Build invested pension funds alongside pay-as-you-go benefits
Adherents:
- Senators Bill Cassidy (Republican) and Tim Kaine (Democrat): propose a $1.5 trillion fund, invested for 75 years in parallel to the trust fund rather than replacing it, to supplement payroll taxes, promising that “no one on Social Security or nearing retirement sees any change to the benefits whatsoever” (op-ed, July 2025). https://www.cassidy.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/icymi-cassidy-outlines-plan-to-save-social-security-in-op-ed
- Rassemblement National (institution, French party): on 17 November 2026 Marine Le Pen proposed a mandatory funded pillar for all workers under 52, managed by the state-owned Caisse des dépôts and fed by at least 12 billion euros a year of redirected employer levies, which she says would build a fund of nearly 900 billion euros within a generation. https://fr.euronews.com/2026/11/17/dose-de-capitalisation-depart-de-60-a-62-ans-marine-le-pen-devoile-sa-reforme-des-retraite
- Germany’s Alterssicherungskommission (institution): recommended a mandatory, capital-market-funded supplementary pension for all employees on the Swedish model, financed by an extra 2 percent of gross wages split between employers and employees and invested through a state-run fund (ZDF, 22 June 2026). https://www.zdfheute.de/politik/deutschland/rente-kommission-bericht-eintrittsalter-beitragszahler-100.html
Variation inside the camp: Some want a single collective fund owned by the public system, as in the Cassidy-Kaine plan, Canada’s pension plan, Japan’s pension reserve fund and Norway’s funds; others want mandatory individual accounts inside the public system, as in Sweden’s premium pension, the German commission’s proposal and the Rassemblement National plan. The camp also splits on financing: borrowing to invest now (Cassidy-Kaine), redirecting existing levies (Rassemblement National), or adding a new contribution (Germany). Its members disagree with each other about retirement ages, from Le Pen’s 60 to 62 to the German commission’s later ages; what unites them is the claim that pay-as-you-go alone can no longer carry the load.
Case: A pay-as-you-go pension earns, in effect, the growth rate of the workforce and wages, and aging societies are running that rate down. Money invested in a diversified portfolio over decades has historically earned much more. Putting part of pension financing on invested capital makes future pensions depend less on how many children were born 25 years earlier, and lets today’s workers build real assets for tomorrow’s retirees instead of only paying for today’s. Done collectively, with professional management and a long horizon, it can close part of the gap without the tax increases or benefit cuts that voters reject. Most rich countries already do some of this; the United States, which holds its reserves entirely in Treasury bonds earning 2.6 percent, is the outlier.
Best evidence: The trustees report that the trust funds’ reserves earned an effective annual interest rate of 2.6 percent in 2025, held entirely in special-issue Treasury securities (2026 Trustees Report). Cassidy and Kaine estimate their $1.5 trillion could grow to about $30.6 trillion over 75 years (reported in coverage of the plan; [unverified] against a primary document). The US already runs a precedent: the National Railroad Retirement Investment Trust, created in 2001, invests railroad workers’ pension reserves in markets (Cassidy op-ed). Canada reformed its national pension plan in the late 1990s to build an invested reserve, and Sweden’s public system has included mandatory individual premium-pension accounts since its reform of the 1990s. Even the plan’s critic at the Center for Retirement Research, Alicia Munnell, notes that comparable pension plans abroad are funded by taxes or contributions rather than debt, which is the model the German and French proposals follow (CRR, 9 April 2026).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that borrowing to invest is a gamble that does little for solvency: the Center for Retirement Research concluded the Cassidy-Kaine plan “does virtually nothing” to solve Social Security’s financing problems, and the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget argues a sovereign debt fund “can’t save Social Security.” The camp answers that prefunding was never meant to be the whole solution, only the part that reduces how much has to come from cuts and taxes; that over 75 years the gap between equity returns and government borrowing costs has historically been large; and that the risk is pooled across generations by a fund that never has to sell in a crash, unlike an individual saver. Where the fund is financed by contributions rather than borrowing, as in the German and French proposals, there is no borrowing bet at all, only the choice to save now rather than pay later.

Camp: Shrink public pensions to an anti-poverty floor and expand personal saving
Adherents:
- Cato Institute (institution, US libertarian think tank): recommends moving Social Security to a flat benefit focused on preventing poverty rather than replacing income, citing a Congressional Budget Office estimate that a flat benefit at 125 percent of the poverty line would eliminate the long-term shortfall, alongside slower benefit growth and more private saving (Cato blog, December 2024, and its 2025 book Reimagining Social Security; read via search extract because cato.org blocks automated fetching). https://www.cato.org/blog/doge-recommendations-social-security
- Andrew Biggs (senior fellow, American Enterprise Institute): describes converting Social Security to a flat benefit at 125 or 150 percent of the federal poverty threshold, which he says would “eliminate poverty among seniors and the disabled” and “(easily) address Social Security’s long-term funding gap,” while higher-income households are “more than capable of saving for retirement on their own” (North State Journal reprint, 25 May 2025). http://nsjonline.com/article/2025/05/biggs-what-might-a-flat-benefit-social-security-reform-look-like/
Variation inside the camp: Some favor voluntary saving with tax incentives and automatic enrollment in workplace plans; others favor mandatory personal accounts, as in Australia’s superannuation system paired with a means-tested public pension, or Chile’s individual-account system. Some would means-test the public benefit; others would pay a universal flat amount, as New Zealand does. Senate Budget Committee chairman Ron Johnson said in November 2026 that if the program had been privatized from its inception, “we’d be in a lot better shape today,” though at the same hearing he proposed a law to prevent benefit cuts (Spectrum News, 18 November 2026, https://spectrumnews1.com/wi/madison/politics/2026/11/18/sen-ron-johnson-social-security-budget-hearing); few elected officials now propose personal accounts for Social Security outright.
Case: Government’s job in retirement is to make sure no one grows old in poverty, not to replace the wages of people who could save for themselves. Today’s earnings-related systems pay the largest checks to the highest earners, who also live longest, and finance them with a payroll tax on young workers, many of whom earn less than the retirees they support. A flat floor set above the poverty line would raise benefits for the poorest retirees, close the funding gap, and leave middle- and higher-income workers to save in accounts they own, can invest, and can pass on to their families. Personal saving builds real capital and does not depend on a political promise that the next generation of voters must keep. Countries such as Australia and New Zealand show that a flat or means-tested public pension combined with private saving can deliver adequate retirement incomes at lower public cost.
Best evidence: Biggs reports that a flat benefit at 125 or 150 percent of the poverty threshold would raise benefits for the bottom quarter or third of beneficiaries and close the long-term gap (North State Journal, 2025, citing Congressional Budget Office analysis). In the Penn Wharton Budget Model’s 2026 comparison, the package relying entirely on benefit reductions raises GDP by 6.5 percent by 2050 and 12.1 percent by 2060, compared with a small loss to 2050 under the all-tax package, because people save and work more. Seven in ten US private-sector workers already have access to a workplace retirement plan (BLS, March 2026). Federal spending per American aged 65 and over is about 5.8 times spending per child (Peterson Foundation, citing the Urban Institute).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a flat benefit would gut the middle class’s retirement security and destroy the political coalition that sustains Social Security, turning it into a welfare program. The camp answers that a well-designed floor would be phased in over decades, so no current retiree or near-retiree would be affected, and that middle earners would gain an account they own in place of a promise that is already set to be cut by about a fifth in 2032 under current law. Programs for everyone, they argue, can stay popular even when they are flat: New Zealand’s universal pension is both flat and politically secure. And defending today’s formula is not neutral, because it transfers money from lower earners, who die younger, to higher earners, who collect benefits longer.

## Crux

Should longer lives mean longer working lives, or should society pay for longer retirements?

Almost every pension fight reduces to how the years that longer lives add are divided between work and retirement, and who pays for the retirement years. If the extra years go mostly to work, the gap shrinks without large tax increases or cuts to monthly benefits; if they go mostly to retirement, someone has to pay for them, whether today’s workers through higher contributions, future workers through debt, or savers through invested funds. Those who would keep current ages, those who would raise them with longevity, and those who would prefund or shrink the public pension all take a side on this division, explicitly or not.

What evidence could settle: whether most people in their late sixties are healthy enough and employable enough to keep working, and how that differs by income, education and occupation; how much of the gain in life expectancy is reaching lower earners, after the widening gap found for 2001 to 2014; what later pension ages actually do to employment, disability claims and old-age poverty in the countries that have raised them, such as Germany, Denmark and France; how large the economic gains from later retirement are compared with the costs of higher contributions; and how much faster productivity, more immigration or invested funds could plausibly pay for longer retirements without anyone working longer. The US trustees’ sensitivity tests already suggest that no plausible path for growth, fertility or immigration alone closes the gap, but those projections are themselves contested.

What is a value choice no data can close: whether a pension is a fixed, earned promise that should be kept as written, or a shared bargain whose terms each generation should reset as lives lengthen; how much weight to give lower earners who live shorter lives against young workers who would carry higher taxes; whether people near retirement deserve more protection than people decades away from it; and whether security in old age should come mainly from a collective guarantee or from individual ownership. Two people who agree on every projection can still disagree on these, and that is where the camps finally part.
