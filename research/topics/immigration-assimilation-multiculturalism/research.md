# Immigration & Assimilation — research

## Overview

Immigration and assimilation, as this topic uses the terms, is the question of what a host society may expect of people once they have arrived and settled: whether they should learn the national language, adopt its customs, values or identity, keep or shed the cultures they brought, and what the state owes them in return. It covers the conditions attached to permanent residence and citizenship (language levels, civics and values tests, residence periods), integration programs and their mix of support and obligation, official multiculturalism and its retreat, religious dress and accommodation, residential concentration and “parallel societies,” discrimination against immigrants and their children, and the outcomes that each side reads as proof of success or failure, from jobs and earnings to schooling, intermarriage and the progress of the second generation. It also covers the view that for some groups the goal should be return rather than integration, because that view is a direct answer to what is expected after arrival. It deliberately excludes admission: how many people a state lets in, through which visas, asylum at the border, enforcement against people without status and the ethics of borders belong to the related topic on immigration and borders. Broader fights over group identity and universalism, and over religion in public life, belong to their own related topics and appear here only where they bear on immigrants. Most of the evidence comes from Western Europe, the United States, Canada, Australia and the United Kingdom, where both the policies and the data are richest.

Why now: on 25 August 2026 US Citizenship and Immigration Services wrote “neighborhood investigations” of naturalization applicants into its Policy Manual, formalizing a practice it had revived in August 2025 after decades of disuse (USCIS Policy Manual updates, https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/updates). The agency describes the revived interviews of neighbors, landlords and co-workers as a way of “assuring proper assimilation into American values and culture,” and the October 2025 overhaul of the citizenship civics test as meant to “ensure proper assimilation of lawful immigrants into the United States and promote a unified American identity” (same page; USCIS news release, 17 September 2025, https://www.uscis.gov/newsroom/news-releases/uscis-unveils-first-changes-to-naturalization-test-in-multi-step-overhaul-of-american-citizenship). In May 2026 Senator Tommy Tuberville introduced an “ASSIMILATION Act” and Representative Nancy Mace a “No Immigration Without Assimilation Act” that would screen applicants for views judged incompatible with American principles (https://www.tuberville.senate.gov/newsroom/in-the-news/yellowhammer-news-tuberville-introduces-assimilation-act-tells-immigrants-assimilate-or-go-home/; https://mace.house.gov/media/press-releases/rep-nancy-mace-introduces-bill-shut-door-immigrants-who-reject-american-values). The same turn is visible elsewhere. On 6 June 2026 Sweden, for decades a model of official multiculturalism, raised the residence requirement for citizenship from five to eight years and added a self-support rule and, for the first time, a civics test (Swedish Migration Agency, https://www.migrationsverket.se/en/news-archive/news/2026-05-06-new-rules-for-swedish-citizenship-from-6-june-2026.html). In Britain the government’s “earned settlement” plan would double the standard wait for permanent residence to ten years and require English at upper-intermediate (B2) level, with the Home Secretary writing that “this does not mean assimilation” but “it does demand contribution and integration” (Home Office, A Fairer Pathway to Settlement, November 2025, https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691edda450b16caf978153d8/Command_Paper_final_-_reviewed7.pdf); the consultation closed in February 2026 and final rules were still pending in early October 2026 [unverified]. In Australia, Pauline Hanson told the National Press Club on 17 June 2026 that multiculturalism was “utterly flawed” and that the country “must be monocultural” (ABC News, https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-17/pauline-hanson-press-club-address-banner-stunt-one-nation/106808570). And in Canada the Supreme Court heard the challenge to Quebec’s ban on religious symbols for some public employees in March 2026 and has yet to rule (Canadian Press via paNOW, https://panow.com/2026/03/26/supreme-court-wraps-up-hearings-over-quebec-secularism-law/).

The debate is live because a generation of high immigration has made the integration of settled immigrants and their children a question about a large share of the population rather than a margin: about one person in four in Germany now has an immigration history, and the rich democracies have each been running a different experiment for decades, from Canada’s official multiculturalism to France’s civic republicanism and Denmark’s demanding mix of obligation and pressure. The record those experiments have produced is mixed enough that every side can cite it. Children of immigrants close most of the income gap their parents faced in most destinations, and in North America they often overtake the children of the native-born; yet employment gaps persist in much of Northern Europe, school results for immigrant children lag in Europe, and surveys find both rising discrimination against Muslims and persistent religious conservatism among some immigrant communities. Public opinion is pulled two ways at once: majorities in most rich countries say speaking the national language and sharing customs matter for belonging, and majorities in Australia still say multiculturalism has been good for the country. The participants include national governments on the center left and center right that have converged on “civic integration” (language, civics and work requirements framed as a two-way bargain); national-conservative and populist movements, from the Trump administration and One Nation to Quebec’s governing party, that call for assimilation to a national culture; defenders of multiculturalism in governments such as Canada’s and among political theorists such as Will Kymlicka and Tariq Modood; anti-racism and civil-liberties groups who see the problem mainly in discrimination by the host society; far-right parties such as Germany’s AfD that argue for “remigration” over integration; and economists and sociologists whose findings on the second generation each camp claims.

Value: −5

Viz: ranked
Series: Immigrant parents −5 (highlight); Sons of immigrants −3; Daughters of immigrants 0
Data year: parents’ income 1994–2000; children (born 1978–1983) measured in 2014–2015
Source label: Boustan, Jensen, Abramitzky, Jácome, Manning, Pérez et al., “Intergenerational Mobility of Immigrants in 15 Destination Countries,” NBER Working Paper 33558, March 2025
Source URL: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33558/w33558.pdf
Note: Across 15 rich countries, immigrant parents earned a median of 5 percentile points less than local-born parents; their sons closed most of that gap and their daughters closed all of it.
Context: The study, by 38 economists using linked administrative or survey data from 11 European and four non-European countries, ranks everyone within their birth cohort from 0 to 100 and compares the children of immigrants with the children of the local-born. First-generation gaps ranged from −20 to −2 rank points in ten destinations and were more than 10 points in Denmark, Norway, Sweden, France and the Netherlands; in Canada, Israel, Italy, Spain and the United States immigrant parents earned at parity or slightly more. By the second generation the gaps were much smaller: in the ten countries with first-generation shortfalls they ranged from −9 to +5 points for sons and −4 to +7 for daughters, with sons in the Netherlands an outlier at −15. Austria was the one country where gaps grew between generations. In the United States the children of immigrants out-earned the children of the local-born by 2 to 4 points. About half of the difference between countries is explained by how poor immigrant parents were to begin with; the rest reflects how fast children raised at the bottom moved up, which the authors link to destination labor markets and immigration policy rather than to where families came from. Sons of immigrants out-did comparable sons of locals only in Australia, Canada, Israel, the United States and the United Kingdom. The paper is a working paper and has not been peer reviewed; an earlier study of the United States over two centuries by several of the same authors (Abramitzky, Boustan, Jácome and Pérez, American Economic Review, 2021) found the same pattern of upward mobility among children of immigrants. Assimilationists, multiculturalists and civic integrationists all cite this convergence, each as evidence that its own model works.

Context: Across the OECD the immigrant employment rate averaged 70.9 percent in 2024, against 72.1 percent for the native-born; in the EU it was 67.9 percent. Immigrants’ employment rates reached or exceeded those of the native-born in 15 of 36 countries, but their unemployment rates were higher in all but six. The pattern follows who came and why. Countries whose immigrants arrived mainly through employer-selected work routes or as intra-EU workers (the United States, the United Kingdom, Southern Europe, Central Europe) show small or positive gaps; countries that took large numbers of refugees and family migrants, often with low formal education, show the widest, notably in Northern Europe. The OECD notes that most employed immigrants in its member countries did not arrive with a job lined up but came for family or humanitarian reasons, and that the gap in the Netherlands, at −12.8 points, was the largest in the OECD. Supporters of tougher civic integration read the Northern European gaps as the cost of generous welfare and weak work obligations; defenders of multiculturalism and anti-discrimination policy read them as partly the product of discrimination and slow recognition of foreign qualifications. Both readings are argued in the literature, and the gap figures alone cannot separate them.

Note: Germany naturalized a record 290,000 people in 2024, up 46 percent in a year, while the United States remained the largest naturalizing country with about 820,000 new citizens.
Context: More than 2.8 million people acquired the nationality of an OECD country in 2023, and partial data suggest the 2024 total approached 3 million. Germany’s record followed its June 2024 Nationality Modernisation Act, which cut the standard residence requirement from eight to five years and allowed dual citizenship without restriction; the three-year route for especially well-integrated applicants that the law also created was abolished by a Bundestag vote on 8 October 2025, while the five-year rule and dual citizenship were kept (IamExpat, https://www.iamexpat.de/expat-info/germany-news/law-scrapping-route-german-citizenship-after-3-years-comes-effect). The United Kingdom (270,000), Spain and Italy also set records. Relative to their foreign populations, Canada (11.4 percent of foreign residents naturalizing in 2023) and Sweden (7.8 percent) led the OECD, against an average of 2.6 percent. At the same time many countries are raising the bar: the OECD records Finland lengthening its residence requirement from five to eight years in October 2024, France raising the language level for naturalization to B2 in its January 2024 law, and Sweden preparing an eight-year rule with language and civics requirements, which took effect on 6 June 2026. Whether citizenship should come early, as a tool that speeds integration, or late, as a reward for integration already achieved, is one of the sharpest practical disagreements in this debate. A joint OECD and European Commission report found that just over half of settled immigrants (ten or more years of residence) in the EU held host-country citizenship around 2020, against about 80 percent in Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United States (OECD/European Commission, Indicators of Immigrant Integration 2023); in the United States, 46 percent of all immigrants were naturalized citizens in 2023 (Pew Research Center, https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/08/21/key-findings-about-us-immigrants/).

Note: In a 23-country survey, a median of 91 percent said speaking the country’s main language is important for being truly part of the nation, and 81 percent said the same of sharing its customs and traditions.
Context: The question asked how important each item is “for being truly” a national of the respondent’s country, and the figures combine “very important” and “somewhat important.” Language was not asked in Nigeria or South Africa and religion was not asked in Japan or South Korea, so those medians cover 21 countries; customs and birthplace cover 23. Pew notes that emphasis on shared customs has declined somewhat since 2016, by double digits in Germany, Japan and the United Kingdom. The pattern supports two readings at once. Large majorities in nearly every country expect newcomers to speak the language and take part in national customs, the core of the assimilationist and civic integration programs; but ascriptive markers that newcomers cannot acquire, birthplace and religion, divide publics, and their weight has been falling in much of Western Europe.

Note: On a scale counting eight multicultural policies, Australia scored the maximum 8 in 2020 and Canada, Finland and Sweden 7, while France, Denmark, the Netherlands and Switzerland scored 1.5 or lower.
Context: The index awards up to one point for each of eight policies: official affirmation of multiculturalism, multiculturalism in the school curriculum, ethnic representation in public media, exemptions from dress codes and similar rules, dual citizenship, public funding for ethnic group organizations, bilingual or mother-tongue education, and affirmative action for disadvantaged immigrant groups. It measures national policy on paper, not outcomes or public attitudes. The trend lines tell the story of the last forty years. The Netherlands, once the European model of group-based multiculturalism, rose from 2.5 in 1980 to 4 in 2000 and then fell to 2 in 2010 and 1 in 2020 as it switched to compulsory civic integration. Finland went from 0 in 1980 to 7, Norway from 0 to 4.5, and Australia has held the maximum since 1990. The project is led by Keith Banting and Will Kymlicka, and Kymlicka is a leading theorist of multiculturalism; the project’s own summary argues that despite talk of a backlash, multicultural policies have generally persisted and in many cases expanded (https://www.queensu.ca/mcp/immigrant-minorities). Critics of multiculturalism read the same series as showing that the countries that went furthest down the multicultural road, above all the Netherlands, were the ones that turned back hardest.

Note: In 2025, 83 percent of Australian adults agreed that multiculturalism has been good for the country, but only 33 percent agreed that ethnic minorities should get government help to maintain their customs and traditions.
Context: Australia is the country with the strongest official commitment to multiculturalism on the Multiculturalism Policy Index, and this long-running survey shows what that commitment means to the public. Symbolic support is broad: besides the 83 percent, 90 percent agreed that someone born overseas is just as likely to be a good citizen as someone born in Australia. Support for spending public money to preserve minority cultures is far lower, and the 33 percent figure is in line with its average since 2007. The share agreeing that accepting immigrants from many countries makes Australia stronger fell from 78 to 67 percent between 2023 and 2025, and the share saying immigration is too high rose from 24 percent in 2022 to 49 percent in 2024 and 51 percent in 2025, the highest the survey has recorded. The authors stress that concern about numbers has not, so far, become rejection of diversity itself. Each camp finds something here: multiculturalists point to the durable majority, assimilationists and civic integrationists to the reluctance to fund separate cultural maintenance. The survey predates Pauline Hanson’s June 2026 call for a “monocultural” Australia.

A country where one person in four has a recent immigrant lineage cannot treat questions of language, citizenship and belonging as peripheral, whatever answer it gives.

Context: The 2015 cohort arrived in the year Angela Merkel said “Wir schaffen das” (“We can do this”), and its progress has become a test case for every side. Context: The figure comes from the OECD’s PISA tests and concerns the second generation, children born and schooled in the host country. In Europe and Latin America the children of immigrants read less well than their peers with native-born parents; in most other non-European OECD countries the reverse is true, reflecting who immigrated there. The gap has narrowed: the native-born children of immigrants improved their reading scores in both the OECD and the EU since 2009 while their peers with native-born parents stayed roughly level. Among disadvantaged pupils, the share of top reading performers is higher among children of immigrants than among children of native-born parents in most countries. More than half of pupils with foreign-born parents attend schools in the top quarter for concentration of such pupils, and that concentration rose over the decade in almost all countries, especially the United Kingdom, Italy and the Nordic countries. Around 17 percent of native-born young people (15 to 34) with foreign-born parents were neither in work, education nor training in 2020–21, higher than their peers of native-born parentage in virtually all EU countries. The school gap is central to the debate because it is about people who never migrated: for assimilationists it signals failed integration at home and in segregated schools, for multiculturalists unequal schools and discrimination.

Note: In studies that sent matched job applications differing only in apparent ethnicity, white native applicants received about 25 percent more callbacks than nonwhite applicants in the least discriminating countries and nearly twice as many in the most discriminating.

## Questions

Question: Should a host society expect immigrants to adopt its culture, or only its language, laws and democratic rules?
Tag: value
Poles: Shared national culture / Shared civic rules only
Why contested: Almost everyone in the debate agrees that newcomers must obey the law, and most agree they should learn the national language. The fight is over everything beyond that: customs, dress, family norms, holidays, the weight of religion in daily life, and above all identity, whether immigrants and their children should come to see themselves first as members of the nation. The answer depends on what one thinks a nation is, a political community bound by shared principles or a cultural community with a particular history, and on how much a liberal state may ask of individuals’ private lives.
Case for shared national culture: Those who favor assimilation argue that democracies run on trust, and trust rests on more than agreement about procedures. People share burdens, pay taxes for strangers and accept electoral defeat because they feel they belong to one people with a common language, memory and way of life. Abstract principles such as democracy and equality are claimed by every Western country alike and cannot by themselves make anyone feel French or American. A society that asks nothing of newcomers beyond obeying the law, they argue, leaves them adrift between two cultures and invites parallel communities, while a clear expectation of cultural belonging gives immigrants a path to full membership. The historical record of the great American immigration of 1880 to 1920, when newcomers and above all their children were expected to become American, is in this view a success story that a confident culture can repeat.
Case for shared civic rules only: Those who favor civic integration or multiculturalism argue that a liberal state may demand obedience to its laws, knowledge of its language and acceptance of its democratic rules, but not conformity in taste, faith or private life, which it does not demand of native citizens either. National cultures are not fixed: they have always absorbed newcomers and changed in the process, and “the national culture” in practice means whatever its current majority prefers. Demands for cultural conformity, they argue, fall hardest on visible minorities, especially Muslims, who can learn the language and the constitution but cannot stop being seen as different, and so the demand turns into a permanent test that they can never pass. Shared civic rules are enough to sustain a common life, as the record of diverse, stable democracies such as Canada suggests.

Question: Is the main obstacle to integration in immigrants’ own choices and values, or in barriers raised by the host society?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Newcomers’ choices / Host barriers
Why contested: Both sides point to the same gaps in jobs, schooling and residence and explain them differently. Field experiments have measured discrimination precisely, and surveys have measured conservative religious and social views among some immigrant groups; what no single study has settled is how much of the remaining gap each explains, and the answer differs by country and group.
Case for newcomers’ choices: Those who stress immigrants’ own choices point out that outcomes vary widely by origin group within the same host country, facing the same institutions, which suggests that culture, family structure, women’s participation in work and religious practice matter. Surveys such as Ruud Koopmans’s 2008 six-country study found that a majority of Muslim respondents placed religious rules above national laws, and low employment among women in some refugee groups persists years after arrival. On this view, emphasizing discrimination excuses choices that newcomers and their communities can change, and integration policy should ask more of them.
Case for host barriers: Those who stress host-society barriers point to field experiments in which identical job applications receive fewer callbacks when the name sounds foreign, a gap that persists for native-born children of immigrants who speak the language and hold local degrees (Quillian and colleagues, 2019). Research in France by Claire Adida, David Laitin and Marie-Anne Valfort found that otherwise identical candidates fared worse when identified as Muslim, and they argue that discrimination and withdrawal reinforce each other. Surveys by the EU’s Fundamental Rights Agency find reported discrimination against Muslims rising. On this view, demands placed only on immigrants misdiagnose the problem, and integration is a two-way process that requires the host to open jobs, housing and schools.

Question: Does official multiculturalism help or hinder the integration of immigrants?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Helps integration / Hinders integration
Why contested: Countries with strongly multicultural policies differ from those without in many other ways, above all in which immigrants they selected, so cross-country comparisons are easy to make and hard to read. The Netherlands abandoned group-based multiculturalism after 2000 while Canada and Australia kept it, giving each side its favorite case.
Case for helps integration: Supporters argue that recognition makes belonging easier: immigrants who are not asked to choose between their heritage and their new country identify with it more readily, naturalize more and participate more. Canada and Australia, which score highest on the Multiculturalism Policy Index, have among the highest naturalization rates and some of the best second-generation outcomes in the rich world, and a comparative study by Matthew Wright and Irene Bloemraad (2012) found no evidence that multicultural policies weaken immigrants’ attachment or political participation. Historical evidence also suggests that coercion backfires: Vasiliki Fouka (2020) found that bans on German-language schooling in US states after the First World War made German Americans less likely to assimilate, not more.
Case for hinders integration: Critics argue that multiculturalism, by funding and representing groups as groups, freezes identities, empowers conservative community leaders over individuals, and lowers the incentive to learn the language and enter the labor market. Ruud Koopmans (2010) found that European countries that combined multicultural policies with generous welfare states had worse immigrant employment and segregation outcomes. The Netherlands, the European pioneer, concluded through its own political debate that the model had failed and replaced it with compulsory civic integration. Canada and Australia, critics add, do well because they select skilled immigrants, not because of multicultural policy.

Question: Should citizenship come early, as a tool that speeds integration, or late, as a reward for integration achieved?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Early catalyst / Earned reward
Why contested: Governments are moving in both directions at once. Germany cut its residence requirement to five years in 2024, while Finland, Sweden and the United Kingdom have lengthened theirs and added tests. Part of the question is empirical (does naturalization itself improve outcomes?) and part is about what citizenship means.
Case for early catalyst: Supporters of early naturalization point to natural experiments in Switzerland, where some municipalities decided naturalization by close popular votes: Jens Hainmueller, Dominik Hangartner and Giuseppe Pietrantuono (2015, 2017) found that immigrants who narrowly won citizenship became more politically engaged and better socially integrated over the following years than those who narrowly lost, with the largest effects for the most marginalized groups. Secure status lets people invest in a future, and long waits leave large parts of the population without a vote in the places they live.
Case for earned reward: Those who favor later citizenship argue that citizenship is a commitment by the nation as well as the immigrant and should follow, not precede, proof of attachment: language skill, self-support, a clean record and knowledge of the country. Easy citizenship, they argue, cheapens membership and removes leverage at exactly the point where a state can still ask for effort. The UK government’s 2025 earned-settlement plan and Sweden’s 2026 law put this view into practice, and Pew’s 2023 survey found large majorities in most countries saying that speaking the national language matters for belonging.

Question: Should integration be compulsory, with sanctions, or voluntary and supported?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Obligations with sanctions / Support and incentives
Why contested: Since the late 1990s much of Western Europe has made language and civics courses mandatory and tied benefits and residence permits to them. Evaluations of individual reforms exist, but their results point in different directions depending on the instrument.
Case for obligations with sanctions: Advocates argue that courses which can be skipped are skipped by exactly those who most need them, often women kept at home by family pressure. Tying residence permits and benefits to language and work sends a clear signal and protects the welfare state’s legitimacy among taxpayers. Denmark’s 2002 cut in benefits for newly arrived refugees produced a sharp short-run rise in employment, according to research by Lars Højsgaard Andersen, Christian Dustmann and Rasmus Landersø.
Case for support and incentives: Critics of the coercive turn point to the same Danish research, which found that the employment gains had largely disappeared nine to ten years after arrival, that many affected households fell close to subsistence, and that children in those families did worse in school and committed more crime as teenagers (https://www.cream-migration.org/publ_uploads/CDP_05_19.pdf). By contrast, Mette Foged, Linea Hasager, Giovanni Peri and colleagues found that Denmark’s 1999 expansion of free language training produced lasting gains in refugees’ earnings (Review of Economics and Statistics, 2024). On this view, investment works better than pressure.

Question: May the state restrict religious dress and practice in the name of integration or a shared public life?
Tag: value
Poles: Common public norms / Religious freedom
Why contested: France bans religious symbols in public schools and full-face coverings in public, Quebec bans religious symbols for some public employees, and several European countries have face-veil bans, while Canada’s federal government, Britain and the United States accommodate religious dress. The European Court of Human Rights upheld France’s face-veil ban in S.A.S. v. France (2014), accepting “living together” as a legitimate aim, and the Supreme Court of Canada is now weighing Quebec’s law.
Case for common public norms: Supporters argue that a secular public sphere protects everyone, including members of minority faiths who do not wish to be pressured by their own communities, and that visible face-to-face interaction is a basic condition of a shared civic life. In France’s republican tradition, the state treats citizens as individuals, not as members of religious communities, and neutrality in public institutions is part of equal treatment. The European court accepted that states have a wide margin to decide these questions (ECHR factsheet, https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/fs_religious_symbols_eng).
Case for religious freedom: Opponents argue that such bans fall almost entirely on Muslim women, the very people they claim to free, and push them out of schools and jobs rather than into the mainstream. Aala Abdelgadir and Vasiliki Fouka (2020) found that France’s 2004 ban on headscarves in public schools reduced the secondary educational attainment of Muslim girls (American Political Science Review). On this view, a liberal state integrates religious minorities by accommodating them, as it once accommodated Catholics and Jews.

Question: Should refugees and other humanitarian migrants be integrated from arrival, or treated as temporary residents expected to return?
Tag: value
Poles: Integrate from arrival / Protection is temporary
Why contested: International refugee law allows protection to end when the danger in the home country ends, and Denmark’s 2019 “paradigm shift” made return rather than integration the stated aim for refugees. Germany’s AfD calls for the return of refugees once the reason for flight has ended, citing Syrians. Yet most refugees stay for many years, and their children grow up in the host country.
Case for integrate from arrival: Supporters argue that wars rarely end quickly and most refugees never go home, so delaying integration only wastes years in which language, work and schooling could have been built. The German experience with the 2015 cohort, which reached 64 percent employment after nine years, shows that integration is slow and that each year lost at the start costs later. Temporary status discourages investment by refugees and employers alike.
Case for protection is temporary: Supporters of temporary protection argue that asylum is a promise of shelter from danger, not of a new homeland, and that keeping return as the goal preserves public support for taking refugees in at all. Return also serves the home country, which needs its people to rebuild. After the fall of Syria’s Assad government in December 2024, they argue, the case for Syrian refugees in Europe to go home became concrete rather than theoretical.

Question: Does ethnic and cultural diversity erode trust and solidarity?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Erodes trust / Trust adapts
Why contested: Robert Putnam’s 2007 study of US communities, which found lower trust in more diverse places, set off a large literature. Its results are consistent but modest and vary with how trust and diversity are measured.
Case for erodes trust: A meta-analysis of 1,001 estimates from 87 studies by Peter Thisted Dinesen, Merlin Schaeffer and Kim Mannemar Sønderskov (Annual Review of Political Science, 2020) found a statistically significant negative relationship between ethnic diversity and social trust, strongest for trust in neighbors and when diversity is measured locally (https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/ethnic-diversity-and-social-trust-a-narrative-and-meta-analytical/). Those who worry about cohesion argue that this is exactly why a shared culture, or at least a strong shared identity, matters: it supplies the trust that diversity draws down, and it underpins the solidarity that welfare states depend on.
Case for trust adapts: Others note that the same literature finds the effect small, that it is often tied to segregation and inequality rather than diversity as such, and that contact between groups over time tends to raise trust. Highly diverse cities and countries such as Canada sustain high levels of trust, and what erodes trust, on this view, is segregation and rapid change without investment in shared institutions, not diversity itself.

Question: Should governments break up concentrated immigrant neighborhoods, or let ethnic communities cluster?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Disperse concentrations / Let communities cluster
Why contested: Denmark has set out to dismantle what it calls parallel societies, and other countries steer refugees away from large cities, while research finds that ethnic networks also help newcomers find work.
Case for disperse concentrations: Supporters argue that neighborhoods where most residents are immigrants and few work entrench unemployment, low school results and crime, keep children from learning the national language well, and allow informal norms at odds with the host society’s laws to persist. Denmark’s government credits its policy for the drop in listed areas from twelve to five between 2023 and 2025.
Case for let communities cluster: Critics argue that forced dispersal breaks the networks through which newcomers find jobs, housing and help. A natural experiment in Sweden by Per-Anders Edin, Peter Fredriksson and Olof Åslund (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2003) found that living in an ethnic enclave raised the earnings of less-skilled immigrants. The IAB’s German research finds that sending refugees to regions with weak labor markets lowered their employment. Removing tenants by ancestry, critics add, treats citizens unequally, a question now before the courts after the EU Court of Justice ruling of December 2025.

## Positions

Camp: Require a common language and civic values, not a common culture
Also known as: civic integration; integration as a two-way process; interculturalism (in its Quebec and Council of Europe versions)
Adherents:
- UK Home Office under Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood (institution). Its November 2025 statement on earned settlement says Britain’s migration story has depended on newcomers becoming “a part of our wider cultural life” and paying back to the country, and adds: “This does not mean assimilation. We have built a multi-faith, multi-ethnic democracy that is pluralistic, grounded in tolerance towards difference. But it does demand contribution and integration.” It proposes English at B2 level and the Life in the UK test as conditions of settlement. URL: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691edda450b16caf978153d8/Command_Paper_final_-_reviewed7.pdf
- Sunder Katwala, director of the think tank British Future. Writing on the government’s Casey Review in December 2016, he endorsed its call to promote English as “an essential passport to full economic, social and democratic participation” while arguing that integration must be “a two-way street of rights and responsibilities” that also asks things of the majority. URL: https://www.britishfuture.org/the-casey-review-and-the-need-for-an-integration-strategy/
- Also in this camp in practice: Sweden’s 2026 citizenship law, which adds a civics test, language requirement and self-support rule (https://www.migrationsverket.se/en/news-archive/news/2026-05-06-new-rules-for-swedish-citizenship-from-6-june-2026.html), and the mandatory civic integration programs of the Netherlands, Germany, France and Austria.
Case: Integration is a bargain with obligations on both sides. The host society offers equal rights, language classes, recognition of qualifications and, in time, full citizenship; the newcomer learns the language, works where able, obeys the law and accepts the democratic rules everyone lives by: equality of men and women, freedom of speech and religion, the separation of religious authority from the law. Beyond that core, culture is free. People may keep their faith, food, festivals and family languages, as earlier generations of immigrants did. This position rejects two mistakes. One is the old multiculturalism that asked too little, treated communities as blocs represented by self-appointed leaders, and in places tolerated isolation in the name of respect. The other is assimilationism, which asks for something a liberal state has no right to demand and no means to enforce: that people change what they believe in private and who they feel themselves to be. A common language and shared civic rules are what make a common life possible in a diverse democracy; clear, published requirements, backed by real support, are fairer to newcomers than vague expectations they can never be sure they have met, and they sustain the public consent on which continued immigration depends.
Best evidence: Language is the strongest single predictor of immigrants’ employment and earnings, and the OECD finds settled immigrants in the EU almost twice as likely as recent arrivals to report advanced proficiency. Denmark’s 1999 expansion of free language training raised refugees’ earnings permanently (Foged, Hasager, Peri and colleagues, Review of Economics and Statistics, 2024). Natural experiments in Switzerland show that citizenship, once granted, deepens political and social integration (Hainmueller, Hangartner and Pietrantuono, 2015 and 2017). Public opinion in 23 countries treats language and participation in common customs as central to belonging while dividing on birthplace and religion (Pew, 2024), which matches this camp’s line between what may and may not be asked. Since the 2000s governments of left and right across Western Europe have converged on this model.
Answer to objections: To assimilationists who say shared civic values are too thin to hold a nation together, this camp answers that a shared language, shared institutions such as schools, the army and the workplace, and equal citizenship are not thin, and that Britain and Canada have built common identities on them across many faiths. Cultural demands beyond that would require policing private life, which no liberal state can do without becoming illiberal. To multiculturalists who see tests and requirements as gatekeeping, it answers that the requirements are modest, are paired with free courses, and give newcomers the tools to compete; refusing to ask anything of immigrants has been the surest way to lose public support for immigration altogether.
Variation: The camp spans a demanding wing, which uses residence permits, benefits and long waits for citizenship as levers (the Netherlands, Denmark, the UK’s earned-settlement plan, Sweden since 2026), and a facilitating wing, which treats early citizenship and dual nationality as integration tools (Germany’s 2024 law, Canada). Quebec’s interculturalism and the Council of Europe’s Intercultural Cities program stress interaction across groups around a common public culture and language. The camp also divides on religious dress, with France and Quebec restricting it in public institutions and Britain accommodating it.

Camp: Expect newcomers to adopt the national culture
Also known as: assimilation; Americanization; the melting pot; Leitkultur (leading culture) in Germany
Adherents:
- US Citizenship and Immigration Services under Director Joseph Edlow (institution). Announcing its new civics test on 17 September 2025, the agency said it wanted to ensure that “those joining us as fellow citizens are fully assimilated and will contribute to America’s greatness,” and its policy guidance describes the test and revived neighborhood investigations as ways to “ensure proper assimilation of lawful immigrants into the United States and promote a unified American identity.” URL: https://www.uscis.gov/newsroom/news-releases/uscis-unveils-first-changes-to-naturalization-test-in-multi-step-overhaul-of-american-citizenship
- Senator Tommy Tuberville of Alabama. Introducing his ASSIMILATION Act on 13 May 2026, he said: “Assimilation has absolutely nothing to do with race. It’s about values,” and “Mass migration without assimilation will erase our country’s identity.” URL: https://www.tuberville.senate.gov/newsroom/in-the-news/yellowhammer-news-tuberville-introduces-assimilation-act-tells-immigrants-assimilate-or-go-home/
- Also in this camp: Pauline Hanson of Australia’s One Nation, who told the National Press Club on 17 June 2026 that Australia “must be monocultural” while remaining multiracial (https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-17/pauline-hanson-press-club-address-banner-stunt-one-nation/106808570); Representative Nancy Mace, whose May 2026 bill would screen applicants for views judged incompatible with American principles, including refusal to learn English (https://mace.house.gov/media/press-releases/rep-nancy-mace-introduces-bill-shut-door-immigrants-who-reject-american-values); and, in a francophone version, Quebec’s government, whose 2025 national integration law makes Quebec culture and French the common culture all residents are expected to join (Global News, https://globalnews.ca/news/11062824/quebec-bill-integrating-immigrants/amp).
Case: Every successful nation of immigrants has asked newcomers to become part of it, not merely to reside in it. A nation is more than a legal framework; it is a people with a language, a history, habits and loyalties, and it can absorb large numbers of newcomers only if they and especially their children come to share them. That is not a question of race: people of every origin have become fully American, Australian or French, and the demand to do so is a mark of respect, an invitation to full membership rather than permanent guest status. What failed in recent decades was not immigration but the loss of confidence to ask this, replaced by a multicultural ethic that told newcomers they need not change and told natives they must not mind. The results are visible in neighborhoods where the national language is rarely heard, in communities where religious rules trump national law, and in second and third generations who feel loyalty to neither country. Citizenship should be the crown of a real transformation, tested seriously, and the state should promote the national language, history and civic culture in schools and public life without apology.
Best evidence: The camp points to the great US immigration of 1880 to 1920, which brought millions of Southern and Eastern Europeans, was followed by a deliberate Americanization effort, and produced descendants now fully part of the American mainstream; today’s children of immigrants in the United States continue to move up fast (Abramitzky, Boustan and colleagues). Intermarriage outside one’s group rises from 15 to 39 percent between immigrant and US-born Hispanic newlyweds and from 24 to 46 percent among Asian newlyweds (Pew, 2017). Surveys in Western Europe have found that majorities of Muslim respondents of Turkish and Moroccan origin placed religious rules above national laws (Koopmans, 2008 data), which the camp reads as evidence that value differences do not fade on their own. Majorities in most countries say sharing customs is important to being truly part of the nation (Pew, 2024), so this camp argues it speaks for the public.
Answer to objections: To the charge that assimilation is coercive and racially coded, it answers that the demand is cultural and civic, open to anyone who accepts it, and that its loudest advocates include immigrants who did so. To the evidence that German-language bans after the First World War backfired, it answers that persecution is not the same as invitation; what works is a confident culture that expects newcomers to join and rewards them when they do. To civic integrationists, it answers that values such as democracy and equality are claimed by every Western country and cannot by themselves create the loyalty and trust a nation needs; people die and pay taxes for their country, not for procedures.
Variation: Some in the camp stress values and loyalty (Tuberville, USCIS); others stress culture and customs, including religion and dress (Hanson, the German Leitkultur debate). Most rely on schools, citizenship tests and official-language laws; some, like Representative Mace’s bill, would also make residence conditional on holding compatible views, which shades into the return camp below.

Camp: Let newcomers keep their cultures and accommodate them
Also known as: multiculturalism; pluralism; integration without assimilation
Adherents:
- Government of Canada, through the Canadian Multiculturalism Act (institution). Canadian Heritage describes the act as “recognizing and preserving the multicultural heritage of Canadians” and as holding that “all Canadians should have the opportunity to preserve, enhance and share their cultural heritage,” while “assisting individuals and communities of all origins to eliminate barriers to their participation in Canadian society.” URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/canadian-heritage/services/about-multiculturalism-anti-racism/about-act.html
- Tariq Modood, political theorist at the University of Bristol. In “A defence of multiculturalism” he argues that multiculturalism “is not a politics of separatism; on the contrary, it is a politics of diversity and pluralism,” built on equality and on “integration without assimilation,” an interactive process in which minorities and the majority both change. URL: http://www.tariqmodood.com/uploads/1/2/3/9/12392325/a_defence_of_multiculturalism.pdf
- Also in this camp: the Runnymede Trust, Britain’s race-equality think tank (https://www.runnymedetrust.org/), Will Kymlicka, co-director of the Multiculturalism Policy Index, and the Australian government’s multicultural framework.
Case: Equal citizenship in a diverse society means more than equal treatment by laws written for the majority. People do not leave their identities at the border, and a state that asks them to treat their language, faith and heritage as something to be shed is telling them they are not fully welcome. Multiculturalism recognizes that identities are plural: a person can be Canadian and Sikh, British and Muslim, and the second identity is no threat to the first. Public institutions should accommodate difference where they reasonably can (in dress codes, school curricula, broadcasting and public holidays), and the state should fight the discrimination that is the main barrier immigrants and their children face. Integration happens, and happens best, when people feel secure: they learn the language because they want to take part, not because a test forces them, and their children grow up with a confident dual identity rather than a choice between betraying their parents and being rejected by their neighbors. The record of the most multicultural countries, Canada and Australia, shows that recognition and cohesion go together.
Best evidence: Canada and Australia, the top scorers on the Multiculturalism Policy Index, have among the highest naturalization rates in the OECD (Canada naturalized 11.4 percent of its foreign residents in 2023, against an OECD average of 2.6 percent) and children of immigrants who out-earn the children of the local-born (Boustan, Abramitzky and colleagues, 2025). In Australia 83 percent of adults say multiculturalism has been good for the country (Scanlon Institute, 2025). Comparative research has found no trade-off between multicultural policy and immigrants’ civic attachment (Wright and Bloemraad, 2012), and coercive assimilation has backfired: US bans on German-language schooling after 1918 slowed German Americans’ assimilation (Fouka, 2020). Field experiments show large hiring discrimination against minorities in every country studied (Quillian and colleagues, 2019), and Muslims in the EU report rising discrimination (FRA, 2024).
Answer to objections: To the charge that multiculturalism breeds separate communities, it answers that the evidence points the other way: segregation and withdrawal follow exclusion and discrimination, and the countries that kept multiculturalism have more integrated second generations than many that never had it. The Dutch problems critics cite grew out of a guest-worker policy that for decades expected migrants to go home and gave them little reason to settle, not out of recognition. To the charge that it lets illiberal practices hide behind culture, it answers that multiculturalism has always operated within liberal limits: rights, not group traditions, have the final word, and accommodation is about headscarves and holidays, not forced marriage. And to the claim that Canada and Australia succeed only because they choose skilled immigrants, it answers that selection explains the parents’ advantages, not the second generation’s sense of belonging.
Variation: A strong version asks for group recognition and public funding of cultural maintenance; a lighter version, common among anti-racist organizations, centers on removing discrimination and making integration a two-way process. A libertarian strand reaches a similar conclusion from the opposite direction, holding that the state should neither fund cultures nor push assimilation; the Cato Institute argues that today’s immigrants assimilate well on their own and that government assimilation programs can backfire (https://www.cato.org/blog/government-immigrant-assimilation-programs-can-backfire-case-german-americans; attribution to Alex Nowrasteh [unverified], the page blocked automated retrieval).

Camp: Aim for return, not integration
Also known as: remigration; reverse migration; temporary protection
Adherents:
- Alternative for Germany (AfD) (institution). Its 2025 federal election program, adopted at the party congress in Riesa on 11 January 2025, made “remigration” a goal and defined it as the consistent deportation of those required to leave, the return of refugees once the reason for flight in their home country has ended (“etwa von Syrern,” such as Syrians), and the return of criminals and people considered dangerous. Party leader Alice Weidel told delegates: “If it’s supposed to be called remigration, then it’s called remigration.” URL: https://www.stuttgarter-zeitung.de/inhalt.bundestagswahl-2025-afd-parteitag-nimmt-forderung-nach-remigration-ins-wahlprogramm-auf.c7181816-7204-4b94-a7ba-33094bf26f5d.html
- President Donald Trump. After the shooting of two National Guard members in Washington in November 2025, he wrote that he would “permanently pause migration from all Third World Countries,” “denaturalize migrants who undermine domestic tranquility, and deport any foreign national who is a public charge, security risk, or non-compatible with Western civilization.” URL: https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-11-28/donald-trump-migration-pause-from-third-world-countries-to-us/106079562
- Related policy: Denmark’s 2019 “paradigm shift,” passed in February 2019, made refugee protection temporary and return rather than integration the stated aim (Migration Policy Institute, https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/denmark-migration-profile-pioneer).
Case: The integration project has been tried for half a century and, for large groups from culturally distant regions, it has not delivered: employment gaps, welfare dependence, crime, parallel neighborhoods and hostility to the host society’s values persist into the second generation in country after country. More integration programs will not change that, because the problem is scale and cultural distance, not funding. Asylum was always meant to be protection for the duration of a danger, not a permanent new homeland; when wars end, as Syria’s has, refugees should go home and rebuild their countries. Those who have no right to stay should leave, and residence for others should depend on being a net contribution and accepting the nation’s way of life. Nations have the right to preserve their identity, and voters in many countries have repeatedly asked for less immigration and been ignored. Return, by incentives and lawful enforcement, is the only policy that addresses the root of the problem rather than managing its symptoms, and it is the condition for welcoming those who do integrate.
Best evidence: Large employment gaps for immigrants persist in the Netherlands, Germany, Finland and Sweden (OECD, 2025); refugee women’s employment in Germany was 35 percent nine years after the 2015 arrivals (IAB, 2025); surveys have found majorities of Muslim respondents of Turkish and Moroccan origin placing religious rules above national laws (Koopmans, 2008 data). The camp argues that Europe’s experience with guest workers who were expected to leave but stayed shows that temporary status must be enforced if it is to mean anything, and it cites the fall of Syria’s Assad government in December 2024 as removing the reason many Syrians fled.
Answer to objections: To the charge that remigration means expelling citizens by ancestry, the AfD answers in its January 2024 position paper that it means only the lawful return of foreigners required to leave, that it rejects the deportation of German citizens with a migration background as unconstitutional, and that well-integrated citizens of migrant origin are “ausdrücklich willkommen” (expressly welcome) (https://www.afd.de/afd-positionspapier-zur-remigration-raeumt-mit-deportationsluege-auf/). To the charge that it is impractical or cruel, it answers that every state already removes people without a right to stay, that incentives for voluntary return are humane, and that leaving the status quo unchanged is crueler to the natives and immigrants who live with its consequences. To integrationists, it answers that their programs have had decades and vast budgets to show results.
Variation: The narrow version, the AfD’s official line and Denmark’s refugee policy, applies to people without a right to stay and to refugees whose protection has lapsed. The broad version, voiced by President Trump and by activists such as the Austrian Martin Sellner [Sellner’s position unverified against a primary source], extends to legal residents and naturalized citizens judged not to fit, through denaturalization or pressure to leave. Germany’s domestic intelligence service classified the federal AfD as a confirmed right-wing extremist organization in May 2025, citing among other things efforts to restrict the rights of German Muslims; in February 2026 the Administrative Court of Cologne ruled in emergency proceedings that the service may not use that label for now, finding individual clearly unconstitutional demands but insufficient evidence that the party as a whole is hostile to the constitution, and the main case is pending (Mediendienst Integration, https://mediendienst-integration.de/extremismus/rechtsextremismus/ist-die-afd-rechtsextrem/).

## Crux

Crux: Do immigrants and their children come to share the host society’s language, values and sense of belonging on their own, given equal rights and a fair chance, or only when the society expects them to adopt its culture, and is a shared culture something a democracy is entitled to ask of them at all?

What evidence could settle: How fast and how fully convergence happens, and what speeds or slows it, are empirical questions that are already partly answered and could be answered better. Long-run data that link parents and children across generations show income gaps narrowing sharply in most countries, but not all, and not equally for sons and daughters. Better evidence could tell how values and attachment, not just earnings, change across generations for different origin groups; how much of the remaining gaps reflect discrimination, which field experiments can measure, and how much reflects choices and norms within communities; whether multicultural recognition, mandatory courses, early citizenship, benefit sanctions or forced dispersal each speed integration or slow it, which natural experiments in Denmark, Switzerland and the United States have begun to show; and whether diversity lowers trust over the long run or only during periods of rapid change. If convergence proves fast and broad under light-touch policies, the case for cultural demands and for return weakens; if it stalls for some groups under every policy, the case for demanding more, or for less immigration, gains.

What is a value choice: No data can say what a nation is. One view holds that a nation is a cultural community with a particular language, history and way of life that its existing members may legitimately wish to preserve, so that asking newcomers to join that culture, or to leave if they will not, is a reasonable condition of membership. The other holds that a liberal nation is a civic compact open to anyone who accepts its laws and democratic rules, so that asking more than that intrudes on freedoms the state owes to natives and newcomers alike. Between them lies a judgment about how much weight the preferences of the existing majority should carry against the freedom of newcomers to live as they choose, and about whether citizens of immigrant origin should ever face conditions that native-born citizens do not. Each camp on this page weighs those claims differently, and evidence about outcomes can inform the weighing but cannot do it.
