# Content Moderation — research

## Overview

This topic asks how much responsibility online platforms should bear for speech that is legal but harmful, and whether the legal shields that protect them from liability for what their users post should stay, shrink or go. “Lawful but harmful” covers the large territory between protected speech and crime: harassment and pile-ons that fall short of criminal threats, health and election falsehoods, content that glorifies self-harm or eating disorders, hateful but legal speech, and material that is harmless to adults but not to children. The topic covers how platforms write and enforce their own rules (removal, demotion, warning labels, fact-checks, crowd-sourced notes, account bans), the US liability shield in Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act and its counterparts abroad (the EU’s Digital Services Act, the UK’s Online Safety Act, Brazil’s Marco Civil da Internet), government pressure on platforms to remove or restore content, state laws that would force platforms to carry lawful speech, and the newer effort to hold platforms liable for product design rather than for content. It deliberately excludes national speech law, meaning what a government may itself criminalize or punish, such as hate speech, blasphemy or defamation law (covered in the hate speech law topic); deepfakes and the wider collapse of shared facts except where platform duties are at issue (covered in the epistemic crisis topic); and the evidence on social media and adolescent mental health as a subject of its own (covered in the smartphones and childhood topic).

Why now: on 10 September 2026 Representatives Mark DeSaulnier, a California Democrat, and Jimmy Patronis, a Florida Republican, introduced the Sunset Section 230 Act (H.R. 10332), which would repeal Section 230 two years after enactment without putting a new liability rule in its place (DeSaulnier press release, https://desaulnier.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/representatives-desaulnier-and-patronis-introduce-legislation-hold-tech; bill text, https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr10332ih). It is the House companion to a Senate bill of the same name that Lindsey Graham and Dick Durbin introduced in December 2025 with eight cosponsors split evenly between the parties, among them Chuck Grassley, Josh Hawley, Amy Klobuchar and Richard Blumenthal (Senate Judiciary Committee, https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/press/dem/releases/durbin-graham-introduce-bill-to-sunset-section-230-immunity-for-tech-companies-protect-americans-online). The bills arrive in the law’s thirtieth year (it was signed on 8 February 1996) and after a Los Angeles jury, in the first bellwether trial of California’s social media addiction litigation, found Meta and YouTube negligent in designing their apps on 25 March 2026, the leading edge of a wave of lawsuits built around product design rather than user content so as to get past Section 230 (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K.G.M._v._Meta_et_al.). Europe is moving the other way from the current US administration: on 5 December 2025 the European Commission issued the first fine under the Digital Services Act, €120 million against X for a deceptive verification checkmark and failures of ad and researcher transparency (European Commission, https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-fines-x-eu120-million-under-digital-services-act), and on 31 August 2026 it brought ChatGPT, Reddit and Roblox under the act’s strictest tier of obligations (European Commission, https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-designates-chatgpt-reddit-roblox-under-digital-services-act). In late December 2025 the US State Department imposed visa bans on Thierry Breton, the former EU commissioner who steered the act, and four campaigners against online hate and disinformation, with Secretary of State Marco Rubio saying they had “advanced censorship crackdowns by foreign states – in each case targeting American speakers and American companies” (Euronews, https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2025/12/24/us-visa-ban-targets-former-eu-commissioner-breton-over-alleged-social-media-censorship).

The debate is live because nearly everyone agrees that a handful of companies now decide what billions of people can say and see, and they disagree sharply about what follows. One side, strongest among child-safety advocates, European regulators and many Democrats, holds that platforms profit from harmful content and should answer for it, through a duty of care, product liability or the loss of their immunity. Another, strongest among Republicans and the current US administration, holds that the bigger danger is platforms, often under government pressure, suppressing lawful political speech; it points to COVID-era moderation and wants platforms restrained from removing views. Civil-liberties groups, the tech industry and the authors of Section 230 argue that the shield is what lets the internet host user speech at all, and that repeal would produce more censorship, not less. Between them sit reformers who would keep immunity for hosting speech but not for addictive design or algorithmic amplification, and technologists who would move moderation decisions out of both company and government hands and give them to users. Meta’s January 2025 decision to end third-party fact-checking in the United States and loosen its hate speech rules, and X’s earlier retreat from moderation, have turned the question from theory into a running experiment. Public opinion has shifted with the parties: support among Democrats for tech companies restricting false information fell from 81 to 73 percent between 2023 and 2025, while Republicans stayed near 47 percent (Pew Research Center).

Viz: radial
Data year: 2023 (131 million statements filed 19 to 28 November 2023 by very large platforms)

Note: In a ten-day sample of 131 million EU moderation decisions by the largest platforms, 99.8 percent cited a breach of the platform’s own terms of service and 0.2 percent cited illegal content.
Context: This is the clearest measure of how much moderation concerns lawful but disallowed content. Platforms can ground a decision either in law or in their own rules, and they overwhelmingly use their own rules, which reach far beyond what any law forbids and are quicker to apply than a legal judgment. The researchers caution that platforms may label illegal material as a terms-of-service breach for convenience, so the split understates how much removed content was also illegal; Facebook, for instance, used a single standard reason for all its illegal-content reports. The sample predates later database changes, and the share may differ by platform and year.

Supporters answer that notes are meant to be few and trusted, and that their effect when shown is large (see the next figure).

About 43 percent of the decisions involved defamation claims. Critics reply that the cases that reach a decision are a filtered sample, since the shield deters many suits from ever being filed. The law’s text is short: “No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider,” plus a second clause protecting good-faith removal of “objectionable” material (EFF, https://www.eff.org/issues/cda230).

Label: Brazil’s Supreme Court vote to narrow the platform liability shield
Value: 8
Unit: justices in favor, of 11
Viz: bar
Data year: 2025 (judgment concluded 26 June 2025)
Source label: Global Network Initiative, “From Shield to Scrutiny: Brazil’s Supreme Court Redefines Platform Liability”; Baker McKenzie summary of the ruling
Source URL: https://globalnetworkinitiative.org/from-shield-to-scrutiny-brazils-supreme-court-redefines-platform-liability/
Note: By 8 votes to 3, Brazil’s Supreme Federal Court declared Article 19 of the Marco Civil da Internet partly unconstitutional, making platforms liable for many kinds of user content if they fail to remove it after a private notice rather than only after a court order.
Context: Article 19, adopted in 2014, had been one of the world’s strongest shields outside the United States: platforms were liable only if they ignored a court order to remove content. The ruling keeps the court-order rule for crimes against honor such as defamation, moves most other content to notice-and-takedown, and imposes a duty of care for a list of serious categories, along with transparency and due-process obligations (Baker McKenzie, https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/brazil-the-supreme-court-stf-establishes-that-article-19-of-the-brazilian-internet-legal-framework-is-partially-unconstitutional-creating-a-new-regime-of-civil-liability/). It applies to events after 26 June 2025. Supporters in Brazil cited disinformation and attacks on democratic institutions; the Electronic Frontier Foundation, writing as the majority formed, called it a “major setback” and warned that liability relying on AI-based flagging “is a recipe for over censorship” (https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/06/major-setback-intermediary-liability-brazil-risks-and-blind-spots). It is the clearest recent case of a major democracy moving away from the US model.

## Questions

Question: Would weakening the liability shield make platforms safer, or mainly make them remove more lawful speech?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Safer platforms / More censorship
Why contested: The United States has little direct evidence of what repeal would do, its main test case being the 2018 FOSTA-SESTA carve-out for sex trafficking, and the evidence from elsewhere comes from systems that differ in many ways at once. Both sides predict how companies would behave under a legal regime that does not yet exist.
Case for safer platforms: Liability is how the law makes every other industry internalize the harm its products cause. Platforms that can be sued for foreseeable harm will invest in safety, design their products more carefully and take down content they now leave up because it drives engagement. The tech industry predicted ruin before Europe’s rules and before the UK’s Online Safety Act, and user speech continued. Section 230 was written in 1996 to protect fledgling message boards from suits over a single post, not to shield some of the world’s most valuable companies from any accountability for systems they design and profit from. The youth-harm lawsuits, the first bellwether of which reached a jury verdict against Meta and YouTube in March 2026, show that families harmed by platforms have had no other route to court. A two-year sunset, as in the current bills, forces industry and Congress to write a better rule rather than leave the shield untouched.
Case for more censorship: When a host can be sued over what users say, the cheapest response is to remove anything a plaintiff might object to and to stop hosting some speech altogether, and the burden falls hardest on controversial, minority and critical voices. The 2018 FOSTA-SESTA carve-out from Section 230 for sex trafficking led sites such as Craigslist to shut whole sections; the Government Accountability Office found that federal prosecutors had brought only one case under the new criminal provision by March 2021 (GAO, https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-385). Studies of European notice-and-takedown find that most removed comments are legal. Large platforms can afford lawyers and filters; small sites, nonprofits like Wikipedia and new competitors cannot, so repeal would entrench the giants its sponsors want to rein in.

Question: Does removing or demoting lawful-but-harmful content reduce real-world harm?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Moderation works / Harm moves elsewhere
Why contested: Platforms rarely let outsiders run the experiments that would answer this, the harms at issue (radicalization, self-harm, vaccine refusal, threats that become violence) are rare and caused by many things, and the best natural experiments are confounded by the events that triggered them.
Case for moderation works: Removing a small number of accounts can cut a large share of harmful content, because a few super-spreaders produce most of it. After Twitter removed about 70,000 accounts following the attack on the US Capitol in January 2021, misinformation circulation fell both among those accounts and among their followers, and many of the remaining spreaders left the platform (McCabe et al., Nature 2024, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07524-8). Community Notes, a mild intervention, cut reposts of noted false posts by 46 percent once attached. The coroner in the case of Molly Russell, a British 14-year-old who died in 2017, concluded in 2022 that she died “from an act of self-harm whilst suffering from depression and the negative effects of on-line content,” a finding that shaped the UK’s child-safety duties (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Molly_Russell; coroner’s report, https://www.judiciary.uk/prevention-of-future-death-reports/molly-russell-prevention-of-future-deaths-report/). When Meta relaxed enforcement in 2025, one watchdog measured a fourfold rise in threats aimed at members of Congress.
Case for harm moves elsewhere: The Nature authors themselves could not estimate the size of the effect, because the removals coincided with the events of January 6th. Removed users migrate to smaller, less moderated platforms such as Telegram and Gab, where they become harder to observe and counter, and removal feeds the grievance that radicalizes them. Meta reports that platform-wide prevalence of hateful content did not rise after it loosened its rules, and that its enforcement mistakes fell by half. Much of what is removed, including a majority of deleted comments in the European sample, is ordinary opinion, so the measured benefit of catching real harm has to be set against a large volume of speech lost to catch it, a cost that rarely shows up in harm studies.

Question: Is platform moderation politically biased, or do enforcement gaps reflect differences in what people post?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Systematic bias / Neutral rules, uneven posting
Why contested: Conservatives were suspended more often than liberals during the peak years of misinformation policy, which each side reads as proof of its own view; platforms’ rules and enforcement data are only partly public, and the people who wrote the rules leaned left politically.
Case for systematic bias: Platforms made politically loaded calls that later proved wrong or overreaching: Facebook and Twitter limited the spread of the New York Post’s Hunter Biden laptop story in October 2020, and Mark Zuckerberg told the House Judiciary Committee in August 2024 that Meta had faced “relentless” pressure from Biden administration officials to remove COVID-19 content, including humor and satire, and regretted not pushing back (Fortune, https://fortune.com/2024/08/27/mark-zuckerberg-meta-censorship-biden-administration-letter/). Facebook removed claims that COVID-19 was man-made or manufactured from February 2021 until late May 2021, when it lifted the ban “in light of ongoing investigations into the origin of COVID-19” (ABC News, https://abcnews.go.com/Technology/facebook-lifts-ban-posts-claiming-covid-19-man/story?id=77931433). “Misinformation” and “hate” are contested categories, and staff who define them come overwhelmingly from one side of politics. Meta’s own January 2025 statement conceded “mission creep that has made our rules too restrictive and too prone to over-enforcement,” adding that “it’s not right that things can be said on TV or the floor of Congress, but not on our platforms.”
Case for neutral rules, uneven posting: A 2024 Nature study found that pro-Trump accounts on Twitter were far more likely to be suspended in 2020 but also shared far more links to low-quality news sites, even when quality was judged by politically balanced or all-Republican panels of laypeople, and concluded that neutral policies would produce politically uneven enforcement (Mosleh et al., https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07942-8). Twitter’s own randomized experiment found its ranking algorithm amplified the mainstream political right more than the left in six of seven countries studied (Huszár et al., PNAS 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34934011/). The disputed decisions are a handful of cases among billions; the systematic evidence shows no general suppression of conservative content.

Question: Are a platform’s recommendation algorithms and design features its own conduct, separate from the users’ speech it carries?
Tag: value
Poles: Design is conduct / Curation is speech
Why contested: Courts have split. The Supreme Court held in 2024 that platforms’ choices about what to show and how to arrange it are protected editorial expression (Moody v. NetChoice), and the Third Circuit then reasoned in Anderson v. TikTok that if curation is the platform’s own expression, it is not third-party content protected by Section 230. Whether infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks and engagement-maximizing recommendation are product features or editorial judgments decides whether they can be regulated or sued over.
Case for design is conduct: A platform that chooses to push a dangerous “blackout challenge” video to a ten-year-old is doing something its user did not do; the harm comes from the machine that selects, ranks and repeats, not from any single post. Features like infinite scroll, variable-reward notifications and stranger-messaging defaults for minors would be harmful whatever content flowed through them, and product-safety law has always covered defective design. Distinguishing the pipe from the posts lets the law reach the business model without anyone deciding what users may say. The EU’s 2026 findings against TikTok and Meta for addictive design, and US juries in 2026, have begun to draw exactly this line.
Case for curation is speech: Every choice about what to show first is an editorial judgment of the kind newspapers make, and the First Amendment protects it; the Supreme Court said so in Moody. Almost any harm claim can be recast as a design claim (“the algorithm showed it to me”), so a design exception would swallow Section 230 entirely and invite suits over every recommendation. Ranking is also how platforms demote harmful content; exposing ranking to liability discourages the very curation that reduces harm. Features such as autoplay matter only because of the content they deliver, so regulating them is regulating speech by another name.

Question: Should governments set binding legal duties for how platforms handle speech that is legal?
Tag: value
Poles: Democratic oversight / Keep state out
Why contested: The EU and UK now impose systemic duties that reach lawful content, framed as risk management rather than censorship, while the US Constitution sharply limits any law that turns on the content of lawful speech. The same rule reads as accountability to its supporters and as state censorship by proxy to its opponents, and the United States government now treats Europe’s rules as a threat to Americans’ speech.
Case for democratic oversight: A few private companies already decide what billions can say and see, under rules they write, enforce and change unilaterally, often to suit advertisers or political winds. Democratic oversight replaces that unaccountable private power with public rules, transparency, audits, appeal rights and independent researcher access. The Digital Services Act does not tell platforms to remove lawful posts; it requires them to assess and mitigate the risks their systems create and to explain their decisions, which respects speech more than opaque private moderation does. Every other powerful industry answers to public regulators; the idea that speech platforms alone should not is an artifact of American exceptionalism and industry lobbying.
Case for keep the state out: When the government sets duties about lawful speech, it controls the speech indirectly, and a “risk” duty covering disinformation or civic discourse invites regulators to lean on platforms over contested political content. Regulators change with governments; tools built by one administration are used by the next. The House Judiciary Committee’s majority documented European regulators pressing platforms over political speech, and the British government itself dropped “legal but harmful” duties for adults in 2022 because they would push firms to over-remove. Lawful speech should be governed by the people who post, read and host it, not by officials whose preferences inevitably shape what counts as harmful.

Question: When officials urge platforms to remove content, is that legitimate persuasion or censorship?
Tag: value
Poles: Pressure is censorship / Persuasion is legitimate
Why contested: Governments have a legitimate interest in flagging threats, terrorism, foreign influence operations and public-health falsehoods, but platforms depend on government for regulation, antitrust and contracts, so a request can carry an implied threat. The Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that the plaintiffs challenging Biden-era contacts lacked standing (Murthy v. Missouri, 6 to 3) without deciding where the line lies, and the practice now runs in the other direction under an administration that pressures platforms and foreign regulators itself.
Case for pressure is censorship: The First Amendment forbids the government from doing indirectly what it cannot do directly, and a request from the White House or a regulator to a company that needs its goodwill is rarely just a request. In the Missouri litigation the Fifth Circuit found in 2023 that White House and Surgeon General’s office officials had coerced platforms, before the Supreme Court set the ruling aside on standing grounds (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murthy_v._Missouri). In March 2026 the federal government agreed to a consent order barring the Surgeon General, the CDC and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency from threatening platforms into removing protected speech (Public Knowledge, https://publicknowledge.org/murthy-v-missouri-settlement-does-nothing-to-combat-illegal-jawboning/). Whatever the party in power, informal pressure escapes the accountability that formal law would face.
Case for persuasion is legitimate: Governments hold information platforms need: which accounts are foreign intelligence fronts, which posts are coordinating violence, which health claims are dangerous in a pandemic. Sharing it and asking platforms to enforce their own rules is not censorship when the platform remains free to say no, as platforms often did. Treating every contact as coercion leaves platforms blind to threats. The practice has continued under the current administration, which has pressed platforms and broadcasters and imposed visa bans on a former EU commissioner and European campaigners over their dealings with platforms, which suggests the real dispute is over whose requests are made, not whether governments make them.

Question: Can crowd-sourced notes and user controls do the work that removal does?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Context suffices / Removal still needed
Why contested: X and then Meta replaced professional fact-checking with Community Notes, and the UK chose user filtering tools over removal duties for adults, so the question is being tested at scale; the early evidence shows notes work when shown but are shown rarely and late.
Case for context suffices: Notes written and rated by users across political lines are trusted more than fact-checks by professionals many users regard as partisan, and they add information rather than suppress it. When attached, they cut reposts of false posts by nearly half. Average time to publication on X fell from more than 100 days in 2022 to 14 days in 2025. Filters and labels respect adults’ right to decide what they see, avoid the error rate of mass removal, and scale without a company or state appointing itself arbiter of truth.
Case for removal still needed: Only about 7 percent of English-language notes on X and about 6 percent on Meta have ever been published, because the bridging requirement fails on exactly the most polarizing claims, and over a post’s lifetime notes cut reposts by only about 12 percent because most sharing happens before a note appears. Notes do nothing against harassment, threats, pile-ons or self-harm content, where context is not the problem. Meta’s Oversight Board warned in March 2026 that expanding notes globally poses human rights risks in elections, conflicts and repressive states (https://www.oversightboard.com/decision/pao-007g5zuv/).

Question: Should platforms owe children stronger protections than adults, even if that means verifying everyone’s age?
Tag: value
Poles: Protect children first / Keep the open internet
Why contested: Nearly everyone agrees that some lawful content is unsuitable for children, but protecting children in practice requires knowing who is a child, which means age checks for all users, and duties to shield minors from “harmful” content can spread to what adults see.
Case for protect children first: Children cannot consent to engagement-maximizing design and are uniquely vulnerable to content promoting self-harm, eating disorders and dangerous challenges. Societies routinely restrict children’s access to lawful products, from alcohol to gambling to pornography, and age checks are standard offline. The UK’s children’s safety duties, the EU’s actions on minors and US state design codes set a floor that platforms had years to reach voluntarily and did not. Age assurance technology has improved and can be done without handing identity documents to platforms.
Case for keep the open internet: Age verification for all users ends anonymous access to lawful speech, creates databases of identity documents vulnerable to breach, and falls hardest on those who most need anonymity, including abuse survivors and LGBTQ youth seeking community. Duties to shield minors from harmful content lead platforms to restrict whole topics, such as sexual health or mental health discussion, for everyone. Lower courts have blocked many state social media laws on these grounds, and the Congressional Research Service notes that laws targeting particular content face strict scrutiny while content-neutral design rules may face a lower standard (CRS, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R49308/R49308.2.pdf). The Supreme Court did uphold Texas’s age-verification requirement for pornography sites in 2025 (Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Speech_Coalition_v._Paxton), which age-check advocates read as opening the door and opponents as limited to sexual material obscene for minors.

Question: Should the largest platforms be treated as common carriers, required to host lawful speech?
Tag: value
Poles: Must carry lawful speech / Platforms choose
Why contested: Texas and Florida passed laws in 2021 forbidding large platforms from removing users or posts based on viewpoint; the Supreme Court held in 2024 that content curation is protected expression and sent the cases back, while three justices, in a concurrence by Justice Alito, doubted that the challengers had shown the laws invalid, and Justice Thomas had suggested in a 2021 concurrence that large platforms might be regulated as common carriers (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moody_v._NetChoice,_LLC; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biden_v._Knight_First_Amendment_Institute_at_Columbia_University). The question divides the right as well as separating it from the left.
Case for must carry: A handful of platforms function as the public square, and being banned from them is close to exclusion from public life. Telephone companies, railroads and the post office were long required to serve all comers without judging the message, and platforms with network effects hold similar power. They enjoy a federal liability shield on the theory that they are conduits, not publishers; they should not get the benefits of both roles. Requiring viewpoint neutrality for lawful speech protects users from unaccountable private censorship without the government deciding anything about content.
Case for platforms choose: Deciding what to host is the core of editorial freedom, and forcing a private service to carry speech it rejects is compelled speech; Moody v. NetChoice said as much. A must-carry rule would require platforms to host spam, harassment and extremist propaganda that happens to be lawful, driving away users and advertisers and making the services worse for everyone. Section 230’s second clause exists precisely so that platforms can remove objectionable material without becoming liable for what they leave up. If users dislike a platform’s rules, competition and new services, from Bluesky to Truth Social, are the remedy.

## Positions

Camp: Hold platforms legally responsible for harm
Adherents:
- European Commission (institution), which enforces the Digital Services Act’s requirement that the largest platforms identify and reduce systemic risks including illegal content, threats to electoral processes, gender-based violence, public health and the protection of minors and “physical and mental wellbeing”: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/digital-services-act-package
- Senators Lindsey Graham and Dick Durbin, lead sponsors of the Sunset Section 230 Act; Graham: “Giant social media platforms are unregulated, immune from lawsuits and are making billions of dollars in advertising revenue off some of the most unsavory content and criminal activity imaginable”; Durbin: “Children are being exploited and abused because Big Tech consistently prioritizes profits over people. Enough is enough.”: https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/press/dem/releases/durbin-graham-introduce-bill-to-sunset-section-230-immunity-for-tech-companies-protect-americans-online
- Also in this camp: Representatives Mark DeSaulnier and Jimmy Patronis (House bill, https://desaulnier.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/representatives-desaulnier-and-patronis-introduce-legislation-hold-tech); the Center for Countering Digital Hate; the UK government and Ofcom under the Online Safety Act; the Molly Rose Foundation; Brazil’s Supreme Federal Court majority.
Variation inside the camp: Americans in this camp mostly want to repeal or sunset Section 230 so that ordinary tort law, applied by courts and juries, decides when a platform is responsible; Europeans and the British prefer a regulator enforcing duties to assess and reduce risk, with fines rather than lawsuits. Some want duties that reach lawful content for adults; others, like the final UK law, limit lawful-content duties to children and give adults filtering tools. The camp spans the left and parts of the right: US repeal bills have equal numbers of Democratic and Republican sponsors, some of whom see repeal mainly as a way to curb platforms’ power over political speech.
Case: Every other industry that profits from a product answers for the harm it foreseeably causes. Platforms are not passive bulletin boards; they are some of the most sophisticated and profitable businesses in history, and they earn more when content provokes, enrages and keeps people scrolling, including content that harasses, deceives and endangers children. For thirty years Section 230 has told them that no matter what their systems amplify, no victim can hold them to account in court. The result is predictable: safety teams are cut when budgets tighten, as at X after 2022 and at Meta in 2025, because nothing forces them to be kept. We do not ask platforms to review every post in advance or to censor opinions. We ask what the law asks of a carmaker or a drug company: identify the risks your product creates, take reasonable steps to reduce them, and pay when you fail. Europe has shown this can be done through transparency, audits and risk duties without shutting down online speech, and the tech industry’s warnings that every such law would break the internet have not come true.
Best evidence: Platforms’ own records show they can reduce harm when they choose to, and let it rise when they stop: after Meta loosened enforcement in January 2025, CCDH counted violent threats against 100 members of Congress on Facebook rising from 1,800 to 7,600 in six months (https://counterhate.com/blog/violent-threats-against-members-of-congress-quadrupled-after-meta-rolled-back-moderation-policies/). X told Australia’s regulator it cut its trust and safety staff by 30 percent and its safety engineers by 80 percent in seven months. Twitter’s removal of 70,000 accounts after January 6th reduced misinformation circulation (McCabe et al., Nature 2024). The UK coroner found that online content contributed to Molly Russell’s death. And the Digital Services Act’s first fine, against X in December 2025, was about deceptive design and blocked research access, not about any user’s opinion, which shows systemic duties need not mean censorship.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that liability will make platforms delete lawful speech to avoid lawsuits. But platforms already remove speech on a vast scale under their own rules, 99.8 percent of European moderation decisions cite terms of service, not law, and they do it with no due process, no transparency and no appeal to anyone independent. Accountability adds exactly those things: notice, reasons, appeal, audits and researcher access. A duty of reasonable care is judged by what a careful company would do, not by whether a single post slipped through, and a two-year sunset gives Congress time to write a careful standard. The alternative, trusting companies to police themselves, has been tried for thirty years.

Camp: Protect lawful viewpoints from removal
Adherents:
- Federal Trade Commission under Chairman Andrew Ferguson (institution), which in February 2025 opened an inquiry into how platforms “deny or degrade” users’ access “based on the content of their speech or affiliations”; Ferguson: “Tech firms should not be bullying their users. This inquiry will help the FTC better understand how these firms may have violated the law by silencing and intimidating Americans for speaking their minds.”: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/federal-trade-commission-launches-inquiry-tech-censorship
- Representative Jim Jordan and the Republican majority of the House Judiciary Committee, whose July 2025 staff report calls the Digital Services Act “a powerful censorship law that gives European regulators the ability to suppress speech globally”: https://judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/republicans-judiciary.house.gov/files/2025-07/DSA_Report%26Appendix%2807.25.25%29.pdf
- Also in this camp: President Trump’s 20 January 2025 executive order “Restoring Freedom of Speech and Ending Federal Censorship” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/restoring-freedom-of-speech-and-ending-federal-censorship/); the State Department under Marco Rubio; FCC Chairman Brendan Carr; the Texas and Florida legislatures that passed the 2021 must-carry laws; Elon Musk.
Variation inside the camp: Some in this camp want the state to require large platforms to carry lawful speech regardless of viewpoint, as Texas and Florida tried; others would rely on consumer-protection law against platforms that break their own promises of neutrality, on transparency rules, or on narrowing Section 230’s protection for removals so that platforms that act as censors lose publisher immunity. Some Republicans who share this camp’s concerns, such as Senator Josh Hawley, whose 2019 Ending Support for Internet Censorship Act would have made large platforms’ Section 230 immunity depend on an FTC audit certifying their moderation as politically neutral (https://www.hawley.senate.gov/senator-hawley-introduces-legislation-amend-section-230-immunity-big-tech-companies), also cosponsor full repeal. Nearly all prefer counter-speech tools such as Community Notes to removal, and oppose foreign laws like the Digital Services Act and the UK Online Safety Act when they reach American speech.
Case: The danger in content moderation is not that platforms leave up too much; it is that a few companies, often prodded by government officials, have decided which lawful opinions Americans may share on the platforms where political life now happens. During the pandemic and the 2020 election, platforms removed or throttled true reporting, legitimate scientific hypotheses and ordinary political dissent, and the White House leaned on them to do more. “Misinformation” and “hate” turned out to mean whatever the people writing the rules disagreed with. Meta itself has now admitted its rules suffered from mission creep and that one to two in ten of its removals may have been mistakes. The answer is not to hand platforms or regulators new duties to police lawful speech, which in practice means more removal of disfavored views, but to stop the government from pressuring platforms, hold platforms to their promises of fair treatment, and resist foreign regimes that would export their speech codes to Americans.
Best evidence: Meta’s own January 2025 admission of over-enforcement, and its report that US enforcement mistakes fell by half once it narrowed its rules while prevalence of most harmful content barely moved (https://transparency.meta.com/reports/integrity-reports-q1-2025/). Mark Zuckerberg’s August 2024 letter to the House Judiciary Committee saying Biden administration officials “repeatedly pressured” Meta to remove COVID-19 content, including humor and satire, and that Meta should not have demoted the Hunter Biden laptop story. Facebook’s four-month ban on claims that COVID-19 was man-made, lifted in May 2021. The Future of Free Speech finding that 87.5 to 99.7 percent of comments deleted from large Facebook pages and YouTube channels in France, Germany and Sweden were legal. And public opinion: 83 percent of Americans think platforms intentionally censor political viewpoints.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that platforms are private companies with their own free speech rights, and that forcing them to carry speech is itself government control. But the camp’s central demand is that government stop pressuring platforms to remove speech, which protects platforms’ independence rather than infringing it. Where the camp does favor rules, they are the kind long applied to dominant carriers and to firms that break promises to consumers, and they leave platforms free to remove spam, pornography, illegal content and harassment under clear, evenly applied rules. What platforms should not be able to do is claim immunity as neutral conduits for everything they carry while acting as partisan editors of political debate.

Camp: Keep the liability shield and platforms’ editorial freedom
Adherents:
- Electronic Frontier Foundation (institution): Section 230 lets “countless niche websites, as well as big platforms” host user speech, and without it “the biggest platforms would likely become locked-down and heavily censored” and “the next great websites and apps won’t even get started”: https://www.eff.org/issues/cda230
- Senator Ron Wyden, co-author of Section 230, at its thirtieth anniversary in February 2026: “individuals who post on the internet are responsible for that content, not the people who run the platform,” while “the big guys should do more to moderate”: https://www.klcc.org/politics-government/2026-02-12/wyden-remains-in-support-of-section-230-the-internet-company-protection-law-he-authored-31-years-ago
- Also in this camp: NetChoice, whose 2024 statement warned that a Section 230 sunset would bring “less competition” and curb Americans’ ability to speak online (https://netchoice.org/congress-shouldnt-end-the-internets-sunrise-with-a-section-230-sunset-repeal/); the Center for Democracy & Technology; the Cato Institute; the Wikimedia Foundation; former Representative Chris Cox; the Internet Society; the legal scholar Eric Goldman.
Variation inside the camp: Civil-liberties groups in this camp want platforms both free and encouraged to moderate responsibly, and often press them to moderate better, with more transparency and fairer appeals; industry groups emphasize competition and innovation. Some, including the Center for Democracy & Technology, accept that product-liability claims unrelated to publishing user content can proceed; others, such as the Cato Institute, see design-defect suits as a way of circumventing Section 230. Wyden himself has said Section 230 does not protect content generated by AI systems. The camp opposes both repeal and must-carry laws, and generally the European and British approach as well.
Case: Section 230 is the reason the internet is built on user speech. It says a simple thing: the person who posts something is responsible for it, not the site that hosts it, and a site that removes some offensive material does not thereby become liable for everything it leaves up. Without it, every review site, forum, comment section, Wikipedia page and small community would face lawsuits over what users say, and the rational response would be to host far less and remove anything anyone complains about. The largest companies would survive that world; their small competitors and nonprofits would not. Repeal would not make platforms more careful; it would make them more cautious, which means taking down speech about sexual health, abortion, police misconduct, #MeToo accusations and unpopular politics. The First Amendment also protects platforms’ right to choose what to carry, so the Texas and Florida approach is unconstitutional, as the Supreme Court signaled in 2024. The right answer to bad moderation is competition, transparency and user choice, not courts and regulators deciding what lawful speech may stay up.
Best evidence: The 2018 FOSTA-SESTA carve-out from Section 230 led Craigslist to close its personals section and other sites to remove content used by sex workers for safety, while federal prosecutors brought only one case under the new criminal provision by 2021 (GAO, https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-385). In jurisdictions with notice-and-takedown liability, most removed content is lawful: 87.5 to 99.7 percent in the Future of Free Speech study of three European countries, highest in Germany under its strict NetzDG law. The Internet Association’s review of more than 500 Section 230 decisions found the immunity was the primary basis in only 42 percent, with many of the rest dismissed on the merits, suggesting its main function is an early, affordable exit from suits that would fail anyway. And public opinion does not favor suing platforms: 63 percent of Americans said harassment targets should not be able to sue the platform (Pew, 2020).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the shield lets hugely profitable companies escape accountability for harms their own products cause. But Section 230 has never protected platforms from federal criminal law, intellectual property law or liability for content they create themselves, and courts have allowed claims about platforms’ own conduct to proceed. What it blocks is liability for other people’s speech, and there the question is not whether harm exists but whether making a host liable reduces it without destroying far more lawful speech. The evidence from FOSTA and Europe says it does not. Harms caused by platforms’ business practices are better addressed by privacy law, competition law and transparency requirements that do not require anyone to decide which lawful words are too dangerous to host.

Camp: Hold platforms liable for design, not speech
Adherents:
- Public Knowledge (institution), April 2026: “the product liability theory of platform accountability has merit,” and courts should “address genuinely harmful design features without reaching into the user-content layer that Section 230 and the First Amendment are meant to protect”; it names infinite scroll, autoplay, variable-reward notifications, stranger messaging defaults for minors and location-sharing maps as features that should not be shielded: https://publicknowledge.org/where-section-230-ends-and-accountability-begins/
- Jaron Lanier, Allison Stanger and Audrey Tang, “Sunset and Renew: Section 230 Should Protect Human Speech, Not Algorithmic Virality” (Harvard Ash Center, October 2024), proposing to keep protection for human expression while holding platforms accountable for engagement-driven amplification: https://ash.harvard.edu/articles/sunset-and-renew-section-230-should-protect-human-speech-not-algorithmic-virality/
- Also in this camp: the sponsors of the Kids Online Safety Act’s design-based duty of care; the state attorneys general and plaintiffs’ lawyers bringing addictive-design cases; the Third Circuit panel in Anderson v. TikTok (2024) in its reasoning; the whistleblower Frances Haugen.
Variation inside the camp: The camp divides over recommendation algorithms. Some, like Public Knowledge, would keep algorithmic ranking protected as editorial judgment and target only features that cause harm regardless of content, such as endless feeds and compulsive notifications. Others, like Lanier, Stanger and Tang, would remove protection for engagement-optimized amplification itself. Some want courts to draw the line under existing law; others want Congress to write it into a narrowed Section 230 or a design-based duty of care.
Case: Both the repealers and the defenders of Section 230 are fighting about the wrong thing. The harm that has turned parents, juries and regulators against platforms mostly does not come from any user’s words; it comes from product choices the companies make: feeds engineered never to end, notifications timed to pull children back, recommendation systems that learn a lonely teenager lingers on self-harm content and serve more of it. Those are the company’s own conduct. Holding a company liable for its design leaves users’ speech untouched and puts responsibility where the decision was made, which is exactly how product-safety law treats every other consumer product. Keep the shield for hosting speech, so that no site is sued for what its users say; remove it for the machinery that decides what to push at whom and how hard.
Best evidence: On 25 March 2026 a Los Angeles jury found Meta and YouTube negligent in the design of their apps in a case brought by a young woman identified as K.G.M., the first of three bellwether trials in California, on a theory framed around design rather than content (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K.G.M._v._Meta_et_al.). On 6 February 2026 the European Commission preliminarily found TikTok in breach of the Digital Services Act for addictive design, citing infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and its recommender system, enforcement aimed at a platform’s architecture rather than its content (https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-preliminarily-finds-tiktoks-addictive-design-breach-digital-services-act); it issued similar preliminary findings against Instagram and Facebook on 10 July 2026 (Slaughter and May, https://thelens.slaughterandmay.com/post/102nbs3/meta-joins-tiktok-in-the-commissions-crosshairs-over-addictive-design-under-th). Twitter’s randomized experiment showed that its ranking algorithm, not users, determined which political content was amplified (Huszár et al., PNAS 2022). The Congressional Research Service notes that content-neutral design regulations may face a lower level of First Amendment scrutiny than content rules (https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R49308/R49308.2.pdf).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from both flanks, is that the line between design and content cannot be held: every feature delivers content, so “design” claims will become a backdoor to liability for speech. The test answers it: ask whether the alleged harm would exist no matter what content flowed through the feature. Compulsive-use loops, nighttime notifications to children and default location sharing fail that test; a claim that the platform showed someone an offensive post passes it and stays barred. Courts draw comparable lines in product law all the time, and a clear statutory standard would draw it better than today’s patchwork of conflicting rulings.

Camp: Give users control of moderation
Adherents:
- Bluesky Social (institution), March 2024: “there is no one-size-fits-all approach to moderation — no single company can get online safety right for every country, culture, and community in the world”; users can “install filters from independent moderation services, layering them like building blocks”: https://bsky.social/about/blog/03-12-2024-stackable-moderation
- Francis Fukuyama (Stanford University), “Making the Internet Safe for Democracy,” Journal of Democracy, April 2021: “The power of large internet platforms to amplify or silence certain voices at a scale that can alter major political outcomes poses a grave threat to democracy,” proposing to “outsource content curation from the dominant platforms to a competitive layer of ‘middleware companies’”: https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/making-the-internet-safe-for-democracy/
- Also in this camp: Jack Dorsey, the Twitter co-founder, and advocates of open social protocols; Project Liberty; Mike Masnick, author of “Protocols, Not Platforms”; the Stanford working group on platform scale that proposed middleware. Daphne Keller of Stanford has analyzed middleware sympathetically while setting out its obstacles (https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/the-future-of-platform-power-making-middleware-work/).
Variation inside the camp: Some pursue this through new decentralized networks built on open protocols, such as Bluesky’s AT Protocol and the Mastodon fediverse; others want regulation, such as interoperability and data-access mandates, that forces dominant platforms to let third-party curation and filtering services plug in. The UK’s choice of user filtering tools over “legal but harmful” duties for adults is a partial version inside an otherwise regulatory framework, and Community Notes, where users rather than staff supply context, is a related idea inside a single company.
Case: The question “should platforms or governments decide which lawful speech people see?” has no good answer, because both put one central authority in charge of speech for billions of people with different values, languages and tolerances. The problem is not whether moderation is too strict or too loose; it is that there is one dial and someone else holds it. Split the job up. Let a base layer handle what is illegal, and let users choose among competing filters, labelers and feeds run by communities, publishers, advocacy groups or anyone they trust, so a parent can pick strict filters for a child, a researcher can see everything, and a community can enforce its own norms. That removes the incentive to capture the central moderator, whether by a company’s advertisers or a government’s officials, and it lets people who disagree about harm live with the choices they make for themselves.
Best evidence: Bluesky has run composable moderation at scale since 2024, with independent labeling services users can subscribe to on top of the company’s own base moderation (https://docs.bsky.app/docs/advanced-guides/moderation). Community Notes, which relies on users rather than staff and requires agreement across viewpoints, reduces reposts of false posts by 46 percent once attached (Slaughter et al., PNAS 2025) and draws trust from users who distrust professional fact-checkers. The Kozyreva experiment found Americans’ moderation preferences vary widely by party and topic, which is the heterogeneity a single central standard cannot satisfy. And middleware, as Keller notes, raises fewer First Amendment problems than laws telling platforms what to remove or carry.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that user choice creates filter bubbles and leaves harassment targets unprotected, since abusers will simply choose filters that show them their victims. The camp does not propose abandoning base-layer rules: illegal content, threats and targeted harassment remain the host’s responsibility, and blocking and muting tools protect targets whatever filters others use. For the vast gray zone of lawful content, the choice is not between user choice and a neutral, expert moderator; it is between user choice and a moderator who answers to shareholders or to whichever government holds power. Filter bubbles are a real cost, but a smaller one than a single point of control over what billions may say.

## Crux

The disagreement under the disagreements: when someone is given the power and the duty to police lawful but harmful speech on platforms used by billions, does that reduce real harm by more than it suppresses legitimate speech, and whom, if anyone, should be trusted to hold that power: the companies, democratic governments, courts through liability, or users themselves?

Every camp answers this question, usually without saying so. The liability camp believes duties and lawsuits will make companies prevent more harm than the lawful speech they sweep away, and trusts courts and regulators more than companies. The viewpoint-protection camp believes the power to remove lawful speech has been used, by companies and by governments leaning on them, against disfavored political views, and trusts neither with more of it. The shield camp believes liability would multiply removals of lawful speech without matching gains in safety, and prefers imperfect private editors to public ones. The design camp believes the harm lives mostly in product mechanics rather than in speech, so the trade-off can be largely avoided. The user-control camp believes no central holder of the power can be trusted and that the trade-off should be made by each person for themselves.

What evidence could settle: how much harm moderation of lawful content actually prevents, and how much lawful speech is lost to it. These are measurable in principle. The natural experiments are running now: X’s cuts to safety staff after 2022, Meta’s January 2025 retreat, the EU’s Digital Services Act and the UK’s Online Safety Act in force, Brazil’s June 2025 move to notice-and-takedown liability, and US design-liability verdicts. Researcher access to platform data under the Digital Services Act, audits of removal accuracy, and before-and-after studies of harassment, threats, self-harm and radicalization in these settings could show whether stronger duties produce measurable safety gains, whether they produce measurable over-removal of lawful and political speech, and whether design rules achieve the gains without the speech costs. Evidence could also show whether enforcement falls unevenly on political groups once differences in posting behavior are accounted for. Today the evidence is thin on both sides: the best studies of removal cannot estimate the size of the effect, the best studies of over-removal cannot say who deleted what or whether the deleted speech broke the platform’s own rules, and platforms control most of the data.

What no data can close: how to weigh a concrete harm to an identifiable person, a harassed teenager, a threatened official, a child shown self-harm content, against a diffuse loss of lawful speech spread across millions who each lose a little, including speech that later proves to have been right. And whose judgment about harm to trust: a company answerable to shareholders and advertisers, a government answerable to voters but also to whoever next holds office, a court applying standards set after the fact, or individuals choosing for themselves at the cost of shared norms. People who have seen power over speech abused by governments weigh the risk of state involvement differently from people who have seen harm left unchecked by companies, and both experiences are real. Even perfect evidence about effects would leave this choice open.
