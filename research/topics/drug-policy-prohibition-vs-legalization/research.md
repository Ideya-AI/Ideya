# Drug Policy — research

## Overview

This topic is about how governments should treat drugs that are now illegal, and the people who use them. At one end is prohibition: making it a crime to produce, sell and possess drugs such as cannabis, cocaine, heroin, methamphetamine and fentanyl, and enforcing those laws with police, courts and prisons, and in some countries with the death penalty or military force. Alongside it sit several alternatives: decriminalization, which keeps drug supply illegal but stops treating personal possession as a crime; legal regulation, in which the state licenses and controls a legal market, as many US states, Canada and Uruguay now do for cannabis; and approaches to people who use drugs that range from abstinence-based recovery, including treatment people are required to accept, to harm reduction, which aims to keep people alive and healthy while they still use, through naloxone, syringe services, supervised consumption sites and drug checking. The topic covers the evidence on overdose deaths, drug use, crime, incarceration and racial disparities in enforcement, and the experience of places that have changed course, from Portugal’s decriminalization in 2001 to Oregon’s and British Columbia’s experiments and reversals. It deliberately excludes the pricing and patenting of prescription medicines (covered in the drug pricing topic), policing practice and sentencing in general (covered in the policing reform and crime and punishment topics), alcohol and tobacco policy except as points of comparison, and the medical use of psychedelics as therapy.

Why now: on 23 April 2026 the US Justice Department moved marijuana covered by a state medical license, and marijuana in FDA-approved drugs, from Schedule I to Schedule III of the Controlled Substances Act, ending the blanket placement of marijuana in Schedule I, the strictest category, where it had sat since the act took effect in 1970, acting on President Trump’s 18 December 2025 executive order to expedite rescheduling (NPR, https://www.npr.org/2026/04/23/nx-s1-5797245/marijuana-rules-reschedule-medical-legalization; Gibson Dunn, https://www.gibsondunn.com/dea-downschedules-state-medical-marijuana-to-schedule-iii-expedited-hearing-set-to-consider-broader-rescheduling/). All other marijuana, including the adult-use cannabis sold legally in 24 states, stays in Schedule I. The Drug Enforcement Administration held an 11-day hearing on rescheduling all marijuana from 29 June to 15 July 2026 (Morgan Lewis, 24 September 2026, https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/09/high-time-for-a-change-post-dea-hearing-broader-marijuana-rescheduling-questions-remain), and on 29 September 2026 its chief administrative law judge stayed the case while he weighs a motion by opponents of rescheduling to add a new Government Accountability Office report on drug-scheduling procedures to the record, with the government’s response due 13 October (The Marijuana Herald, https://themarijuanaherald.com/2026/09/dea-judge-stays-marijuana-rescheduling-case-gives-government-until-october-13-to-respond/). The same administration has taken a harder line on drug supply and on harm reduction: since 2 September 2025 US forces have carried out at least 78 strikes on boats the government says were carrying drugs in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific, killing at least 234 people by 21 September 2026 (InSight Crime, https://insightcrime.org/news/timeline-us-strikes-against-alleged-drug-boats/) [Correction 2026-10-05: as of October 4, 2026, at least 235 people had been killed in at least 70 US military strikes since September 2, 2025 (Associated Press via ABC News, latest US strike on alleged drug-smuggling boat, October 4, 2026, https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/us-military-latest-strike-alleged-drug-smuggling-boat-136994059).], and a July 2025 executive order directed federal health grants away from “so-called ‘harm reduction’ or ‘safe consumption’ efforts” and toward civil commitment and drug courts (Federal Register, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/07/29/2025-14391/ending-crime-and-disorder-on-americas-streets). On the same day as the rescheduling order, the International Criminal Court confirmed crimes-against-humanity charges against former Philippine president Rodrigo Duterte over killings in his drug war and sent the case to trial (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/23/icc-confirms-crimes-against-humanity-trial-of-ex-philippine-leader-duterte). Voters in Massachusetts will decide on 3 November 2026 whether to end the state’s legal retail cannabis market (Question 8; The Marijuana Herald, https://themarijuanaherald.com/2026/10/marijuana-and-the-2026-election-what-voters-will-decide-on-november-3/).

The debate is live because the evidence is moving and pointing in more than one direction. US overdose deaths, which peaked at about 112,000 in the 12 months to August 2023, have fallen to about 67,000 in the 12 months to April 2026 (CDC provisional data, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/vsrr/drug-overdose-data.htm), and each side credits its own policies. Experiments with decriminalization have been reversed: Oregon made possession of hard drugs a crime again on 1 September 2024 after three and a half years of Measure 110 (OPB, https://www.opb.org/article/2024/04/01/drug-possession-oregon-kotek-sign-bill/), and British Columbia let its decriminalization pilot expire on 31 January 2026, its health minister saying it “did not deliver the results” hoped for (Global News, https://globalnews.ca/news/11613636/bc-ends-three-year-drug-decriminalization-pilot-program/); studies of whether Oregon’s law raised deaths reach opposite conclusions, the latest, a July 2026 working paper, estimating about 1,186 excess deaths there from 2021 to 2023 (NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w35427). Worldwide, the UN Office on Drugs and Crime counts 331 million people who used an illegal drug in 2024, a record, and cocaine production more than four times its 2014 level (World Drug Report 2026, https://www.unodc.org/documents/data-and-analysis/WDR_2026/WDR26_Highlights.pdf), figures reformers read as proof that prohibition has failed and its defenders read as proof that enforcement must be stronger. On one side are governments that keep drugs illegal and push people toward recovery, such as the US administration and the province of Alberta, and anti-legalization groups such as Smart Approaches to Marijuana; in the middle are governments such as British Columbia’s and Oregon’s that keep possession illegal but fund treatment and harm reduction; further along are advocates of decriminalization such as Portugal’s drug agency and the Drug Policy Alliance; and at the far end are those who want legal, regulated markets for all drugs, such as the Transform Drug Policy Foundation, the Global Commission on Drug Policy and the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, who said in December 2024 that “the so-called War on Drugs has failed, completely and utterly” (UN News, https://news.un.org/en/story/2024/12/1157836).

Harm-reduction advocates, treatment advocates and enforcement advocates each cite the fall as evidence for their approach. Even after the decline, the 12-month total remains close to the level of 2019, about 72,000, the year before the pandemic-era surge.

The shift in what kills people is central to the policy debate. Heroin deaths have fallen from a peak of about 16,300 in the 12 months to June 2017 to about 2,100, as fentanyl displaced heroin in the illicit supply; deaths from prescription painkillers peaked around 2017 as prescribing was curbed. Reformers argue that this succession, from prescription pills to heroin to fentanyl to adulterants such as xylazine and nitazenes, shows how prohibition pushes markets toward ever more potent and concealable drugs. Defenders of enforcement argue that it shows the need to disrupt the supply chains, mostly running through Mexico with precursor chemicals from China, that bring fentanyl into the country.

Note: An estimated 331 million people, 6.2 percent of the world’s population aged 15 to 64, used an illegal drug in 2024, up from 5.2 percent in 2014.
Context: The number is 34 percent higher than a decade earlier; UNODC attributes part of the rise to population growth of about 10 percent and better data, and part to a real increase in prevalence. Cannabis users grew by 40 percent over the decade, and the highest regional rates of use are in Oceania (15.5 percent) and the Americas (15 percent). The opioid figure counts people using any opioid non-medically, almost half of them using opiates such as heroin and opium. UNODC stresses that most drug use is occasional, and that a smaller group with frequent and dependent use accounts for most of the harm: an estimated 63 million people had a drug use disorder in 2024, a separate figure that coincidentally matches the opioid count. Both sides of the debate use the rising totals: reformers say five decades of global prohibition under the UN drug conventions have not reduced use, and supporters of prohibition say use would be far higher if drugs were legal and that the trend argues for stronger prevention and enforcement.

Drug deaths include both direct deaths, such as overdoses, and indirect ones, such as liver disease from hepatitis C acquired through injecting. The UN Office on Drugs and Crime, using the Global Burden of Disease study, puts drug-related deaths at about 492,000 in 2023, 29 percent more than a decade earlier, with hepatitis C-related liver disease the largest single cause (46 percent) (World Drug Report 2026, https://www.unodc.org/documents/data-and-analysis/WDR_2026/WDR26_Highlights.pdf). Advocates of legal regulation use the comparison to argue that legal status does not track harm, since the two deadliest substances are legal. Defenders of prohibition reply that alcohol and tobacco kill so many precisely because they are legal, commercial and widely used, and that legalizing other drugs would add to that toll rather than replace it.

Every camp in the debate wants more treatment; they disagree about whether the law should push people into it and whether abstinence or ongoing medication and safer use should be the goal.

Note: People dependent on opioids died at about one third the rate while in methadone treatment as when out of it, 11.3 against 36.1 deaths per 1,000 person-years.
Context: The meta-analysis pooled 19 cohorts following 122,885 people treated with methadone and 15,831 treated with buprenorphine. The risk was highest in the first four weeks of starting methadone and in the weeks just after leaving either treatment. The buprenorphine cohorts had lower death rates both in and out of treatment, so the rows for the two medications are not directly comparable. The authors caution that comparisons in and out of treatment may be affected by confounding and selection. Medication treatment sits awkwardly in the abstinence-versus-harm-reduction debate: supporters of recovery-first approaches increasingly accept it as treatment, while some abstinence-based programs still regard it as replacing one drug with another.

SAMHSA’s 2025 survey counted 19.3 million people with a marijuana use disorder, the most common drug use disorder in the country, most of them mild (https://www.samhsa.gov/blog/release-2025-nsduh-using-data-to-drive-great-american-recovery).

## Questions

Question: Do criminal penalties for drug possession reduce drug use and its harms, or add to them?
Type: empirical
Poles: Penalties protect / Penalties harm
Why contested: Drug laws are meant to deter use, push people toward treatment and keep public spaces orderly, but they also produce arrests, criminal records and imprisonment, which carry their own costs. Countries and states that changed their laws did so alongside other changes, from fentanyl’s arrival to new treatment funding, so isolating the effect of the law itself is hard, and the leading natural experiments, Portugal, Oregon and British Columbia, point in different directions.
Case for “Penalties protect”: The threat of arrest deters some people from starting to use drugs and gives police and courts leverage to get people who are addicted into treatment they would not otherwise seek. When Oregon removed criminal penalties in 2021, almost no one called the treatment hotline that was supposed to replace them, open-air drug use spread in Portland, and overdose deaths rose faster than in most states; a July 2026 study estimated about 1,186 excess overdose deaths in Oregon and 1,895 in Washington from 2021 to 2023 relative to matched comparison states (NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w35427). British Columbia ended its pilot after three years because, in its government’s words, it did not deliver the results hoped for. Long-term US cannabis data show use rising when policy loosens and falling when it tightens. People with severe addiction are often isolated from family and work, so, as Stanford’s Keith Humphreys has put it, if there is no legal pressure there is no pressure at all.
Case for “Penalties harm”: Most people who use drugs never become addicted, and those who do are not deterred by laws they already break; criminal records instead cost people jobs, housing and custody of their children, and fear of arrest makes people use alone and hide overdoses. Portugal, which stopped treating possession as a crime in 2001 and invested in treatment, has among the lowest overdose death rates in Europe, and use there did not surge. Studies that account for fentanyl’s arrival in Oregon, which coincided with Measure 110, find no association between decriminalization and overdose deaths (Zoorob et al., JAMA Network Open, 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39235814/). The United States arrested more than 800,000 people for drug offenses in 2024, most for possession, and enforcement falls far more heavily on Black Americans, who use drugs at similar rates to white Americans.

Question: Did Oregon’s and Washington’s decriminalization raise overdose deaths, or did fentanyl?
Type: empirical
Poles: Decriminalization did / Fentanyl did
Why contested: Oregon’s Measure 110 took effect on 1 February 2021, and Washington’s Supreme Court struck down its possession law in State v. Blake weeks later, at almost exactly the time fentanyl was flooding West Coast drug markets. Overdose deaths rose steeply in both states, and the studies disagree mainly over how to separate the two events. The answer bears directly on whether other places should try decriminalization.
Case for “Decriminalization did”: Synthetic-control studies that compare Oregon and Washington with similar states find more deaths after the law changed: Noah Spencer estimated 182 additional overdose deaths in Oregon in 2021 alone, 23 percent more than predicted (Journal of Health Economics, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37556870/), and David Hall, Benjamin Hansen and Kyutaro Matsuzawa estimate about 1,186 excess deaths in Oregon and 1,895 in Washington from 2021 to 2023, after drug arrests fell sharply (NBER, July 2026, https://www.nber.org/papers/w35427). They argue that the fentanyl measure other studies use partly reflects the fall in police drug seizures that decriminalization itself caused, so controlling for it hides the law’s effect, and that alternative fentanyl measures still show the law raising deaths. Oregon ranked among the states with the largest increases in overdose deaths while the law was in force.
Case for “Fentanyl did”: Fentanyl reached Oregon’s supply in the first half of 2021, at the same moment as Measure 110, and fentanyl drove overdose increases across the country regardless of drug laws. A study by Joshi and colleagues, including CDC researchers, found no statistically significant change in deaths in either state in the first year (JAMA Psychiatry, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37755815/). When Zoorob and colleagues adjusted for the timing of fentanyl’s arrival in each state, the apparent effect in Oregon reversed sign and lost significance (JAMA Network Open, 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39235814/). Measure 110’s treatment funding was slow to reach providers, so the experiment tested removing penalties without the health system Portugal built, and its critics are judging it on deaths that states without decriminalization also suffered.

Question: Should public money pay for harm reduction services such as supervised consumption sites, or should it go only to treatment aimed at recovery?
Type: value
Poles: Keep people alive / Aim for recovery
Why contested: Harm reduction accepts that some people will keep using and tries to make that use less deadly, through naloxone, sterile supplies, drug checking, supervised consumption sites and, in some places, prescribed alternatives to street drugs. Recovery-oriented approaches treat ending drug use as the goal and see services that ease ongoing use as delaying it. The US executive branch now directs federal grants away from “so-called ‘harm reduction’ or ‘safe consumption’ efforts,” while New York City, Rhode Island, Vancouver, Glasgow and much of Western Europe operate supervised sites. Naloxone itself is now broadly accepted, including in the 2026 US National Drug Control Strategy (National League of Cities summary, https://www.nlc.org/article/2026/06/17/white-house-releases-2026-national-drug-control-strategy/).
Case for “Keep people alive”: A dead person cannot recover. Supervised consumption sites put trained staff beside people when they overdose: New York’s OnPoint reports reversing 1,983 overdoses in its consumption rooms in four years, and STAT reported in December 2025 that no client had died at the facility, nor had a death been documented at any officially sanctioned site worldwide (https://www.statnews.com/2025/12/18/on-point-nyc-drug-consumption-site-trump-defiance/); after Vancouver’s Insite opened, overdose deaths within 500 meters fell 35 percent, against 9.3 percent in the rest of the city (Marshall et al., The Lancet, 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21497898/). New York’s two sites, the first sanctioned in the United States, were not associated with increases in crime or disorder nearby (Chalfin et al., JAMA Network Open, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37955901/). Syringe programs prevent HIV and hepatitis C, which cause more drug-related deaths worldwide than overdoses. These services are also often the first contact through which people later enter treatment.
Case for “Aim for recovery”: Services that make drug use easier without asking anything of the user can keep people in addiction for years, concentrate drug use and dealing in particular neighborhoods, and signal that daily use of fentanyl or methamphetamine is a tolerable way to live. The goal of policy should be getting people well, and the evidence for long-term recovery through treatment, housing and work is the strongest case for spending. British Columbia’s safer-supply program, which prescribed opioids to people who used street drugs, faced reports that prescribed drugs were being diverted and resold, and in February 2025 the province required that doses be taken in front of a health professional (Victoria Times Colonist, https://www.timescolonist.com/local-news/bc-ends-take-home-safer-supply-for-drug-users-to-stop-diversion-10257487). Alberta, which has built a recovery-oriented system with more than 10,000 new publicly funded treatment spaces, reports a nearly 40 percent fall in opioid deaths in 2024 compared with 2023 (Alberta Recovery Model, https://open.alberta.ca/publications/alberta-recovery-model).

Question: Is it right to compel people with severe addiction into treatment?
Type: value
Poles: Compel when needed / Voluntary care only
Why contested: Drug courts, civil commitment and new laws such as Alberta’s Compassionate Intervention Act, passed in May 2025, let courts order people into treatment, and the July 2025 US executive order encourages “maximally flexible” civil commitment. Opponents say forced treatment violates rights and works poorly; supporters say severe addiction erodes the capacity to choose, and leaving people on the street is not respect for their autonomy.
Case for “Compel when needed”: Addiction at its most severe takes away the very capacity for free choice that voluntary systems rely on, and people living on the street with fentanyl and methamphetamine use are dying in large numbers while waiting to be ready. Society already compels treatment for people whose mental illness makes them a danger to themselves, and families of people in crisis often ask for exactly that power. Drug courts give people a choice between treatment and jail and keep many out of prison. As Alberta’s premier, Danielle Smith, put it, “there is no compassion in leaving people to suffer in the throes of addiction” (Global News, https://globalnews.ca/news/11133527/alberta-government-mandatory-addiction-treatment).
Case for “Voluntary care only”: People forced into treatment often relapse when released, and their tolerance has fallen, which raises the risk of fatal overdose in the weeks after release; a meta-analysis of opioid treatment found the same spike in deaths in the weeks just after people leave treatment (Sordo et al., BMJ, 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28446428/). Involuntary detention for drug use has a poor record internationally, and UN agencies have called for closing compulsory drug detention centers. Money spent on locked facilities could fund voluntary treatment, for which millions are waiting: only 16 percent of Americans who needed substance use treatment received any in 2025. Treatment works best when people choose it.

Question: Has legalizing cannabis for adults done more good than harm?
Type: empirical
Poles: Net benefit / Net harm
Why contested: Twenty-four US states, Canada and Uruguay have more than a decade of experience between them, but outcomes move slowly, the products have changed, and many effects, on psychosis, impaired driving, youth use and the illicit market, are hard to measure. The answer shapes both the US rescheduling fight and whether legalization is a model for other drugs.
Case for “Net benefit”: Legalization has ended hundreds of thousands of arrests a year, most of them of young people and disproportionately of Black Americans, without the surge in teen use that opponents predicted: a study of national youth surveys found recreational legalization associated with an 8 percent decrease in the odds of teen marijuana use (Anderson et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31282944/). Legal markets offer tested products with labeled potency, generate tax revenue and take business from criminal organizations. Adults have a right to choose a substance that is less lethal than alcohol, and 55 percent of Americans say it should be legal for recreational use.
Case for “Net harm”: Legal commercial markets have produced products far more potent than the cannabis of earlier decades and an industry that profits from heavy users: more Americans now use cannabis daily or near daily than drink daily, 17.7 million against 14.7 million in 2022 (Caulkins, Addiction, 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38775461/). In Denmark, the share of schizophrenia cases associated with cannabis use disorder rose from about 2 percent in 1995 to 6 to 8 percent since 2010 as potency rose (Hjorthøj et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34287621/). Illicit markets persist in legal states, and federal rescheduling, opponents say, would hand a tax break to “Big Weed,” as Smart Approaches to Marijuana puts it.

Question: Would legal, regulated markets for drugs such as cocaine and heroin shrink criminal markets and deaths, or expand addiction?
Type: empirical
Poles: Regulate the market / Keep it prohibited
Why contested: No country has legalized the sale of cocaine, heroin or methamphetamine for non-medical use, so the evidence is indirect: alcohol prohibition in the United States, heroin-assisted treatment in Switzerland and elsewhere, prescribed safer supply in Canada, and the experience of cannabis and of legal drugs such as alcohol, tobacco and prescription opioids. The question turns on whether a legal market could be held to strict limits, and on how much prohibition itself causes the harms.
Case for “Regulate the market”: Prohibition hands a trade worth hundreds of billions of dollars to criminal organizations, fuels violence from Mexico to Ecuador and makes the supply unpredictable and adulterated, which is why people die from fentanyl they did not know they were taking. Cocaine production has quadrupled since 2014 despite five decades of enforcement (World Drug Report 2026). A regulated market, with sales by prescription or through licensed pharmacies, controls on potency and packaging and no advertising, would let people know what they are taking, starve cartels of revenue and free police for violent crime. As the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights put it in April 2025, “responsible regulation should aim to take control of illegal drug markets, and eliminate profits from illegal trafficking, criminality and violence.”
Case for “Keep it prohibited”: The opioid crisis began with a legal, regulated product sold by a licensed company, OxyContin, and the most deadly drugs in the world, tobacco and alcohol, are legal, heavily marketed and kill millions a year. Legal availability lowers prices and stigma and would bring many more people into use of highly addictive drugs, and the harms would land hardest on the poor and vulnerable. Criminal organizations would not disappear; they would undercut taxed products, sell to minors and move into other crimes, as illicit cannabis markets persist in legal states. Prohibition keeps prices high and use far below that of legal drugs: past-year use of cocaine in the United States, about 4.2 million people in 2025, is a fraction of the 129 million who drank alcohol in the past month (SAMHSA, https://www.samhsa.gov/blog/release-2025-nsduh-using-data-to-drive-great-american-recovery).

Question: Do adults have a right to use drugs if they harm no one else?
Type: value
Poles: Personal liberty / Shared protection
Why contested: Underneath the empirical arguments is a disagreement about the proper reach of the law. Libertarians and many reformers hold that the state has no business punishing what people put in their own bodies; others hold that addiction destroys the autonomy it claims to exercise and spreads harm to families and communities, so society may protect people from it.
Case for “Personal liberty”: In a free society the law punishes people for harming others, not for risks they take with themselves; the state permits drinking, smoking, gambling and dangerous sports. Criminalizing drug use treats millions of adults who use drugs without harming anyone as criminals, and gives the state wide power to search, arrest and imprison. The harms that do come from drug use, from impaired driving to neglect of children, are already crimes in their own right and can be punished as such.
Case for “Shared protection”: Drugs such as fentanyl and methamphetamine do not stay private: addiction leads to lost jobs, broken families, children in foster care, crime to fund use and public disorder, and its costs fall on everyone. People in the grip of addiction are not making free choices, and a law that protects them from starting, or from continuing, respects their future selves. The law already limits personal risks, from seatbelts to prescription-only medicines, and the dangers of these drugs are far greater.

Question: Is drug trafficking best fought as a crime, a public health problem or a security threat?
Type: value
Poles: Security threat / Crime and health
Why contested: The United States has designated cartels as foreign terrorist organizations and since September 2025 has used military force against boats it says carry drugs, killing at least 234 people [Correction 2026-10-05: as of October 4, 2026, at least 235 people had been killed in at least 70 US military strikes since September 2, 2025 (Associated Press via ABC News, latest US strike on alleged drug-smuggling boat, October 4, 2026, https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/us-military-latest-strike-alleged-drug-smuggling-boat-136994059).]; in April 2026 the International Criminal Court sent former Philippine president Rodrigo Duterte to trial over drug-war killings. Supporters of the military approach say traffickers kill tens of thousands of Americans a year; opponents say the strikes are extrajudicial killings and that past militarized campaigns did not reduce supply.
Case for “Security threat”: Cartels are armed organizations that control territory, corrupt governments and kill on a scale that ordinary policing cannot match, and fentanyl trafficking has killed hundreds of thousands of Americans. Treating them as terrorist organizations brings military, intelligence and financial tools to bear, and the administration says the strikes deter traffickers and disrupt shipments. A government’s first duty is to protect its citizens from those attacking them.
Case for “Crime and health”: Drug smuggling is a crime to be prosecuted under law, not an act of war, and killing suspects without trial abandons due process and risks killing fishermen and migrants; the government has offered little public evidence about those killed. Half a century of eradication, interdiction and militarized campaigns in Colombia, Mexico and the Philippines has not reduced supply: cocaine production is at a record and Mexico’s drug war has coincided with soaring homicides. Demand drives supply, so the more durable answer is treatment and prevention at home.

## Positions

Camp: Enforce drug laws and steer people toward recovery
Adherents: The US federal executive under President Trump, whose July 2025 executive order directs that federal substance use grants “do not fund programs that fail to achieve adequate outcomes, including so-called ‘harm reduction’ or ‘safe consumption’ efforts that only facilitate illegal drug use and its attendant harm,” and orders the Attorney General to “prioritize available funding to support the expansion of drug courts” and to promote civil commitment (Executive Order 14321, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/07/29/2025-14391/ending-crime-and-disorder-on-americas-streets); the Government of Alberta, which has built what it calls a “recovery-oriented system of care” with more than 10,000 new publicly funded treatment spaces and the Compassionate Intervention Act allowing court-ordered treatment, and whose premier, Danielle Smith, said “there is no compassion in leaving people to suffer in the throes of addiction” (Alberta Recovery Model, https://open.alberta.ca/publications/alberta-recovery-model; Global News, https://globalnews.ca/news/11133527/alberta-government-mandatory-addiction-treatment); Smart Approaches to Marijuana, the main US group opposing cannabis legalization, whose president Kevin Sabet said after the April 2026 rescheduling that marijuana “should be carefully researched, of course, but not legalized through the backdoor” (NPR, https://www.npr.org/2026/04/23/nx-s1-5797245/marijuana-rules-reschedule-medical-legalization); Charles Fain Lehman of the Manhattan Institute, who has argued that the decriminalization experiments in Oregon, Washington and British Columbia failed and that “we need involuntary treatment to solve the public drug scourge” (https://manhattan.institute/article/eric-adams-is-right-we-need-involuntary-treatment-to-solve-the-public-drug-scourge).
Case: Addiction is not a lifestyle choice to be accommodated; it is a condition people can and do recover from, and the purpose of drug policy should be to get them there. The law against using and selling drugs expresses a shared judgment that these substances destroy lives, keeps them more expensive and less available than they would otherwise be, and gives families, police and courts the leverage to bring someone in crisis into care before it is too late. The experiments of the last decade showed what happens without that leverage: when Oregon and British Columbia stopped treating possession as a crime, almost no one used the treatment hotlines, public drug use spread through downtowns, and overdose deaths rose faster than elsewhere, until both reversed course. Services that hand out supplies and supervise drug use without asking anything of the person keep people alive in the short term but too often keep them using, and money spent on them is money not spent on treatment beds, recovery housing and long-term care. The better model pairs firm enforcement against dealers and public drug use with a large, accessible treatment system, drug courts that offer treatment instead of jail, and, for the few whose addiction has taken away their capacity to choose, court-ordered care. On cannabis, the same logic applies: commercial legalization has produced a high-potency industry that profits from heavy users, and it should not be extended or blessed by the federal government.
Evidence: Studies of Oregon and Washington estimate 182 additional overdose deaths in Oregon in 2021 (Spencer, Journal of Health Economics, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37556870/) and about 1,186 in Oregon and 1,895 in Washington from 2021 to 2023 (Hall, Hansen & Matsuzawa, NBER, 2026, https://www.nber.org/papers/w35427) relative to similar states; British Columbia ended its decriminalization pilot on 31 January 2026 after its health minister said it “did not deliver the results” hoped for (https://globalnews.ca/news/11613636/bc-ends-three-year-drug-decriminalization-pilot-program/); Alberta reports a nearly 40 percent fall in opioid deaths in 2024 compared with 2023 under its recovery model; British Columbia moved to witnessed dosing for prescribed safer supply in February 2025 after reports of diversion; more Americans now use cannabis daily or near daily than drink daily (Caulkins, Addiction, 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38775461/), and the share of schizophrenia cases in Denmark associated with cannabis use disorder has risen three- to fourfold since 1995 (Hjorthøj et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34287621/); US overdose deaths have fallen about 40 percent from their 2023 peak during a period of intensified enforcement against fentanyl trafficking.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that prohibition has failed, since drug use and cocaine production are at record levels, the camp answers that enforcement has not been tried consistently, that use would be far higher if drugs were legal and cheap, as alcohol and tobacco show, and that the problem is not too much law but too little treatment alongside it, which it proposes to fix. To the charge that compulsory treatment violates rights and leaves people at risk when they leave, it answers that leaving people to die on the street is a worse violation, that it reserves compulsion for those who are a danger to themselves or others, with court oversight, and that it pairs release with continuing care, including medication. To the charge that enforcement falls unfairly on Black and poor communities, it answers that those communities suffer most from open drug markets and the violence around them, and that drug courts and diversion keep most people who use drugs out of prison.
Variation: The camp ranges from governments that accept medication treatment and naloxone and reserve compulsion for extreme cases, as Alberta does, to those that reject most harm reduction outright, as the 2025 US executive order does, to a punitive end beyond the democracies, where Singapore, Iran, Saudi Arabia and China execute people for drug trafficking and the Philippines under Rodrigo Duterte waged a campaign of police killings now before the International Criminal Court; most of the camp’s Western adherents reject those practices. On cannabis it divides: Smart Approaches to Marijuana opposes legalization and federal rescheduling, while the Trump administration itself rescheduled medical marijuana in April 2026. On supply, the US administration has gone furthest, designating cartels as terrorist organizations and striking alleged drug boats with military force, a step others in the camp, including some conservatives, regard as unlawful.

Camp: Keep drug possession illegal while funding treatment and harm reduction
Adherents: The Government of British Columbia, which ended its decriminalization pilot on 31 January 2026 while keeping overdose prevention sites and treatment services, its health minister Josie Osborne saying the pilot “did not deliver the results” hoped for and that the province would focus on “strengthening the approaches that are helping people get timely, appropriate care,” since “addiction is a health issue, not a criminal justice issue” (Global News, https://globalnews.ca/news/11613636/bc-ends-three-year-drug-decriminalization-pilot-program/); Oregon Governor Tina Kotek, who signed House Bill 4002 on 1 April 2024, making possession a misdemeanor again from 1 September 2024 while expanding treatment funding and letting counties offer deflection to treatment instead of prosecution, and whose signing letter said “committing to clarity and coordination is one way to improve its likelihood of long-term success” (KVAL, https://kval.com/news/local/governor-kotek-signs-hb-4002-recriminalizing-possession-of-hard-drugs-salem-oregon-portland-multnomah-county-tina-rob-wagner-julie-fahey-jeff-helfrich; OPB, https://www.opb.org/article/2024/04/01/drug-possession-oregon-kotek-sign-bill/); the Government of Canada, which legalized cannabis in 2018 and authorizes supervised consumption sites, but in May 2024 rejected Toronto’s request to decriminalize possession because it “does not adequately protect public health and maintain public safety,” while saying it “remains committed to addressing substance use and addiction as a health issue” (Global News, https://globalnews.ca/news/10504318/toronto-decriminalize-drug-request-rejected/); Keith Humphreys, a Stanford addiction researcher and former White House drug policy adviser, who supports Portugal-style expansion of addiction care but not decriminalization, arguing that for isolated people with severe addiction in the United States, “if there’s no law pressure, there is no pressure at all” (NPR, https://www.npr.org/2024/02/13/1231221308/portugals-approach-to-the-opioid-epidemic-is-a-flashpoint-in-u-s-fentanyl-debate).
Case: The overdose crisis demands every tool that saves lives, and also a legal framework that keeps public order and gives people a reason to accept help. Naloxone, syringe services, drug checking, medication treatment and supervised consumption sites are proven ways to keep people alive long enough to recover, and refusing them on principle costs lives. But removing criminal penalties altogether, as Oregon and British Columbia tried, did not by itself bring people into care, and it lost public support quickly when drug use became visible on the streets. The workable middle is to keep possession of hard drugs illegal while making the criminal law a door to treatment rather than prison: police deflection, drug courts, record sealing for those who complete treatment, and sentences reserved for trafficking and violence. This approach can hold voters’ trust while the health system is built up, which is the real lesson of Portugal, whose success rests on treatment more than on the legal change. Many in this camp accept legal, regulated cannabis, which carries far lower risks of overdose, while keeping harder drugs prohibited.
Evidence: Retention in methadone treatment is associated with about a two-thirds reduction in deaths from all causes among people dependent on opioids (Sordo et al., BMJ, 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28446428/); overdose deaths within 500 meters of Vancouver’s supervised injection site fell 35 percent after it opened, against 9.3 percent in the rest of the city (Marshall et al., The Lancet, 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21497898/); New York City’s two sanctioned sites were not associated with increases in crime or disorder (Chalfin et al., JAMA Network Open, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37955901/); in Portugal, about 90 percent of people referred by police to drug counseling attend at least one session, by government data reported by NPR (https://www.npr.org/2024/02/24/1230188789/portugal-drug-overdose-opioid-treatment); Oregon and British Columbia both reversed decriminalization within about three years; only 16 percent of Americans who needed substance use treatment received it in 2025 (SAMHSA, https://www.samhsa.gov/newsroom/press-announcements/20260727/samhsa-releases-annual-national-survey-on-drug-use-and-health).
Answer to the strongest objection: To decriminalization advocates who say any arrest for possession does harm, the camp answers that its aim is not to punish people for using drugs but to keep a lever that brings them to treatment and keeps public spaces usable, and that deflection and record sealing limit the damage; it points out that the decriminalization experiments it watched did not deliver the treatment uptake their supporters promised. To enforcement advocates who say harm reduction prolongs addiction, it answers that people who die cannot recover, that supervised sites and medication bring people into contact with care, and that the evidence that these services save lives is stronger than the evidence that they increase use. To the charge that it is inconsistent to legalize cannabis while prohibiting other drugs, it answers that policy should follow risk, and the risks of cannabis and fentanyl are not comparable.
Variation: Members differ on how much harm reduction to support: Canada’s federal government and British Columbia support supervised consumption and, until 2025, take-home safer supply, while many US officials in this camp support naloxone and syringe services but oppose supervised sites. They also differ on coercion: Humphreys argues courts and police must push people with severe addiction into treatment, while others favor voluntary deflection. On cannabis, most in the camp, including Canada and Oregon, accept legal adult-use markets, while some prefer decriminalization without commercialization.

Camp: Decriminalize drug use and treat addiction as a health issue
Adherents: The Drug Policy Alliance, the main US organization working to end the drug war, which calls on governments to “end criminal penalties for drug possession” and “shift drug policy from criminal penalties to public health, focusing on treatment, housing, jobs, healthcare, and crisis-response teams” (https://drugpolicy.org/issue/prioritize-health-end-criminal-penalties/); João Goulão, the physician who designed Portugal’s 2001 decriminalization and has led its national drug agency, who says “decriminalizing drug use is a good step,” and that “the more you have other kinds of responses available, the better” (NPR, https://www.npr.org/2024/02/24/1230188789/portugal-drug-overdose-opioid-treatment).
Case: People who use drugs are not criminals but people, some of whom have a health problem, and arresting them makes every part of that problem worse. A criminal record follows a person for life, closing off the jobs, housing and stability that recovery depends on; fear of arrest drives people to use alone, to avoid calling for help when a friend overdoses, and to stay away from health services. Portugal showed the alternative works: since 2001 it has treated possession of small amounts as an administrative matter handled by health-led commissions, invested in treatment, and seen overdose deaths, HIV infections and drug-related imprisonment fall without the surge in use opponents predicted, and police there now refer people to care instead of arresting them. Decriminalization does not legalize selling drugs, which remains a crime, and it is not meant to work alone: it must come with treatment on demand, housing, harm reduction and outreach. Oregon’s difficulties came from removing penalties before that system existed and amid the arrival of fentanyl, not from the principle, and the best-controlled studies find no effect of its law on deaths. Ending arrests for possession would also end the most racially unequal part of American law enforcement.
Evidence: Portugal averages fewer than 80 overdose deaths a year, against nearly 3,000 in New Jersey, which has a similar population, and NPR reports that over 20 years it cut drug deaths by 80 percent and halved HIV and hepatitis cases (https://www.npr.org/2024/02/24/1230188789/portugal-drug-overdose-opioid-treatment); after adjusting for fentanyl’s arrival, Oregon’s decriminalization was not associated with overdose deaths (Zoorob et al., JAMA Network Open, 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39235814/), and an earlier study found no significant change in either Oregon or Washington in the first year (Joshi et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37755815/); US police made an estimated 822,488 drug arrests in 2024, 87.7 percent for possession (FBI data via Drug Policy Facts, https://www.drugpolicyfacts.org/chapter/crime_arrests); Black Americans were 3.64 times as likely as white Americans to be arrested for marijuana possession in 2018 despite similar use (ACLU, https://www.aclu.org/publications/tale-two-countries-racially-targeted-arrests-era-marijuana-reform); 66 percent of US voters in a 2021 poll favored eliminating criminal penalties for possession and reinvesting in treatment (ACLU and Drug Policy Alliance, https://www.aclu.org/press-releases/50th-anniversary-war-drugs-poll-shows-majority-voters-support-ending-criminal).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that Oregon and British Columbia tried decriminalization and abandoned it, the camp answers that both removed penalties during the worst fentanyl surge on record and before building the treatment and outreach system that makes decriminalization work in Portugal, that the leading peer-reviewed studies find no effect of Oregon’s law on deaths, and that the reversals were driven by public reaction to visible street drug use, which is a housing and public-order problem that criminal penalties for possession did not solve before. To the claim that legal pressure is needed to get people into treatment, it answers that coerced treatment has poor results and that Portugal achieves high attendance through voluntary referral.
Variation: Some in the camp also favor legal regulation of at least some drugs and see decriminalization as a first step: the Drug Policy Alliance campaigns for legal cannabis, and the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, who calls decriminalization “a crucial step towards a more humane and effective drug policy,” also backs regulated markets (see the next camp); others, including Portugal’s government, treat it as the end point and keep supply firmly illegal. Members differ on thresholds and on what replaces criminal penalties, from fines and administrative commissions, as in Portugal, to no sanction at all, as Oregon initially tried. Medical bodies have taken partial positions: the American Society of Addiction Medicine supports removing criminal penalties for cannabis possession and has urged federal officials to decouple criminal penalties from personal drug possession [unverified].

Camp: Legally regulate the supply of drugs
Adherents: The Transform Drug Policy Foundation, a UK charity working for “a safer system of legal regulation for all drugs,” under which “each drug (including different preparations) would be regulated based on a detailed assessment of the risks it presents,” an approach it says ensures “supply is safe, while preventing a market free-for-all” (https://transformdrugs.org/drug-policy/models-of-regulation); the Global Commission on Drug Policy, a panel of former heads of state and senior officials from countries including Brazil, Colombia, Mexico, Portugal and Switzerland, whose 2018 report urged governments to “take control of currently illegal drug markets through responsible regulation” (https://www.globalcommissionondrugs.org/reports/regulation-the-responsible-control-of-drugs); Volker Türk, the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, who said in April 2025 that “criminalization and prohibition have failed to reduce drug use and failed to deter drug-related crime” and that “responsible regulation should aim to take control of illegal drug markets, and eliminate profits from illegal trafficking, criminality and violence” (OHCHR, https://www.ohchr.org/en/statements-and-speeches/2025/04/high-commissioner-turk-harm-reduction-essential-drugs-policy).
Case: Prohibition does not make drugs disappear; it hands their production and sale to criminals. Half a century of the war on drugs has delivered record use, record cocaine production, the most potent and adulterated drug supply in history and hundreds of thousands of deaths, while funding cartels that corrupt governments and kill on the scale of wars, and filling prisons with people who could have been treated. The fentanyl crisis is a product of prohibition: smugglers favor the most compact, potent drugs, and people die because they cannot know what they are taking. Decriminalizing users does nothing about any of this, because the supply stays in criminal hands. The answer is not a commercial free-for-all, which the opioid and tobacco industries show is dangerous, but strict legal regulation matched to each drug’s risk: prescription or pharmacy sale for the most dangerous drugs, licensed and age-restricted outlets for others, controls on potency, price and packaging, and no advertising. That is how societies already manage alcohol, tobacco and medicines, and cannabis legalization has shown it can be done without the catastrophes predicted.
Evidence: Cocaine production more than quadrupled from 2014 to about 4,100 tons in 2024 despite enforcement, and 331 million people used drugs in 2024, a record (World Drug Report 2026, https://www.unodc.org/documents/data-and-analysis/WDR_2026/WDR26_Highlights.pdf); fentanyls were implicated in nearly 70 percent of US stimulant overdose deaths in 2024, showing how adulteration kills people who did not seek opioids (same source); legal status correlates poorly with harm, with alcohol rated the most harmful drug overall in a UK expert assessment (Nutt et al., The Lancet, 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21036393/); recreational cannabis legalization was associated with a small decrease in teen use (Anderson et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31282944/); about 5.9 million people a year come into contact with police for drug offenses worldwide, two thirds of them for use or possession (World Drug Report 2026); Amnesty International recorded 1,257 executions for drug offenses in 2025 (https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/05/death-penalty-in-2025-facts-and-figures/).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that legal availability would multiply addiction, as with alcohol, tobacco and OxyContin, the camp answers that those are examples of commercial promotion and lax regulation, not of the strict, non-commercial models it proposes, and that the alternative is not a world without these drugs but one where they are sold by criminals without any controls. To the objection that no country has tried it for hard drugs, it answers that heroin-assisted treatment in Switzerland, the Netherlands and elsewhere has shown that regulated supply to people with addiction reduces crime and deaths, and that cannabis regulation shows the legal machinery can work. To the claim that illicit markets persist after legalization, it answers that this reflects taxes and rules set too high, which regulation can adjust.
Variation: Proponents differ on how far and how fast: Transform and the Global Commission favor tightly controlled, often non-commercial models designed drug by drug, while libertarians such as the Cato Institute’s Jeffrey Miron argue for treating all drugs like alcohol, with an ordinary taxed market (Cato, https://www.cato.org/commentary/economic-moral-case-legalizing-cocaine-heroin). Some see regulated cannabis and psychedelics as near-term steps and stimulants and opioids as long-term goals; others want medically supplied opioids now as an answer to fentanyl. Many also call for revising the three UN drug control conventions, which require parties to limit drugs to medical and scientific use and which Canada and Uruguay are in tension with.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others is whether using the criminal law against drugs, against the people who use them and against the trade that supplies them, prevents more harm than it causes. Every camp wants fewer deaths, less addiction and less crime; they divide over whether the law’s deterrence and leverage outweigh the arrests, records, prisons, violent black markets and adulterated supply that come with it, and over what goal care for people who use drugs should serve.

The part evidence could settle: how much legal status itself, separate from fentanyl’s spread, treatment funding, housing and policing, changes the number of people who use drugs, become addicted, die of overdoses and commit or suffer crime. The natural experiments so far, Portugal since 2001, Oregon and Washington from 2021, British Columbia from 2023, US cannabis legalization since 2012, and the medically supplied heroin and safer-supply programs of Switzerland and Canada, have produced studies that reach opposite conclusions depending on how they separate the law from everything that changed with it. Better data on what happens after a change in the law, over a longer period and in places where the treatment system was already in place, would move many people in every camp; so would evidence on whether legal pressure actually brings more people into lasting recovery than voluntary outreach does, and on how much use would rise if harder drugs were sold legally under strict rules.

The part no data can close: how to weigh an adult’s freedom to choose what to put in their own body against society’s interest in protecting people, including from their own addiction; whether the aim of policy toward someone who uses drugs is to keep them alive and healthy while they use or to bring them to stop; how much public order, safe streets and the visibility of drug use should count against the costs of arrest and imprisonment; and how much risk of more people using drugs a society should accept in exchange for fewer people being punished, or fewer people being killed in drug markets at home and abroad.
