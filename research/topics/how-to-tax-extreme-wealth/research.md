# Taxing Extreme Wealth — research

## Overview

This topic covers whether and how governments should tax the net worth of the very richest people, billionaires and the few hundred thousand households just below them, rather than taxing only the income they report when they sell assets, take dividends or draw a salary. The instruments on the table are an annual tax on net worth (the classic wealth tax), a one-time levy on fortunes, a minimum tax set as a share of wealth, a tax on unrealized capital gains each year (“mark-to-market”), ending the rule that wipes out capital gains at death, and heavier taxes on inheritances. Underneath them sits one fact that drives the whole debate: most of a large fortune is the rising value of shares in a company the owner built or bought, and that rise is not income for tax purposes until the shares are sold, which the very rich can put off indefinitely by borrowing against them and passing them on at death. The topic deliberately excludes the wider question of how much inequality matters and how to reduce it (covered on the economic inequality page), the general level of top income tax rates and corporate taxes except where they bear on billionaire fortunes, wages and labor bargaining power, and reparations. Government debt appears only as one motive for new revenue.

Why now: on 3 November 2026, Californians vote on Proposition 40, the “One-Time Wealth Tax for State-Funded Health Care Programs Initiative,” which would levy a one-time state tax of 5 percent of net worth on billionaires who were California residents on 1 January 2026, payable in 2027 or over five years with a deferral charge, with 90 percent of the money going to health programs such as Medi-Cal. It qualified for the ballot on 17 June 2026 with 980,438 valid signatures (Ballotpedia News, https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/06/18/california-billionaires-tax-qualifies-for-the-november-ballot-competing-measures-pending-signature-verification/). Its sponsor, the union SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West, puts the revenue at about $100 billion; the state’s nonpartisan Legislative Analyst’s Office says the state would likely collect “tens of billions of dollars” over several years, with a possible ongoing loss of less than $1 billion a year in income tax if billionaires leave or change their behavior, and that the amount is “very hard to predict” (LAO, https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026). A poll of likely voters taken 21 to 24 September 2026 found 48 percent yes, 38 percent no and 14 percent undecided (NBC News, https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/california-poll-voters-back-billionaire-tax-proposal-falls-short-major-rcna600354). The measure has split Democrats: Senator Bernie Sanders, Representative Ro Khanna and the California Democratic Party back it, while Governor Gavin Newsom opposes it and on 26 June 2026 proposed a national minimum tax on everyone worth more than $100 million instead, arguing that “wealth is movable, and it shops for the state with the lowest tax” (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/newsom-urges-a-national-billionaires-tax-while-fighting-one-in-california). Google co-founder Sergey Brin, reported to have moved his residence out of the state before the 1 January cutoff, had given $82 million to the opposition by mid-June (CalMatters, https://calmatters.org/politics/2026/06/california-unions-billionaire-tax-ballot/).

The debate is live well beyond California. In March 2026 Senator Elizabeth Warren and more than 45 lawmakers reintroduced a federal annual wealth tax of 2 percent above $50 million and 3 percent above $1 billion. In France the National Assembly rejected the “Zucman tax,” a 2 percent minimum tax on fortunes over €100 million, by 228 votes to 172 on 31 October 2025, and in Switzerland 78.3 percent of voters rejected a 50 percent federal tax on inheritances over CHF 50 million on 30 November 2025. The G20 committed in November 2024 to “engage cooperatively to ensure that ultra-high-net-worth individuals are effectively taxed,” following a blueprint by the economist Gabriel Zucman for a global 2 percent minimum tax on billionaires. Meanwhile the number of billionaires and their combined wealth reached records in 2026 on the back of an AI-driven stock boom. The participants are economists who have built the case and the evidence on both sides (Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman at Berkeley; Lawrence Summers and Natasha Sarin; the Tax Foundation and the Cato Institute), anti-poverty and labor organizations such as Oxfam and SEIU, progressive lawmakers, governments that run or repealed wealth taxes (Norway, Spain, Switzerland, France, Sweden), centrist Democrats who prefer reaching the same fortunes through the income and estate taxes, and the billionaires themselves, some of whom have moved residence in response.

Data year: 2018 to 2020 averages
Source label: Balkir, Saez, Yagan & Zucman, “How Much Tax Do US Billionaires Pay? Evidence from Administrative Data,” NBER Working Paper 34170 (August 2025)
Source URL: https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/BSYZ2025NBER.pdf
Note: On one leading estimate the 400 wealthiest Americans paid about 24 percent of their economic income in all taxes in 2018 to 2020, less than the 30 percent average for the whole population; a rival adjustment puts the top 400 at about 38 percent against 25 percent overall.
Context: This is the central measurement dispute. Akcan Balkir, Emmanuel Saez, Danny Yagan and Gabriel Zucman matched the Forbes 400 to IRS individual, business, estate and gift tax returns and counted every tax, including corporate tax on the companies they own, which supplies about 9 of the top 400’s 24 points. Their denominator, “economic income,” is national-accounts income including companies’ retained profits but excluding unrealized gains in share prices. They find the top-400 rate fell from about 30 percent in 2010 to 2017 to about 24 percent after the 2017 tax law, and that the gap arises because the companies the very rich own pay out little in dividends and their partnerships report losses. David Splinter, an economist at Congress’s Joint Committee on Taxation writing in a personal capacity, replied in August 2025 (https://www.davidsplinter.com/BillionaireTaxRate.pdf) that the estimate double-counts capital income, treats a Forbes family as if it were a single tax return, and omits state income taxes; with his adjustments the top 400 pay about 38 percent and the population 25 percent (after counting refundable credits as transfers), with a plausible range of 9 to 19 points above the average, and about 59 percent once annual charitable giving is added. Both papers agree the tax system is built to tax income rather than wealth, which is why rates at the very top look lower when people are ranked by wealth.

Value: 0.3
Unit: percent of wealth per year, all individual taxes
Viz: bar
Data year: estimates for the period up to 2024
Source label: Gabriel Zucman, “A blueprint for a coordinated minimum effective taxation standard for ultra-high-net-worth individuals,” report commissioned by Brazil’s G20 presidency (June 2024)
Source URL: https://taxobservatory.world//www-site/uploads/2024/06/report-g20.pdf
Note: Zucman estimates that billionaires worldwide pay individual taxes equal to about 0.3 percent of their wealth each year, while their wealth has earned an average return of about 7.5 percent a year above inflation over four decades.
Context: The figure expresses taxes paid as a share of the stock of wealth, the natural yardstick for a wealth tax, rather than as a share of income. Zucman’s proposal, which the G20 discussed under Brazil’s presidency, is a coordinated minimum: billionaires would owe at least 2 percent of their wealth each year in individual taxes, with income taxes already paid counted toward it. He estimates this would raise $200 to $250 billion a year from about 3,000 people, and $100 to $140 billion more if extended to people worth over $100 million, and that it would cut billionaires’ after-tax return from about 7.2 percent to 5.5 percent rather than shrink their fortunes. The 0.3 percent figure counts only individual taxes; corporate taxes paid by billionaires’ companies are excluded, which is one reason it is far lower than estimates such as the 24 percent above, which measure taxes against income and include corporate tax. The G20 leaders’ declaration of November 2024 committed members to “engage cooperatively to ensure that ultra-high-net-worth individuals are effectively taxed,” “with full respect to tax sovereignty,” but adopted no rate (G20, https://www.g20.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/G20-Rio-de-Janeiro-Leaders-Declaration-EN.pdf).

Source label: Saez, Yagan & Zucman, “Capital Gains Withholding” (2021), Table 1

Note: For American households worth more than $100 million, about 54 percent of their wealth in 2020 was gains in asset values that had never been taxed because the assets had not been sold.
Context: This is why the debate exists. Under the income tax, a gain in the value of shares is taxed only when the shares are sold, and if the owner holds them until death the heirs inherit them at their current value, so the gain is never taxed by the income tax at all (the “step-up in basis”). Wealthy owners can meanwhile borrow against their shares to fund spending, a strategy known as “buy, borrow, die.” The same table shows the gains are mainly business ownership: for the over-$100 million group, 64 percent of unrealized gains were in private business shares, 26 percent in publicly traded stock and 10 percent in real estate, which bears on how hard they would be to value each year. The authors note they assumed half of Forbes billionaires’ wealth is unrealized gains. Estate tax data for 2010 reported by the Treasury show a similar pattern: 44 percent of estates over $20 million consisted of unrealized gains. Zucman estimated in 2024 that the share for Americans worth over $100 million had risen to about 60 percent after the post-pandemic stock rally.

Viz: ranked

Data year: 1990 and 2017
Source label: OECD, “The Role and Design of Net Wealth Taxes in the OECD,” Tax Policy Studies No. 26 (2018)
Source URL: https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/04/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_g1g89919/9789264290303-en.pdf
Note: Twelve OECD countries taxed individuals’ net wealth each year in 1990; by 2017 only four did: France, Norway, Spain and Switzerland.
Context: Austria repealed its wealth tax in 1994, Denmark and Germany in 1997, the Netherlands in 2001, Finland, Iceland and Luxembourg in 2006 and Sweden in 2007; Spain cut its tax to zero with a full credit in 2008 and reinstated it after the financial crisis. The OECD reports that repeals were usually justified by efficiency and administrative concerns, low revenue and failure to meet redistributive goals. France replaced its wealth tax with a tax on real estate wealth only in 2018, leaving Norway, Spain and Switzerland as the European countries with a full annual net wealth tax; Spain added a national “solidarity” tax on fortunes over €3 million in 2022. Those who favor wealth taxes argue that the repealed taxes failed because they had low thresholds, many exemptions and weak enforcement, and that a tax on only the very richest with third-party reporting is a different instrument; those who oppose them read the repeals as the verdict of experience. The OECD’s own conclusion is conditional: it finds “limited arguments” for a net wealth tax where capital income and inheritances are already taxed well, and a stronger case where they are not.

Value: 2
Unit: percent decrease in the stock of wealthy taxpayers for a 1 percentage point increase in the top wealth tax rate
Viz: bar
Data year: Swedish and Danish administrative data on three wealth tax reforms, Denmark’s 1989 cut and 1996 abolition and Sweden’s repeal announced in 2006
Source label: Jakobsen, Kleven, Kolsrud, Landais & Muñoz, “Taxing Top Wealth: Migration Responses and their Aggregate Economic Implications” (working paper, November 2025)
Source URL: https://www.henrikkleven.com/research/working_papers/WealthMig_Nov2025.pdf
Note: Using Scandinavian tax records, the authors find that raising the top wealth tax rate by 1 percentage point reduces the number of wealthy taxpayers in the country by about 2 percent, and that the resulting departures cut employment by 0.02 percent, investment by 0.07 percent and value added by 0.10 percent.
Context: This is the most direct recent evidence on the claim that wealth taxes drive the rich abroad. The authors use the unexpected repeal of Sweden’s wealth tax in 2006 and two Danish reforms, and find clear responses: the repeal cut the propensity of wealthy Swedes to emigrate by 30 percent. Because the wealthy own many businesses (in Sweden the top 2 percent by wealth control close to 10 percent of employment through private firms), their departures do hurt the firms they own, but the aggregate effect is small. Both camps cite the result: supporters say it shows flight is real but too small to defeat the tax, opponents that even modest rates lose taxpayers. Recent episodes are less well measured: AFP reported that more than 100 of Norway’s wealthiest people moved abroad, mostly to Switzerland, between 2021 and 2023 after Norway raised its rates to 1 and 1.1 percent and tightened its dividend tax (France 24, https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20240524-norway-struggles-to-keep-ultra-rich-tempted-by-exile), and in California Fortune reported six billionaires with about $27 billion in taxable wealth relocating before Proposition 40’s residency date (as summarized by CalMatters). A paper is a working paper until published.

Source label: Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf & Schmidheiny, “Behavioral Responses to Wealth Taxes: Evidence from Switzerland,” American Economic Journal: Economic Policy 14(4), November 2022
Source URL: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpol.20200258
Note: The authors find that a 1 percentage point drop in a Swiss canton’s wealth tax rate raises reported taxable wealth by at least 43 percent after six years.
Context: The response is large, but its sources matter. The authors attribute roughly a quarter of it to taxpayers moving between cantons and about a fifth to house prices capitalizing the tax change; much of the remainder appears to be evasion or avoidance, which is easier in Switzerland because financial wealth is not systematically reported to tax authorities by third parties. A 1 percentage point change is large relative to Swiss rates, which are typically well below 1 percent. Opponents of wealth taxes cite the finding as evidence that the tax base shrinks sharply when taxed. Proponents, including Saez and Zucman, argue it shows the cost of weak enforcement rather than of wealth taxation as such, and point to Danish evidence, where wealth is third-party reported, of much smaller responses (Jakobsen, Jakobsen, Kleven & Zucman, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2020, https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/JJKZ2020.pdf). The Saez and Zucman score of the 2026 Warren bill assumes an avoidance and evasion response of 15 percent.

Balkir, Saez, Yagan and Zucman find that the estates of Forbes 400 members who died paid federal estate tax equal to 0.8 percent of their wealth when married and 7 percent when single, and that estate values reported to the IRS were on average about 30 percent of Forbes’s estimates. Those who favor taxing fortunes at transfer argue this is the right instrument to repair; those who oppose the estate tax argue it falls on savings already taxed once and should be abolished. A mirror of the CRS report is at https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48183.html.

## Questions

Question: Do the richest people pay a lower share of their income in tax than ordinary people do?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Lower than average / Higher than average
Why contested: The answer depends almost entirely on what counts as income and how families are matched to tax returns. Counting unrealized gains, the 400 wealthiest US families paid a federal income tax rate of about 8 percent (White House economists, 2021); counting all taxes against national-accounts income, about 24 percent against 30 percent for everyone (Balkir, Saez, Yagan and Zucman, 2025); with different adjustments, about 38 percent against 25 percent (Splinter, 2025). Each figure is defensible on its own definitions, and the choice of definition is itself part of the argument.
Case for lower than average: The yardstick should be how much richer a person actually became in a year, because that is what a wage earner is taxed on. By that measure the very richest are taxed lightly: most of their gain is the rising value of shares they never sell, their companies pay out little in dividends, their partnerships report tax losses while earning book profits, and the gains disappear for income tax purposes at death. Matched IRS records show the top 400’s total tax rate fell to about 24 percent after 2017, below the 30 percent average and far below the 45 percent paid by top wage earners. A system in which a nurse pays a higher rate than a billionaire fails the most basic test of fairness, whatever its formal progressivity.
Case for higher than average: The tax system taxes income, and by that standard the rich pay far more than anyone else: the top 1 percent of taxpayers received about 21 percent of reported income in 2023 and paid about 38 percent of federal income taxes. Estimates showing low rates at the very top rely on contestable choices: treating a whole Forbes dynasty as one taxpayer, counting the same capital income twice, leaving out state taxes, and treating paper gains that can vanish in a market fall as if they were cash. Correcting these, David Splinter finds the top 400 pay well above the average rate, and much more once their giving to charity, which is concentrated at the end of life, is counted. Unrealized gains are taxed eventually when assets are sold, and the corporate tax already falls on the profits that drive share prices.

Question: Would an annual wealth tax on the very richest raise large sums once avoidance, valuation and departures are counted?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Large net revenue / Little net revenue
Why contested: Estimates for similar US designs span a wide range. Saez and Zucman score the 2026 Warren bill at $6.17 trillion over ten years assuming 15 percent avoidance; the Penn Wharton Budget Model put the 2019 version at $2.3 to $2.7 trillion, with a range of $1.4 to $4.8 trillion depending on avoidance; Sarin and Summers suggested about half of the 2019 campaign’s figure. Europe’s experience, where most wealth taxes raised well under half a percent of GDP and were repealed, is read both as a warning and as a lesson in bad design.
Case for large net revenue: The old European taxes failed for reasons that can be fixed: they started at modest wealth levels, exempted business assets and other favored holdings, relied on self-reporting of financial wealth, and let taxpayers move to a neighboring country with no exit charge. A tax that starts at $50 million, has no exemptions, uses third-party reporting by banks and brokers, values private companies with formulas and audits, and taxes renunciation of citizenship heavily would face a narrower set of escape routes. Where wealth is third-party reported, as in Denmark, measured responses are small, and the best evidence on migration finds a 1 point higher rate reduces the number of wealthy taxpayers by about 2 percent. With US billionaire wealth in the trillions, even after generous allowance for avoidance the revenue would be among the largest available from any single reform.
Case for little net revenue: Every revenue estimate assumes the tax base can be measured, and for the largest fortunes it cannot: most is in private companies, founder stakes with voting control, partnerships, art and trusts that have no market price. The estate tax, which already taxes the same people once a generation, raises far less than its base would suggest because of valuation discounts, trusts and planning, and estate values reported to the IRS average about 30 percent of Forbes estimates. Swiss data show reported wealth rising by at least 43 percent after six years when a canton cuts its wealth tax rate by 1 point, and Norway lost dozens of its richest residents after raising its rate. Wealth taxes also shrink the income and capital gains tax base they sit on top of, so net revenue is smaller than gross.

Question: Would taxing great fortunes each year slow investment, entrepreneurship and growth?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Little economic harm / Real economic harm
Why contested: A wealth tax falls on the stock of capital, much of it the founders’ stakes in operating companies, so it could plausibly change how much they save, invest and build. Direct evidence on growth is scarce because few countries have taxed billionaire-scale fortunes, and the effects that are measured, on reported wealth, migration and saving, mix real changes with relabeling.
Case for little economic harm: Billionaire fortunes are mostly the accumulated return on companies already built, and taxing a small slice of them each year does not change the reward for building a new one much: a founder whose company succeeds still becomes enormously rich. The Scandinavian migration study finds that even the departures a wealth tax causes reduce employment by 0.02 percent and value added by 0.10 percent for a 1 point higher rate. Money raised can fund public investment in education, research and health that does more for growth than the marginal consumption or speculation of the very richest, and concentrated fortunes can themselves suppress competition and innovation by entrenching incumbents.
Case for real economic harm: Wealth is savings, and savings fund investment. A 2 or 3 percent tax on net worth, levied whether or not the assets earn anything, can be a tax of 30 to 60 percent or more on the normal return to capital, and over 100 percent in years of losses. It falls hardest on founders whose wealth is tied up in growing companies, who would have to sell shares or borrow to pay, diluting their control and channeling capital away from the riskiest and most productive ventures. The Penn Wharton Budget Model projected that the 2019 Warren plan would reduce long-run GDP. Countries that repealed wealth taxes did so in part because they found these costs outweighed the revenue.

Question: Is a rise in the value of assets that have not been sold income that the state may fairly tax?
Tag: value
Poles: Gains are income / Only realized gains
Why contested: Economists’ standard definition of income, consumption plus change in net worth, counts unrealized gains, and the very rich can enjoy those gains by borrowing against them. But tax systems everywhere have generally waited until a sale, because paper gains can reverse, may not be convertible to cash and are hard to value. Which conception is right is a judgment about what taxation is for and what a person owes on value they have not converted to money.
Case for gains are income: A billionaire whose shares rise by $10 billion is $10 billion richer and can spend against that wealth at once by borrowing, which is exactly what “buy, borrow, die” exploits. Treating that gain as untaxable until a sale the owner can postpone forever, and then erasing it at death, lets the people with the most economic power choose whether they pay. The income tax already taxes some unrealized gains, such as those on futures contracts and some foreign holdings, and the Supreme Court upheld one form of tax on undistributed income in 2024. Taxing gains as they accrue, or at least at death, would simply apply the income tax to the rich as it applies to wage earners.
Case for only realized gains: Income is what a person actually receives. Share prices rise and fall, and taxing a gain that may vanish next year forces people to sell or borrow to pay tax on money they never had, a burden that falls hardest on founders and family businesses with little cash. A tax on net worth taxes savings that were built from income already taxed once, and repeats the charge every year. The realization rule is not a loophole but a recognition that the government should tax transactions it can measure, not appraisals it must guess at. If the problem is “buy, borrow, die,” it can be addressed by targeting borrowing or the step-up at death without abandoning the principle.

Question: Is the concentration of wealth at the very top a harm in itself, apart from what taxing it would raise?
Tag: value
Poles: Concentration is harmful / Earned wealth is legitimate
Why contested: Much of the case for wealth taxes is not about revenue at all but about power: the belief that fortunes of hundreds of billions of dollars buy political influence, control of media and platforms, and dynastic advantage incompatible with democratic equality. Those who reject this view hold that a fortune honestly earned by creating something people value is the owner’s, and that the political problem, where real, should be addressed directly.
Case for concentration is harmful: When a single person’s wealth rivals a country’s economy, it converts into power that no election can check: funding campaigns and ballot measures, owning news outlets and social networks, and shaping which laws pass and which never come up. The richest 0.1 percent of Americans now hold 15 percent of household wealth, nearly double their share in 1989, and the compounding of high returns on large fortunes means concentration will keep rising unless something slows it. A wealth tax is valuable precisely because it slows that compounding; revenue is a secondary benefit. Some in this view, such as the philosopher Ingrid Robeyns, go further and argue that no one should hold more than a set limit.
Case for earned wealth is legitimate: The largest fortunes today were mostly built by founders of companies that millions of people chose to buy from, and the wealth is the market value of those companies, not cash hoarded away from others. Taking a share of it every year treats success as an offense and the state as the rightful owner of whatever it chooses to levy. Political influence is a real concern, but it is better met by campaign finance and disclosure rules than by a tax on everything a person owns. A society that welcomes people to build great companies should not punish them for having done so.

Question: Can a single state or country tax its billionaires on its own, or does it take international coordination?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Can act alone / Needs coordination
Why contested: Billionaires are mobile and their assets more so. California’s measure uses a fixed residency date to defeat departures, the Warren bill a 40 percent exit tax on renouncing citizenship, and the Zucman blueprint a coordinated global minimum so that there is nowhere to move. Opponents point to billionaires already leaving California and Norway; supporters point to the small measured migration responses and to the United States’ citizenship-based taxation.
Case for can act alone: Countries already tax their citizens and residents on worldwide income and can tax wealth the same way. The United States taxes citizens wherever they live, so a federal wealth tax could be escaped only by renouncing citizenship, which an exit tax can make expensive. Fixing the residency date in the past, as California’s measure does, means no one can avoid a one-time levy by moving after the fact. Scandinavian evidence shows the stock of wealthy taxpayers falls only about 2 percent for each point of tax, too little to defeat the tax. Waiting for global agreement is a recipe for never acting, and the G20’s 2024 commitment shows that coordination follows countries willing to move first.
Case for needs coordination: Within a federation, where people can move freely, a state wealth tax mainly drives the tax base elsewhere; California has already seen billionaires leave before its residency cutoff and could lose income tax revenue for years. Even national taxes face competition from low-tax jurisdictions, as Norway found when its wealthiest moved to Switzerland. Governor Newsom’s argument is that “wealth is movable, and it shops for the state with the lowest tax,” which is why he favors a national rather than a state measure, and the case for a global minimum standard rests on the same logic. Acting alone risks losing both the new tax and the existing taxes the departing residents used to pay.

Question: Should great fortunes be taxed each year while they are held, or when they pass to heirs?
Tag: value
Poles: Tax while held / Tax at transfer
Why contested: Both approaches aim at the same accumulated wealth, and both face valuation and avoidance problems. The choice turns on what the tax is for (limiting concentrated power during a lifetime, or limiting inherited advantage), and on whether a single large charge or a small repeated one is fairer and more workable. The OECD’s 2018 review favored taxing capital income and inheritances well over adding an annual net wealth tax, while advocates of annual taxes note that inheritance taxes have proven easy to plan around.
Case for tax while held: The problem with extreme fortunes is not only that they are passed on but that they compound and exercise power during the founder’s lifetime, which may last decades. An inheritance tax touches a fortune once a generation and has proved easy to avoid with trusts, discounts and lifetime planning; the Forbes 400 paid estate tax equal to 0.8 percent of their wealth when married. A modest annual tax spreads the burden, raises steady revenue, and slows concentration as it happens.
Case for tax at transfer: Inheritance is where the case for taxing wealth is strongest: heirs did not earn what they receive, and taxing it supports equal opportunity without penalizing anyone for building or saving. A tax at death requires valuing an estate once, not every asset every year, and falls when a transfer creates liquidity. Ending the step-up in basis, so unrealized gains are taxed at death, would reach the same untaxed growth that motivates wealth taxes. The OECD and many economists who doubt annual wealth taxes favor strengthening inheritance and capital gains taxes for this reason.

Question: Would a federal annual wealth tax survive the US Constitution?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Constitutionally permissible / Unconstitutional without apportionment
Why contested: The Constitution requires “direct” taxes to be apportioned among the states by population, which would make a wealth tax unworkable, and the Sixteenth Amendment exempts only taxes on “incomes.” Whether a tax on net worth is a direct tax has never been decided, and in Moore v. United States (June 2024) the Supreme Court expressly declined to address taxes “on holdings, wealth, or net worth” or on unrealized appreciation, noting that the government had itself indicated that a tax on wealth or net worth “might be considered a tax on property, not income.”
Case for constitutionally permissible: Legal scholars who support a wealth tax argue that the Framers understood “direct taxes” narrowly, as head taxes and taxes on land, and that the Supreme Court’s 1895 decision striking down the income tax (Pollock) is a discredited outlier that the Sixteenth Amendment repudiated. Congress has broad taxing power, and the Court has long avoided reading the apportionment clause to block new forms of taxation. Mark-to-market taxation of gains is on even firmer ground, since it taxes economic gain over a period, as Congress already does for some financial contracts.
Case for unconstitutional without apportionment: A tax on owning property is the clearest example of a direct tax, which is exactly what the apportionment rule was written to restrain, and Pollock’s holding that taxes on property and its income are direct taxes has never been overruled for wealth itself. In Moore four justices, Barrett and Alito concurring in the judgment and Thomas and Gorsuch in dissent, wrote that the Court’s precedent requires gains to be realized before they can be taxed without apportionment, so a tax on unrealized gains could also fail (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-800_jg6o.pdf). Passing a wealth tax would invite years of litigation over a tax whose revenue might have to be refunded.

## Positions

Order note: the debate has three camps. The first wants to tax net worth itself and is the camp that public surveys favor and that the current ballot fights are about. The second, which opposes new taxes on wealth and would move the tax system toward taxing consumption, is the main opposing pole and governs US federal tax policy today. The third is the hybrid: it agrees with the first that the very richest are under-taxed and with the second that an annual net worth tax is the wrong instrument, and it includes Governor Newsom, the Biden administration and prominent centrist economists; its weight is large and growing, and it is placed third only because it is defined by reference to the other two. The limitarian view, that fortunes above some level should not exist, is a wing of the first camp rather than a camp of its own, since its instrument is the same steeply progressive wealth tax. The rejecting position, that the question of how to tax extreme wealth is misframed because wealth is savings that should not be taxed at all, is the second camp’s own view rather than a separate one.

Camp: Tax billionaires’ net worth directly
Adherents: Oxfam International, https://www.oxfam.org/en/press-releases/billionaire-wealth-jumps-three-times-faster-2025-highest-peak-ever-sparking (19 January 2026, calling for “effectively taxing the super-rich to reduce their power, including with broad-base taxes on income and wealth at high enough rates to reduce massive levels of inequality”); Senator Elizabeth Warren, lead sponsor of the Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act of 2026, a 2 percent annual tax on net worth above $50 million and 3 percent above $1 billion, reintroduced with more than 45 lawmakers, https://jayapal.house.gov/2026/03/26/jayapal-warren-boyle-45-lawmakers-renew-push-for-wealth-tax-on-ultra-millionaires-and-billionaires/ (26 March 2026). Also: economists Gabriel Zucman and Emmanuel Saez; SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West, sponsor of California’s Proposition 40; Senator Bernie Sanders; Patriotic Millionaires and Americans for Tax Fairness; the government of Brazil, which put a global billionaire minimum tax on the G20 agenda.
Case: The very richest people in the world pay less of their income in tax than nurses and teachers do, and the reason is structural: their wealth grows as unrealized gains in companies they own, which the income tax does not touch until a sale that never has to happen. Billionaires worldwide pay individual taxes equal to about 0.3 percent of their wealth each year while that wealth earns about 7.5 percent above inflation, so their fortunes compound faster than the economy and their share keeps rising. Taxing the stock of wealth directly is the only instrument that reaches what they actually have, whatever form it takes and whether or not they ever sell. A small annual tax, 2 or 3 percent on fortunes above $50 million, would leave every billionaire a billionaire, raise hundreds of billions a year for health care, child care and education, and slow the concentration of economic and political power in a few hundred families. Most people, across parties, agree: a large majority of Americans favor exactly this design when told what it would raise.
Best evidence: Matched IRS and Forbes data show the top 400 Americans paid 24 percent of their economic income in all taxes in 2018 to 2020, against 30 percent for the population as a whole (Balkir, Saez, Yagan and Zucman, 2025). Unrealized gains make up about 54 percent of the wealth of Americans worth over $100 million (Saez, Yagan and Zucman, 2021). The richest 0.1 percent’s share of US household wealth has risen from 8.6 to 15.0 percent since 1989 (Federal Reserve). The best evidence on migration finds a 1 point higher wealth tax rate reduces the number of wealthy taxpayers by about 2 percent, with small effects on employment and output (Jakobsen, Kleven, Kolsrud, Landais and Muñoz, 2025). A national survey in February 2026 found 80 percent support, including 72 percent of Republicans (Program for Public Consultation).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a wealth tax cannot be enforced: assets will be undervalued, hidden or moved, and Europe’s wealth taxes were repealed because they raised little. The camp’s answer is that Europe’s taxes failed because of their design, not their principle: they started at modest wealth levels, were riddled with exemptions for business and other favored assets, relied on self-reporting of financial holdings, and let taxpayers move next door at no cost. A modern wealth tax would start at $50 million, exempt nothing, rely on third-party reporting by financial institutions, value hard-to-price assets with formulas, retrospective adjustment and audits, give illiquid founders the option to pay in shares or over time, and impose an exit tax on renouncing citizenship. Where wealth is third-party reported, as in Denmark, measured avoidance is small, and the estimate for the 2026 Warren bill already assumes that avoidance and evasion cut liabilities by 15 percent.
Variation inside the camp: Annual taxes (Warren, Sanders) differ from one-time levies (California’s Proposition 40, the UK Wealth Tax Commission’s 2020 recommendation), which fix the valuation date in the past so behavior cannot respond, and from minimum taxes that credit income tax already paid (the Zucman blueprint, France’s rejected “Zucman tax”). National proposals differ from the global minimum standard Brazil championed at the G20, which aims to remove the incentive to move. Motives differ too: some emphasize revenue for public services, others the power that comes with concentrated wealth, and a limitarian wing, including the philosopher Ingrid Robeyns, argues that fortunes above a set level should not exist at all; Sanders’s 2019 campaign plan, with rates rising to 8 percent on fortunes above $10 billion [unverified], was designed to shrink the largest fortunes over time.

Camp: Tax income and spending, not wealth
Adherents: Cato Institute, Adam N. Michel and Chris Edwards, “Failures of Wealth Taxation,” Policy Analysis No. 1021, https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/failures-wealth-taxation (23 July 2026, arguing that wealth taxes “raise little revenue while adding complexity, driving capital flight, and slowing growth,” that wealth in the United States “overwhelmingly consists of productive business capital,” and that a better way to tax the rich is to shift the federal tax system toward a consumption base); Jared Walczak, Tax Foundation, https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/state/california-wealth-tax-billionaires-proposal/ (14 January 2026, writing that “any wealth tax imposes significant economic costs” and calling California’s proposal “economically destructive”). Also: the campaign against Proposition 40, backed by the California Business Roundtable and funded largely by Sergey Brin; most Republicans in Congress, who in 2025 raised the estate tax exemption to $15 million and made it permanent; the French center and right parties that rejected the Zucman tax.
Case: Wealth is not money sitting idle; it is the factories, software, patents and companies that produce the goods people buy and employ the workers who make them. Taxing it every year, whether or not it earns anything, is a tax on saving and investment, and its cost falls ultimately on workers through lower wages and fewer jobs. A 2 percent tax on net worth is equivalent to a tax of 40 percent on a 5 percent return, layered on top of the income, corporate and estate taxes that already apply, and in a bad year it confiscates capital outright. The very rich already pay most of the income tax: the top 1 percent of taxpayers paid 38 percent of all federal income tax in 2023 while receiving 21 percent of reported income. Experience is clear: twelve OECD countries had wealth taxes in 1990 and most repealed them because they raised little, drove out capital and entrepreneurs, and filled with loopholes. If the concern is that the rich live well on borrowed money, the right answer is to tax what people consume, which taxes billionaires fully when they spend, without punishing them for building and holding productive businesses.
Best evidence: The number of OECD countries with annual net wealth taxes fell from 12 in 1990 to 4 in 2017, and the remaining European wealth taxes outside Switzerland raise well under 1 percent of GDP (OECD). In Switzerland a 1 point cut in a canton’s wealth tax rate raises reported wealth by at least 43 percent within six years, much of it apparently from evasion (Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf and Schmidheiny, 2022). Norway saw more than 100 of its wealthiest residents move abroad between 2021 and 2023 after raising its wealth tax (AFP). Splinter’s re-estimate finds the top 400’s total tax rate is about 13 points above the average once family structure, state taxes and double counting are corrected. California’s billionaire tax has already prompted departures before its residency cutoff, and the state’s Legislative Analyst warns of an ongoing loss of income tax revenue.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the current system lets the richest people grow their fortunes for decades without paying tax on most of the gain, so that billionaires can pay lower rates than workers. The camp’s answer is that the gain is not escaping tax but being deferred: when shares are sold, capital gains tax is due, and the companies whose value rises pay corporate tax on their profits. The low-rate estimates depend on counting paper gains as income and attributing a dynasty’s wealth to a single return. Where real gaps exist, such as tax-free borrowing for consumption, they are best closed by moving to a consumption-based tax that taxes the rich on what they spend, not by an annual appraisal of every asset they own, which no tax agency has ever done well.
Variation inside the camp: Some in the camp want to abolish the estate tax and lower taxes on capital gains and dividends further; others accept current taxes on capital but oppose any new tax on wealth or unrealized gains. Some who oppose state wealth taxes, such as California’s, do so mainly because of mobility between states and would not object as strongly to federal action, which brings them close to the third camp. The camp’s positive program is usually a consumption tax, in which saving and investment are exempt and spending is taxed progressively.

Camp: Tax billionaires’ gains and inheritances instead
Adherents: Governor Gavin Newsom of California, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/newsom-urges-a-national-billionaires-tax-while-fighting-one-in-california (26 June 2026, opposing Proposition 40 while proposing a national minimum tax on everyone worth more than $100 million, a ban on borrowing against stock portfolios to fund spending tax-free, and inheritance tax reform, because “the fight belongs at the federal level”); the Biden administration’s Office of Management and Budget, https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/omb/briefing-room/2022/03/28/presidents-budget-rewards-work-not-wealth-with-new-billionaire-minimum-income-tax/ (28 March 2022, proposing a Billionaire Minimum Income Tax requiring households worth over $100 million to pay at least 20 percent of their “full income, including unrealized appreciation,” later raised to 25 percent). Also: Senator Ron Wyden, author of the Billionaires Income Tax, which would tax billionaires’ gains on tradable assets each year and charge interest on deferred gains on other assets, https://www.finance.senate.gov/chairmans-news/wyden-unveils-billionaires-income-tax; economists Natasha Sarin and Lawrence Summers, who favor ending the step-up in basis, ending preferential rates on capital gains and stronger enforcement over a wealth tax, https://www.hamiltonproject.org/assets/files/SarinSummers_LO_FINAL.pdf; the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget on taxing gains at death.
Case: The first camp is right about the problem and wrong about the remedy. The richest Americans pay low rates because the income tax lets them defer gains indefinitely, borrow against them tax-free, and pass them on at death with the gain erased. Those are features of the income and estate taxes, and they can be fixed inside those taxes: tax gains at death or make heirs carry over the original basis, treat large loans against appreciated stock as a sale, set a minimum tax on the full income of the very richest including unrealized gains on traded assets, and enforce the estate tax properly. This reaches the same untaxed growth that motivates a wealth tax without inventing a new tax on the stock of property, which raises valuation problems for private companies, liquidity problems for founders, and constitutional problems in the United States. It also keeps the tax tied to gains, so a fortune that loses value is not taxed as if it had grown. And it must be done nationally: a state that taxes wealth alone simply exports its billionaires, as California has already begun to discover.
Best evidence: The step-up in basis at death costs the federal government about $41 billion in fiscal year 2026 and $570 billion over ten years (Treasury). Unrealized gains are 54 percent of the wealth of Americans worth over $100 million, so taxing gains reaches most of what a wealth tax would (Saez, Yagan and Zucman). Sarin, Summers and Kupferberg estimate that their package of base-broadening and enforcement reforms could raise over $4 trillion in a decade, comparable to wealth tax proposals. The OECD’s 2018 review found “limited arguments” for a net wealth tax in addition to broad-based capital income taxes and well-designed inheritance taxes. In Moore v. United States the Supreme Court expressly reserved the question of taxes on wealth and net worth, so an income-based approach carries less legal risk.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from the first camp, is that income-tax fixes have been proposed for decades and never enacted, that the estate tax shows how easily the very rich plan around taxes that hit only once, and that taxing gains does nothing about wealth that is already concentrated. The camp’s answer is that minimum income taxes and taxing gains at death have drawn support across the Democratic Party and, in carryover-basis form, from Republican Senator Mitt Romney together with Democratic Senator Michael Bennet (Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, https://www.crfb.org/blogs/closing-stepped-basis-loophole), while recent proposals to tax the largest fortunes directly lost in France’s National Assembly and in a Swiss referendum and are polling below 50 percent in California. A Billionaire Minimum Income Tax on unrealized gains, with a one-time charge on the existing stock of gains, does reach already-accumulated fortunes: Zucman himself estimated that a 25 percent minimum on full income would be equivalent to a wealth tax of more than 2 percent a year. The objection from the second camp, that taxing unrealized gains is itself a disguised wealth tax, is met by taxing only gains on traded assets each year and charging interest on deferred gains elsewhere, so nothing is taxed that has not actually increased in value.
Variation inside the camp: Some in the camp support taxing unrealized gains each year for the very richest (Wyden, the Biden minimum tax, Newsom’s national plan); others, including Sarin and Summers, are skeptical of mark-to-market taxation too and prefer to tax gains at death and close shelters within a realization-based system. Some emphasize stronger inheritance taxes, on the view that unearned inheritances are the most justifiable target; outside the United States, the Netherlands plans to replace its tax on deemed returns from savings and investments with a capital gains tax on actual returns from 2028 (NL Times, https://nltimes.nl/2026/09/29/dutch-savers-investors-pay-tax-wealth-2028), and Brazil enacted a minimum income tax of up to 10 percent on incomes above 1.2 million reais a year in November 2025 (Law 15,270/2025, summarized by Trench Rossi Watanabe, https://www.trenchrossi.com/en/legal-alerts/brazil-enacts-law-15270-2025-which-taxes-dividends-and-amend-personal-income-tax-rules/).

## Crux

The question under the others: can a tax on the stock of great fortunes be made to collect what its design promises, without large losses to avoidance, departures, investment and the existing tax base, and if it can, is a society entitled to tax wealth that has grown but has not been sold, simply because concentrated wealth carries power?

What evidence could settle: How much the very richest actually pay relative to their economic gains, which turns on resolvable measurement choices (how families are matched to returns, whether corporate tax and retained earnings are counted, how dynastic wealth is split), and on which the Balkir, Saez, Yagan and Zucman and Splinter estimates could be reconciled with better access to IRS data. How large avoidance, undervaluation and migration responses are under a modern design with third-party reporting and high thresholds, rather than under the low-threshold European taxes of the past; California’s Proposition 40, if it passes and survives legal challenge, would provide the first large test of a tax on billionaire fortunes, including how many leave, how private holdings get valued and what is collected. Whether taxing the stock of wealth reduces investment and business formation by more than taxing gains or inheritances would. And whether a federal wealth tax would survive the Supreme Court, which only a case can decide. Progress on these would move people between the first and third camps and within the second.

What no data can close: Whether a rise in value that has not been sold is income a person owes tax on, or property the state should leave alone until it changes hands. Whether great concentrations of private wealth are a danger to democratic self-government that justifies taxing them even at some cost in growth, or the legitimate result of creating value that the state has no claim to beyond ordinary taxes on income and spending. And whether fairness is judged by what a person has, what they earn, or what they consume. Evidence can show what each instrument raises and what it costs; it cannot tell a society which of these to treat as the measure of what the richest owe.
