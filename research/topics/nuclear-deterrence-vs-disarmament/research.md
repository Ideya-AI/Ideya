# Nuclear Weapons — research

## Overview

Nine countries hold nuclear weapons: the United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, China, India, Pakistan, North Korea and Israel. Together they held an estimated 12,187 warheads in January 2026, of which about 9,745 were in military stockpiles for potential use and about 4,012 were deployed on missiles and aircraft (SIPRI Yearbook 2026, https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2026/increasing-focus-nuclear-weapons-amid-heightened-escalation-risks-new-sipri-yearbook-out-now). This topic covers the argument over what to do with those arsenals: keep and modernize them as a deterrent, cut them by negotiated steps, ban them outright, or let more threatened states build their own; and the scientific dispute over what a nuclear war would do to the climate and the world’s food supply (“nuclear winter” and famine), which shapes how high the stakes of each choice are. It deliberately excludes nuclear power and reactors, which belong to a separate topic, and touches cyber, drone and gray-zone warfare, humanitarian intervention and the wider shape of the international order only where a camp’s case about nuclear weapons depends on them.

Why now: on 5 February 2026 the New START treaty between the United States and Russia expired with no replacement, leaving the two largest arsenals with no legally binding limits on deployed strategic weapons. The US did not formally answer a Russian offer to keep observing the treaty’s central limits informally for a year; President Trump instead said the United States would seek a “new, improved, and modernized Treaty” (Arms Control Today, March 2026, https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2026-03/news/new-start-expires-us-urges-modernized-treaty). The Federation of American Scientists noted that “for the first time since 1972, there is no treaty-bound cap on strategic nuclear weapons” (https://fas.org/publication/the-expiration-of-new-start/). The months since have compounded it. On 22 May 2026 the review conference of the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) ended without an agreed outcome for the third time in a row, deadlocked over Iran after the US-Israeli war on Iran that began on 28 February (UN News, https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/05/1167580; Arms Control Today, https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2026-06/news/2026-npt-review-conference-stymied-disputes). On 8 June SIPRI reported that the number of usable warheads in military stockpiles is rising again even as old warheads are dismantled, with China’s arsenal growing fastest. In March 2026 French President Emmanuel Macron announced an increase in France’s warhead numbers and talks with eight European countries on “forward deterrence” (Élysée, https://www.elysee.fr/en/emmanuel-macron/2026/03/02/visit-to-the-ile-longue-operational-base). The first review conference of the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, which bans the weapons outright and has 75 states parties but no nuclear-armed ones, meets at the UN in New York from 30 November to 4 December 2026 (ICAN, https://www.icanw.org/first_review_conference; UN Treaty Collection, https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=XXVI-9&chapter=26&clang=_en).

The debate is live because the arms-control framework built since the 1970s has largely lapsed just as the number of serious nuclear rivals has grown from two to three or more. Governments of the nuclear-armed states and their allies, backed by defense analysts such as those at the Heritage Foundation and the 2023 US Strategic Posture Commission, argue that deterrence has kept the peace among great powers since 1945 and must be strengthened to face both Russia and China. Arms-control organizations such as the Arms Control Association and the Union of Concerned Scientists accept deterrence for now but want the arsenals capped and cut by negotiated steps. A majority of the world’s non-nuclear states, the International Committee of the Red Cross, the Holy See, the 2024 Nobel Peace laureates Nihon Hidankyo and the campaign group ICAN hold that deterrence is a gamble that will eventually fail and that the weapons should be banned now. A smaller set of scholars and commentators, including John Mearsheimer and writers at the Cato Institute, argue that threatened states such as Ukraine or South Korea would be safer with bombs of their own, a view that 80 percent of South Koreans endorsed in a 2026 poll. And researchers on catastrophic risk, such as ALLFED and Founders Pledge, argue that whichever path is chosen, the world should prepare to limit the damage if deterrence fails, especially the famine that a nuclear winter could bring.

Note: About 4,012 warheads were deployed on missiles and aircraft in January 2026, and SIPRI estimates that between 2,100 and 2,200 of them were on high operational alert on ballistic missiles.
Context: Warheads on high alert can be launched within minutes. SIPRI says nearly all of them belong to Russia and the United States, with smaller numbers kept by France and the United Kingdom, and that China and India may occasionally have begun deploying some warheads on alert as well; China is estimated to have about 34 and India about 12 deployed, both new in SIPRI’s count. The number of deployed warheads rose by about 100 from January 2025, mainly because SIPRI reassessed Russian deployments and counted the new Chinese and Indian ones. The remaining stockpiled warheads are in central storage and would need preparation before use. The short launch time of alert forces is at the center of the debate over de-alerting: arms-control advocates want to lengthen decision time, while those who favor current postures argue that alert forces deter a disarming first strike.

Note: A 2022 study estimated that smoke from a nuclear war between India and Pakistan could leave 255 million to 2.5 billion people without food two years later, depending on its size, and that a US–Russia war could leave more than 5 billion without food.
Context: The authors model a war lasting one week in which cities burn and the smoke (soot, measured in teragrams, or millions of tonnes) rises into the stratosphere, dims sunlight and cools the planet for years. The figures are people who would starve by the end of year two if everyone else received a minimum diet of 1,911 kilocalories a day, with no international trade and half of livestock feed diverted to people; they are in addition to the deaths from the blasts themselves, which the same table puts at 27 million in the smallest scenario and 360 million in the largest. The study’s population base is the 6.7 billion people of 2010. The authors call the scenarios “illustrative examples.” The amount of soot that would reach the stratosphere is the disputed step. A 2018 Los Alamos study (Reisner et al.) of a 100-weapon India–Pakistan exchange found that most soot stayed below about 12 km, where rain washes it out, and that “none of the simulations produced a nuclear winter effect” (https://www.osti.gov/pages/biblio/1422919-climate-impact-regional-nuclear-weapons-exchange-improved-assessment-based-detailed-source-calculations). A 2025 consensus report from the US National Academies concluded that “too many uncertainties remained” to say how nuclear war would affect climate, while agreeing that if large amounts of soot did reach the upper atmosphere, substantial global cooling would follow (as reported in Arms Control Today, October 2025, https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2025-10/features/how-would-nuclear-war-affect-climate-scientists-are-divided). A 2024 study by researchers at ALLFED found that in a 150 Tg scenario, rapidly scaled “resilient foods” and maintained trade could in principle feed the whole global population (Rivers et al., Global Food Security, https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/food-system-adaptation-and-maintaining-trade-could-mitigate-globa/).

Note: China’s nuclear arsenal has grown from an estimated 350 warheads in 2020 to about 620 in 2026, the fastest growth of any country.
Context: SIPRI, using the same authors’ estimates, says China is expanding its arsenal faster than any other country and expects it to keep growing over the coming decade (https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/YB26%2008%20World%20Nuclear%20Forces.pdf). The growth slowed in 2025: the US Defense Department’s December 2025 report to Congress said China’s stockpile “remained in the low 600s through 2024, reflecting a slower rate of production,” but that China “remains on track to have over 1,000 warheads by 2030,” and that it has likely loaded more than 100 missile silos in three new silo fields (as quoted by the Federation of American Scientists, https://fas.org/publication/the-pentagons-slimmed-down-2025-china-military-power-report/). Even at 1,000, China would hold several thousand fewer warheads than either Russia or the United States. China’s growth is the main reason the 2023 US Strategic Posture Commission and others argue that the United States now faces “two nuclear peer adversaries,” and the main reason the US government says any successor to New START must include China. China has declined to join trilateral arms control talks and maintains a declared policy of no first use.

## Questions

Question: Have nuclear weapons kept the peace between great powers since 1945?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Deterrence kept peace / Peace despite the bomb
Why contested: There has been no war between great powers since 1945, the longest such stretch in centuries, and no nuclear weapon has been used in war since Nagasaki. But history offers no control case: no one can rerun the Cold War without the bomb, and other forces (economic interdependence, the memory of two world wars, US alliances, the spread of democracy, the UN system) changed at the same time.
Case for “Deterrence kept peace”: The United States and the Soviet Union confronted each other for four decades over Berlin, Cuba and the Middle East without fighting directly, something rival great powers had rarely managed before. Leaders on both sides recorded that the prospect of nuclear destruction shaped their choices. Nuclear-armed India and Pakistan have fought only limited clashes since 1998, and in May 2025 both sides took deliberate steps to keep their conflict conventional (SIPRI). Russia invaded non-nuclear Ukraine but has not attacked NATO territory. If deterrence explains even part of this record, giving it up would remove the strongest brake on great-power war.
Case for “Peace despite the bomb”: The record includes at least 13 documented near-uses through error or escalation (Chatham House, 2014), several averted by individuals who disobeyed protocol, so the absence of nuclear war may reflect luck as much as design. Conventional wars involving nuclear states have happened anyway: Korea, Vietnam, the 1969 Sino-Soviet border clashes, Kargil in 1999, and Russia’s war in Ukraine. Other explanations for great-power peace are strong on their own. And a record of 80 years says little about a risk that compounds: a small yearly chance of failure becomes large over a century.

Question: Is the standing risk of nuclear catastrophe an acceptable price for the security deterrence provides?
Tag: value
Poles: Acceptable for security / An intolerable gamble
Why contested: Even people who agree on the facts weigh them differently. One side compares deterrence with the conventional world wars it may prevent; the other compares it with the irreversible loss of most of humanity, however unlikely.
Case for “Acceptable for security”: All security policies carry risk, and the alternative to deterrence is not a world without danger but one in which aggressive states with conventional or hidden nuclear advantages can coerce their neighbors. The two world wars killed tens of millions without nuclear weapons. Experts in a 2024 survey put the chance of a nuclear catastrophe killing over 10 million people by 2045 at about 5 percent, and superforecasters at 1 percent (Forecasting Research Institute); managing that risk through safety, communication and prudent doctrine is better than abandoning the tool that makes great-power war unthinkable.
Case for “An intolerable gamble”: A policy that works only if it never fails once, forever, under every future leader, will eventually fail. The consequences are not comparable to conventional war: models of a US–Russia exchange put the number of people left without food after two years at more than 5 billion (Xia et al., 2022). No government, the ICRC says, could provide an adequate humanitarian response. Accepting a few percent per generation of losing civilization is not prudence but a bet with stakes no government has the right to place on behalf of the rest of the world.

Question: Would a large nuclear war cause a nuclear winter and global famine?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Global famine likely / Climate effects overstated
Why contested: The physics of the later steps is fairly well understood: if enough soot reaches the stratosphere, sunlight drops, temperatures fall and harvests fail for years. The dispute is about the first step, how much smoke burning cities would make and how high it would rise, which no experiment can test and on which modelers at civilian universities and US weapons laboratories disagree.
Case for “Global famine likely”: Studies led by Alan Robock, Owen Brian Toon and Lili Xia, using climate, crop and fishery models, find that even a regional India–Pakistan war could leave hundreds of millions to more than 2 billion people without food, and a US–Russia war more than 5 billion (Nature Food, 2022). Large modern wildfires have injected smoke into the stratosphere just as the models assume. A 2025 National Academies report agreed that if large amounts of soot reached the upper atmosphere, substantial global cooling would follow.
Case for “Climate effects overstated”: A 2018 Los Alamos study that simulated the firestorms in detail found that most soot stays low enough to be rained out and that “none of the simulations produced a nuclear winter effect” for a regional war. Modern cities may burn less intensely than assumed, and targeting plans may not strike cities on the scale the scenarios use. The 2025 National Academies report concluded that “too many uncertainties remained” to estimate the climatic effects with confidence. Even researchers who take famine seriously, such as ALLFED, find that adaptation and trade could greatly reduce the toll.

Question: Does the treaty banning nuclear weapons bring disarmament closer?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Stigma moves states / Ban without the armed
Why contested: The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons has 75 states parties but no nuclear-armed state and no ally under a nuclear umbrella. Whether a legal and moral norm adopted by non-possessors can change the behavior of possessors is a prediction about politics, with partial precedents on both sides.
Case for “Stigma moves states”: Bans on chemical weapons, landmines and cluster munitions created norms that shaped even non-signatories’ behavior, by making use politically costly and by persuading banks, investors and companies to withdraw. The ban treaty gives the majority of states, who have kept their NPT side of the bargain, a way to show that the nuclear states’ promised disarmament has stalled for decades. Public opinion in several umbrella states favors joining, and the stigma raises the political price of nuclear threats and new deployments.
Case for “Ban without the armed”: No weapon will be dismantled under a treaty that no nuclear-armed state has signed. The ban has no verification system able to confirm the elimination of an arsenal, and it divides the NPT community that is needed to stop proliferation. It puts pressure mainly on open democracies, where public opinion matters, while Russia, China and North Korea ignore it, which could weaken deterrence among the states least likely to misuse it. Japan’s government, for one, calls it an important goal but says it offers no practical path without the nuclear states.

Question: Should the United States expand its arsenal to deter Russia and China together now that New START has expired?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Build to deter both / Restraint avoids a race
Why contested: The United States is the only nuclear power planning against two nuclear peers at once, and no one knows how many warheads that requires or how rivals would respond to a US buildup.
Case for “Build to deter both”: The 2023 Strategic Posture Commission concluded that the United States will soon face “two nuclear peer adversaries” and recommended preparing to increase deployed warheads and production capacity. China may pass 1,000 warheads by 2030 (US Defense Department), and Russia has a large arsenal of shorter-range weapons never covered by treaty. If the two coordinate, forces sized for one rival may not deter both. The Heritage Foundation argues the US can cheaply load stored warheads onto existing missiles, and that building up is the best way to bring rivals to a negotiating table.
Case for “Restraint avoids a race”: A US buildup would invite Russia to upload its own reserves and China to accelerate, leaving everyone less secure and spending more; CBO already projects $946 billion over ten years for current plans. The United States can hold China’s cities and forces at risk with far fewer than its current numbers, because deterrence depends on the ability to retaliate, not on matching every warhead. The Arms Control Association and the Union of Concerned Scientists argue that mutual adherence to the old limits would preserve stability and leverage to bring China into talks.

Question: Would the world be safer if more threatened states had their own nuclear weapons?
Tag: empirical
Poles: More bombs, more caution / More bombs, more risk
Why contested: The question pits deterrence theory against proliferation risk. If nuclear weapons deter, giving them to threatened states should deter their attackers too; if nuclear weapons are dangerous, every new owner adds a new chance of accident, theft or war.
Case for “More bombs, more caution”: States that gave up or never got the bomb have been invaded or attacked: Ukraine, which gave up the Soviet arsenal on its territory under the 1994 Budapest Memorandum; Libya; Iran, struck in June 2025 and again from February 2026. North Korea, which built the bomb, has not been attacked. John Mearsheimer argued in 1993 that a nuclear Ukraine would be safe from Russia. South Korea and Poland face nuclear-armed neighbors and doubt how long US guarantees will last, and 80 percent of South Koreans now favor their own bomb (Asan Institute, 2026). Kenneth Waltz argued that nuclear states behave cautiously toward one another.
Case for “More bombs, more risk”: Every new arsenal adds new command systems, new crisis pairs and new chances for accident, unauthorized use or theft, and some new owners would be unstable. Proliferation by allies could unravel the NPT, triggering cascades (Japan after South Korea, Saudi Arabia after Iran, Turkey and others). New nuclear states are most vulnerable in the years before their forces can survive a first strike, which can tempt rivals to strike them preemptively. Sanctions and loss of US support would follow under current law.

Question: Is it morally permissible to threaten the mass killing of civilians in order to prevent war?
Tag: value
Poles: Threat prevents worse / The threat itself wrong
Why contested: Deterrence works by credibly threatening retaliation that would kill vast numbers of people who did not choose their government’s aggression. Whether a threat intended never to be carried out is morally different from the act it threatens has divided ethicists, churches and governments since the 1950s.
Case for “Threat prevents worse”: A threat that is never executed kills no one, and if it prevents aggression and great-power war it saves lives. Modern targeting doctrine aims at military targets, and governments can hold a deterrent while working toward reductions; the Catholic bishops of the 1980s, for example, accepted deterrence conditionally as a step toward disarmament. Abandoning deterrence unilaterally would hand coercive power to regimes with fewer scruples.
Case for “The threat itself wrong”: A credible threat requires a real readiness to kill millions, and making that readiness permanent corrupts the states that hold it. Pope Francis said in 2019 that “the possessing of nuclear weapons is immoral,” and Pope Leo XIV has said nuclear deterrence “is based on the irrationality of relations between nations” (Vatican News, 2026). The ICRC holds that any use would be incompatible with the principles of humanity. Even counterforce targeting would kill civilians on a massive scale through fire, fallout and famine.

Question: Should countries prepare to survive a nuclear war, with shelters, food reserves and plans for after first use?
Tag: value
Poles: Prepare to recover / Prevention comes first
Why contested: Preparation could save many lives if war comes, but some argue that it signals acceptance of nuclear war or diverts money and attention from preventing it. The answer depends partly on whether nuclear winter is real, and partly on whether planning for war makes it more thinkable.
Case for “Prepare to recover”: In the famine models, starvation kills many times more people than the blasts (Xia et al., 2022, put direct deaths in the largest scenario at 360 million against more than 5 billion without food), so food preparedness could matter more than anything done on the day. Research by ALLFED suggests that scaled-up “resilient foods” and maintained trade could keep most people fed. Switzerland has kept shelter space for its whole population for decades. Founders Pledge estimates that work on what happens after first use, such as managing escalation, receives at most about one in thirty nuclear-security grants.
Case for “Prevention comes first”: The ICRC says no adequate humanitarian response to nuclear use is possible, so the only effective protection is prevention. Civil defense in the Cold War was widely seen as offering false reassurance, and visible preparation can make leaders more willing to run risks. Money is scarce: every dollar for shelters is a dollar not spent on arms control, crisis communication or the diplomacy that makes war less likely.

Question: Could a world with no nuclear weapons be verified and kept stable?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Zero can be verified / Zero invites cheating
Why contested: Even supporters of eventual abolition differ over whether it can work in practice. Knowledge cannot be uninvented, and the closer the world gets to zero, the more a few hidden weapons would matter.
Case for “Zero can be verified”: Verification has advanced far beyond what existed during the Cold War: on-site inspection under past treaties, the global seismic and radionuclide network of the test-ban organization, satellite imaging and new methods for confirming that warheads have been dismantled. South Africa built and then dismantled its weapons under international inspection. A world near zero would also be one in which a cheater faced a united coalition with large conventional forces and the ability to rebuild.
Case for “Zero invites cheating”: Small stockpiles of fissile material are easy to hide, as the histories of Iraq, Iran and North Korea show. At zero, a state with even a handful of secret weapons would gain decisive leverage, so every crisis would become a race to rebuild. Deterrence experts argue that some arsenal, however small, is more stable than none. The disagreement over Iran’s program at the 2026 NPT review conference shows how hard it is to reach shared conclusions about compliance even under the current system.

## Positions

Camp: Strengthen nuclear deterrence
Adherents: The Heritage Foundation, Robert Peters, “A Road Map for Rebuilding America’s Nuclear Arsenal” (March 2025): https://www.heritage.org/sites/default/files/2025-03/IB5373.pdf | Congressional Commission on the Strategic Posture of the United States, final report (October 2023), chaired by Madelyn Creedon with Jon Kyl as vice chair: https://fas.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Strategic-Posture-Committee-Report-Final.pdf | NATO, “Nuclear deterrence” policy page (“As long as nuclear weapons exist, it will remain a nuclear alliance”): https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/topics_50068.htm | France, President Emmanuel Macron’s speech at Île Longue announcing more warheads and “forward deterrence” (2 March 2026): https://www.elysee.fr/en/emmanuel-macron/2026/03/02/visit-to-the-ile-longue-operational-base
Case: Nuclear weapons are the reason the great powers have not fought a world war since 1945. They make the cost of aggression against a nuclear state, or its allies, unmistakably unacceptable, and that certainty, not treaties or good intentions, is what has kept the peace. The danger today does not come from deterrence but from its erosion: Russia brandishes nuclear threats to shield its war in Ukraine, China is building the fastest-growing arsenal in the world, North Korea is expanding, and Iran has sought the bomb. The United States now faces two nuclear peers at once, with forces designed in the 1980s and a production complex that has not built a fully new warhead in decades. A credible deterrent must be modern, survivable, sized against both rivals together, and backed by a clear will to use it if attacked. Extended deterrence also prevents proliferation: allies who trust the US umbrella do not need bombs of their own. Disarmament is a worthy distant goal, but only when the conditions allow it; giving up weapons while adversaries keep theirs would invite coercion, as Ukraine learned.
Variation: The camp spans those who want to maintain and modernize current forces (NATO’s position, the UK, most US officials across administrations) and those who want to expand them (the Strategic Posture Commission recommended preparing to increase deployed warheads; Heritage proposes uploading stored warheads onto Minuteman III missiles and reopening submarine tubes closed under New START, and being ready to resume explosive testing). Some add missile defenses, such as the US Golden Dome program, as a complement. Most of the camp supports arms control in principle, if it is verifiable and covers China and Russia’s shorter-range weapons; France and the UK are extending their own deterrents to cover European allies.
Best evidence: No direct war between nuclear-armed great powers since 1945 (the empirical core of the camp). In May 2025, nuclear-armed India and Pakistan kept their sharpest conflict in decades at the conventional level (SIPRI Yearbook 2026). China grew from about 350 warheads in 2020 to about 620 in 2026 and, by the US Defense Department’s estimate, is on track for over 1,000 by 2030. Russia is estimated to hold about 1,794 non-strategic warheads in storage that no treaty has ever limited (SIPRI). The Heritage paper notes that the newest weapon in the US arsenal is more than 35 years old.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that deterrence relies on luck and will eventually fail, the camp answers that the alternative is not a world without risk but a world where aggressors are not deterred, and that the close calls of the Cold War led to better safeguards, hotlines and command systems; the right response to the risk of failure is to make deterrence more robust and stable, not to weaken it. To the claim that buildups provoke races, it answers that China’s buildup began while the United States was reducing, so restraint did not buy restraint. To nuclear-winter projections, it notes that the 2025 National Academies report found the climate effects too uncertain to estimate, and that a catastrophic war is exactly what deterrence exists to prevent.

Camp: Reduce arsenals step by step
Adherents: Arms Control Association, statement by Executive Director Daryl Kimball on the end of New START (February 2026): https://www.armscontrol.org/pressroom/2026-02/statement-end-new-start-requires-more-coherent-approach-trump-administration | Union of Concerned Scientists, Jennifer Knox, “Nuclear Weapons Without Limits? Avoiding a New Arms Race After New START” (January 2026): https://www.ucs.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/Nuclear-Weapons-Without-Limits.pdf | Rose Gottemoeller, former US chief negotiator of New START, “Scientists can save nuclear arms control,” Science (2026): https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aeh3556 (blocks automated checks) | Government of Japan, which relies on US extended deterrence while pursuing a “realistic and practical” path to disarmament and declining to join the ban treaty (Diplomatic Bluebook 2025): https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/other/bluebook/2025/en_html/chapter3/c030104.html (blocks automated checks)
Case: Deterrence is a fact of the present that cannot be wished away, but the arsenals are far larger than any rational deterrent requires, and the more weapons there are, on higher alert, in more hands, the greater the chance that one is used by mistake. The way out is the way that has already worked: negotiated, verifiable limits that both sides can trust. Treaties from SALT to New START took the world from about 70,000 warheads to about 12,000, and the INF Treaty eliminated an entire class of missiles. Each agreement brought inspections, data exchanges and predictability, which reduce the worst-case planning that drives arms races. Now that New START has lapsed, the United States and Russia should keep to its limits while negotiating a successor, bring China into talks on risk reduction, preserve the moratorium on explosive testing, and take steps that lengthen leaders’ decision time. The goal is a world without nuclear weapons, reached at a pace security allows and with the nuclear states at the table.
Variation: Within the camp, some (Japan, most NATO members) emphasize keeping deterrence strong during reductions; others (the Arms Control Association, the Union of Concerned Scientists, campaigns such as Back from the Brink) also favor no-first-use or sole-purpose policies, removing missiles from hair-trigger alert, and ending a single president’s sole authority to launch. Some of the camp see the ban treaty as complementary; others, including Japan’s government, see it as divisive.
Best evidence: The INF Treaty eliminated 2,692 missiles by June 1991 (Arms Control Association). The world inventory fell from about 70,300 warheads in 1986 to 12,187 in 2026 (Federation of American Scientists). The Union of Concerned Scientists estimates that without mutual limits, the United States and Russia could field hundreds more weapons in weeks and double their deployed forces within a few years. In the Forecasting Research Institute’s 2024 survey, experts estimated that six practical risk-reduction policies would together roughly halve the risk of a nuclear catastrophe by 2045.
Answer to the strongest objection: To those who favor buildup, the camp answers that arms races leave both sides less secure and poorer, and that the United States can deter China with a second-strike force far smaller than its current one; adding warheads invites Russia to upload its reserves and China to accelerate. To abolitionists who say step-by-step has stalled for decades, it answers that nothing is dismantled without the nuclear states, and that the reductions of the past forty years came from exactly this kind of diplomacy, which paused in a bad period but can be restarted.

Camp: Ban nuclear weapons now
Adherents: International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons (ICAN), Executive Director Melissa Parke, statement on the 2026 NPT review conference (22 May 2026): https://www.icanw.org/npt_review_conference_ends_with_no_plan_for_disarmament | International Committee of the Red Cross, “Hiroshima 80 years on: The humanitarian imperative to eliminate nuclear weapons”: https://www.icrc.org/en/article/hiroshima-80-years-humanitarian-imperative-eliminate-nuclear-weapons | The Holy See, Pope Leo XIV and Pope Francis on nuclear deterrence (Vatican News editorial, 17 April 2026): https://www.vaticannews.va/en/pope/news/2026-04/the-magisterium-of-the-popes-and-nuclear-weapons-editorial.html | Nihon Hidankyo, 2024 Nobel Peace Prize: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2024/press-release/ | 75 states parties to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, led diplomatically by countries such as Austria, Ireland, Mexico and South Africa
Case: Nuclear weapons are designed to kill civilians indiscriminately, and their use would violate the most basic principles of humanity and international humanitarian law. Hiroshima and Nagasaki showed what one small bomb does to a city; today’s arsenals could end modern civilization, and a war between even the smaller nuclear powers could starve billions. No government or aid agency could respond. Deterrence is not a law of nature but a bet that every leader, every early-warning system and every officer will behave rationally and correctly forever, and history records repeated near-misses averted by chance. The nuclear-armed states promised in Article VI of the NPT more than fifty years ago to negotiate disarmament in good faith; instead, they are growing their stockpiles, spending $119 billion a year, and blocking even a mention of the ban treaty at the NPT. Like chemical and biological weapons before them, nuclear weapons must be prohibited outright and stigmatized, so that possessing them is seen as illegitimate. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons does that, and its path to elimination is open to every nuclear state willing to join.
Variation: Some in the camp put the emphasis on the law and the treaty (ICAN, Austria and other states parties), some on moral teaching (the Holy See), and some on humanitarian consequences and witness (the ICRC, Nihon Hidankyo). The camp also uses financial pressure, such as divestment campaigns that persuade banks to stop financing nuclear-weapons producers, and local action, such as city resolutions supporting the treaty.
Best evidence: Acute deaths of 90,000 to 166,000 in Hiroshima and 60,000 to 80,000 in Nagasaki from two small bombs (Radiation Effects Research Foundation). Famine models projecting more than 2 billion without food after an India–Pakistan war and more than 5 billion after a US–Russia war (Xia et al., Nature Food, 2022). Thirteen documented cases of near nuclear use between 1962 and 2002 (Chatham House). Three consecutive failed NPT review conferences (2015, 2022, 2026) and military stockpiles that are now rising (SIPRI). The ICRC’s judgment that no adequate humanitarian response to nuclear use is possible.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the claim that a ban without nuclear states achieves nothing, the camp answers that norms change behavior before they bind everyone: the landmine and cluster-munition bans reduced use and production even by non-signatories, and the ban treaty already shapes investment decisions and public debate in umbrella states. Step-by-step disarmament, it argues, has had its chance for decades and has delivered modernization and growth instead. To the claim that deterrence kept the peace, it answers that a policy which has nearly failed many times cannot be called a success, and that the risk compounds every year the weapons exist.

Camp: Let threatened states build their own bombs
Adherents: John Mearsheimer, University of Chicago, “The Case for a Ukrainian Nuclear Deterrent,” Foreign Affairs (1993): https://www.foreignaffairs.com/articles/ukraine/1993-06-01/case-ukrainian-nuclear-deterrent | Cato Institute, Doug Bandow, “Let South Korea Build the Bomb”: https://www.cato.org/commentary/let-south-korea-build-bomb (blocks automated checks) | Cheong Seong-chang, Sejong Institute, author of “Why We Should Become a Nuclear Power” (2023) and founder of the ROK Forum for Nuclear Strategy [no direct statement URL confirmed]
Case: Nuclear weapons are the one sure guarantee of a state’s survival against a nuclear-armed neighbor, and promises from distant allies are only as good as the next election in Washington. The record is stark: Ukraine gave up the Soviet nuclear weapons on its soil in 1994 in exchange for security assurances and was invaded twice by one of the guarantors; Libya gave up its program and its government was overthrown; Iran stayed short of a bomb and was bombed in 2025 and 2026; North Korea built one and has not been attacked. The same logic that the nuclear powers apply to themselves applies to threatened democracies such as South Korea, which faces a nuclear North Korea and a growing Chinese arsenal. Kenneth Waltz argued that nuclear weapons make states cautious, because no leader can expect to gain from a war that could destroy his own country. Allowing capable, stable allies to defend themselves would also let the United States reduce its own commitments and the risk of being drawn into their wars.
Variation: The camp ranges from realist scholars who see proliferation in general as stabilizing (Waltz, who died in 2013, applied this even to Iran) to those who favor only selective proliferation among stable US allies (Bandow, South Korean advocates such as Cheong), often paired with a US withdrawal from alliance commitments. Most South Korean supporters of a national bomb also support keeping the US alliance.
Best evidence: Eighty percent of South Koreans supported acquiring their own nuclear weapons in 2026, a record for the Asan Institute’s poll. No nuclear-armed state has been invaded and conquered. Iran, without a bomb, was attacked by the United States and Israel in June 2025 and from 28 February 2026, a war that killed its Supreme Leader (Congressional Research Service, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48887/R48887.1.pdf). India and Pakistan, both nuclear-armed, kept their May 2025 conflict conventional (SIPRI).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the warning that more nuclear states mean more chances of accident and war, the camp answers that the nuclear age has produced no war between nuclear states larger than border clashes, and that the stable democracies most likely to seek bombs today would manage them at least as responsibly as the existing nine. To the warning of a proliferation cascade, it answers that the cascade is already being driven by the weakening of US guarantees and by Russian and North Korean threats, and that pretending otherwise leaves allies exposed.

Camp: Prepare to survive a nuclear war
Adherents: Alliance to Feed the Earth in Disasters (ALLFED), research on feeding everyone after an abrupt sunlight reduction (Rivers, Denkenberger et al., Global Food Security, 2024): https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/food-system-adaptation-and-maintaining-trade-could-mitigate-globa/ and https://allfed.info/ | Founders Pledge, Christian Ruhl, “Global Catastrophic Nuclear Risk: A Guide for Philanthropists” (July 2023): https://www.founderspledge.com/research/global-catastrophic-nuclear-risk-a-guide-for-philanthropists
Case: The argument between deterrence and disarmament is about preventing a nuclear war, and prevention matters most. But whichever side wins, nuclear weapons will exist for decades, and no strategy reduces the risk to zero. Almost all the work and money in the field goes to the period before the first detonation; almost none goes to what happens after, even though decisions made then would determine whether a single use becomes a global war, and whether a war becomes a global famine. In the famine models, starvation kills many times more people than the blasts, and starvation is preventable: food can be grown in greenhouses, from seaweed and from industrial processes, if plans and supply chains are ready and trade continues. Preparing to limit the damage is not accepting nuclear war; it is insurance against the failure of every other policy.
Variation: Some in the camp focus on food and supply-chain resilience (ALLFED); others on managing escalation after a first use and keeping communication open between adversaries (Founders Pledge’s “right of boom” agenda); others on traditional civil defense, such as Switzerland’s long-standing policy of shelter space for its entire population.
Best evidence: In Xia et al. (2022), the largest scenario has 360 million direct deaths but more than 5 billion people without food two years later, so most of the toll would come after the war ends. Rivers et al. (2024) found that in a 150 Tg nuclear-winter scenario, scaling up resilient foods and maintaining trade could “unlock the potential to feed the entire global population,” while without adaptation and trade the model predicts global famine. Founders Pledge estimated, from grant databases, that interventions after first use make up at most about one in thirty grants in nuclear-security philanthropy, and called that an upper bound (Ruhl, 2023).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the ICRC’s point that no adequate humanitarian response is possible, the camp answers that this is true for the cities struck but not for the billions far from any blast whose survival would depend on food. To the fear that preparation makes war more thinkable, it answers that the evidence for that effect is weak and that leaving billions to starve for want of planning is not a moral position. It does not ask anyone to give up work on prevention; it asks that a small share of effort go to the scenario everyone else assumes away.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others is whether nuclear weapons, kept indefinitely, reduce the overall danger of catastrophic war or merely postpone it. Every camp wants to avoid a nuclear war; they divide over whether the weapons are the main thing preventing one or the main thing that could cause one, and over how to weigh a small, persistent chance of catastrophe against the wars it may deter.

What evidence could settle: how much of the great-power peace since 1945 deterrence actually explains, compared with other causes; how likely nuclear use is each year under different postures (more or fewer weapons, higher or lower alert, more or fewer owners), judged from close calls, crisis behavior and expert forecasts; whether arms buildups or arms limits have made rivals more or less likely to restrain themselves; whether a ban norm changes the behavior of states that reject it; whether disarmament to low numbers or zero could be verified; and how much smoke a nuclear war would put into the stratosphere, which decides whether a war between any nuclear powers would also be a global famine. Better models of urban firestorms, better data on how leaders actually decided in past crises, and the next decade of US, Russian and Chinese choices after New START would all move these questions.

What is a value choice no data can close: how much a small chance of losing most of humanity should weigh against the security of the people deterrence protects today; whether threatening to kill millions of civilians is permissible if the threat is meant never to be carried out; whether states may stake the lives of people in countries that have no say over their arsenals; how far a country should rely on another’s promise to defend it rather than on its own weapons; and whether preparing to survive a nuclear war is prudence or a step toward accepting one. A person who thinks the yearly risk is tiny but who treats the extinction of humanity as uniquely bad, and a person who thinks the risk is real but who weighs present aggression more heavily, can look at the same evidence and choose different camps.
