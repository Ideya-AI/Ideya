# Free Will — research

## Overview

Free will is the question of whether people choose what they do in a way that makes them the real authors of their actions, given that every choice has prior causes: genes, upbringing, circumstances, brain states and, if physics is deterministic, the whole state of the universe before they were born. Bound to it is the question that gives it stakes: whether anyone ever deserves blame or praise for what they do, in the sense that a person can be owed resentment, condemnation or credit simply because of what they did. This topic covers the philosophy: what freedom would have to be, whether it can exist in a world of causes, what neuroscience, psychology and physics add, and whether moral responsibility survives if the answer is no. It deliberately excludes what the criminal law should do about it — sentencing, retribution versus rehabilitation, prisons and the insanity defense as policy belong to the crime and punishment topic — and treats legal facts here only where they show how institutions already answer the responsibility question. It also leaves aside whether moral facts are real at all, whether life has meaning without cosmic purpose, and whether artificial systems could be agents, which are separate topics.

Why now: in July 2026 the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences published the largest survey yet of neuroscientists’ philosophical views. Of 2,657 neuroscientists surveyed in April and May 2025 by the Mind-Brain Group at the University of Navarra, 17.52 percent agreed or strongly agreed that “the human being does not have freedom in their actions, as their nervous system determines their behavior,” and 59.26 percent disagreed — even though 64 percent endorsed reductive physicalism, the view that mental activity is fully reducible to brain function (Navarro-Peña and colleagues, PNAS 123(29), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13389498/). The finding cut against a popular story in which brain science has disproved free will, and it arrived the same month as a 44-country study in the British Journal of Social Psychology finding that people with stronger free will beliefs were more punitive toward everyday wrongdoing (Walton, Kirkland and Bastian, 2026, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382202/). Both followed a public turn in the argument: the Stanford neurobiologist Robert Sapolsky’s Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will (2023) argued that no one deserves praise or blame, and the Trinity College Dublin neurogeneticist Kevin Mitchell’s Free Agents (2023) argued from the same biology that free will is real. In November 2025 the question was the subject of a sold-out Doha Debates town hall in Qatar with the philosopher Gregg Caruso (Fairfield University, https://www.fairfield.edu/news/2026/january/scholars-examine-free-will-at-qatar-panel.html).

The debate is live because three answers each have strong defenders and none has displaced the others. Compatibilists hold that freedom is the capacity to act on one’s own reasons without coercion, which a caused world allows; they are the majority among professional philosophers, 59 percent in the 2020 PhilPapers Survey. Libertarians, in the philosophical sense, hold that a free choice must not be fixed by the past; the view is the official teaching of the Catholic Church and is held across many religious traditions, and in survey experiments it is close to what most laypeople say when asked in the abstract. Free will skeptics hold that neither caused nor chance choices can make anyone deserve blame or praise in the backward-looking sense, and that blame, punishment and pride should be rebuilt on other grounds. The participants are philosophers, who have argued this since antiquity and in its modern form since Hobbes and Hume; neuroscientists and psychologists, who since Benjamin Libet’s experiments in the 1980s have tried to test it; physicists, whose interpretations of quantum mechanics disagree about whether the world is deterministic at all; theologians, for whom it is tied to divine foreknowledge, grace and sin; and lawyers, whose systems of criminal responsibility assume an answer.

Note: In Libet’s experiments, electrical activity preparing a spontaneous movement began about 550 milliseconds before the act, while people reported first being aware of wanting to move only about 200 milliseconds before it.
Context: Participants flexed a wrist whenever they felt like it and reported, by watching a fast clock, when they first felt the urge. The readiness potential, a slow buildup of electrical activity over the motor areas of the scalp, began about 350 to 400 milliseconds before the reported awareness. Libet concluded that voluntary acts are initiated unconsciously, but argued that conscious will could still veto an action in the roughly 100 milliseconds left before the movement — “free won’t.” The experiment became the most cited argument that the brain decides before we do. Later work challenged that reading: Aaron Schurger and colleagues (2012) modeled the readiness potential as the buildup of random neural noise toward a threshold rather than a decision, and Uri Maoz and colleagues (eLife, 2019, https://elifesciences.org/articles/39787) found the readiness potential present for arbitrary choices but “strikingly absent” for deliberate ones, in a task deciding real $1,000 donations to nonprofits.

## Questions

Question: Can a choice be free if prior causes fixed it?
Kind: value
Poles: Yes, if it is yours / No, the future must be open
Why contested: This is the classic question, and it is not settled by any fact about the world, because both sides agree on what happens when a person deliberates and acts; they disagree about what “free” has to mean for the act to be the person’s own in the way that matters. If freedom is acting on one’s own reasons, without coercion, compulsion or manipulation, a determined world contains plenty of it. If freedom requires that one could have done otherwise holding the entire past and the laws of nature fixed, a determined world contains none. Professional philosophers lean heavily to the first answer, 59 percent compatibilist in 2020; laypeople asked in the abstract lean to the second.
Case for yes, if it is yours: What people care about when they ask whether a choice was free is whether it came from them — from their values, reasons and deliberation — rather than from a gun to the head, a brain tumor or a hypnotist. All of those distinctions survive determinism intact. Harry Frankfurt showed in 1969 that a person can be responsible even when they could not have done otherwise, as long as they acted for their own reasons: if a neuroscientist would have intervened to make you choose X had you started to choose Y, but you chose X on your own, you are responsible for X. John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza’s account makes the relevant capacity precise: an agent is responsible when the mechanism producing the action is their own and responsive to reasons. Daniel Dennett called this “the varieties of free will worth wanting.” Requiring freedom from causation itself asks for something no one could want on reflection: a choice uncaused by one’s character and reasons would not be more one’s own, but less.
Case for no, the future must be open: If determinism is true, every choice you make was fixed by events before you were born together with the laws of nature, and you control neither. Peter van Inwagen’s Consequence Argument makes this precise: if you cannot change the past or the laws, and the past plus the laws entail your action, you cannot do otherwise than you do. Calling a choice free because it flows from your desires does not help when the desires themselves are the products of causes you never chose. Manipulation cases press the point: a person whose values were engineered by neuroscientists to produce a crime is not responsible, and Derk Pereboom argues there is no principled difference between that person and anyone whose values were produced by ordinary deterministic causes. The everyday sense of freedom — that when you choose, more than one future is genuinely available — is exactly what determinism denies.

Question: Can anyone deserve blame for what they could not ultimately control?
Kind: value
Poles: Desert is real / No one deserves it
Why contested: Even if people act freely in some sense, blaming someone in the full moral sense — resenting them, condemning them, holding that they deserve to suffer some harm because of what they did — is a demand that seems to require more. Whether it requires being the ultimate source of oneself, which no one could be, or only the right kind of control over one’s actions, is the deepest disagreement in the debate, and it divides even those who agree that determinism is true.
Case for desert being real: Moral responsibility is built into the relationships that make human life recognizably human. P. F. Strawson argued in “Freedom and Resentment” (1962) that resentment, indignation, gratitude and love are not theoretical conclusions that a discovery about causation could overturn; they are how people take each other seriously as persons rather than as objects to be managed. A person who betrays a friend knowingly and for gain deserves the friend’s resentment because of what they did, and to deny this is to treat the betrayer as a malfunctioning mechanism, which is a failure of respect. On compatibilist accounts, desert follows from responsiveness to reasons: an agent who could grasp that the act was wrong and was able to act on that recognition deserves blame for failing to. Requiring ultimate self-creation sets a standard no possible being could meet, which suggests it is the wrong standard.
Case for no one deserving it: To deserve to suffer for what you did, the action must be up to you in a way that makes the suffering fair. But you did not choose the genes, upbringing, brain and circumstances that produced your character, and your choices flow from that character. Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument puts it plainly: to be truly responsible for how you act you would have to be responsible for how you are, which would require having chosen how you are, which would require already having been someone who could choose — a regress no one can complete. Neither determinism nor chance changes this; a random choice is no more yours. Robert Sapolsky argues from biology that there is “no ground to praise or blame anyone in a moral sense,” and Derk Pereboom and Gregg Caruso argue that the forward-looking reasons for holding people to account — protection, reconciliation, moral growth — survive without desert.

Question: Does brain science show that decisions are made before we are aware of them?
Kind: empirical
Poles: The brain decides first / Conscious choice matters
Why contested: Since Benjamin Libet’s experiments in the early 1980s, brain activity has been found to precede people’s reported awareness of deciding to move, by about a third of a second in Libet’s EEG studies and by several seconds in later fMRI decoding. Whether that activity is the decision, or only background preparation that a later conscious process can use or override, is a question about what the signals mean, and it has become a scientific dispute in its own right.
Case for the brain deciding first: The basic finding has replicated many times: activity that predicts a movement begins before the person reports deciding to make it. In the fMRI studies of John-Dylan Haynes and colleagues, patterns of activity in frontopolar and parietal cortex carried information about a free left-or-right choice up to about seven seconds before awareness, and single-neuron recordings in epilepsy patients by Itzhak Fried and colleagues (2011) predicted the timing of movements before reported intention. The feeling of deciding appears to be a report on a process already under way. Daniel Wegner’s work on the “illusion of conscious will” showed that people can be led to feel they willed actions they did not cause, and to disown actions they did. If the conscious self is informed of decisions rather than making them, the picture of free will most people hold is wrong about how choice works.
Case for conscious choice mattering: The experiments do not measure what they are taken to measure. Aaron Schurger and colleagues showed in 2012 that the readiness potential is well explained as the averaging of random neural fluctuations that cross a threshold, not a decision made in advance, and Uri Maoz and colleagues found in 2019 that it is absent for deliberate choices about real $1,000 donations, appearing only for arbitrary ones. Prediction accuracy is modest — 57 percent at best against 50 percent by chance in the 2011 ultra-high field study — so most of the outcome is not fixed by the early signal. Flicking a wrist at a random moment is a poor model of deciding whom to marry or whether to lie. In the 2026 PNAS survey only 17.5 percent of neuroscientists agreed that the nervous system leaves humans no freedom.

Question: Is the universe deterministic, and would chance help if it is not?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Open future exists / No room in physics
Why contested: Classical physics was deterministic; quantum mechanics, on its standard Copenhagen reading, is not, but deterministic interpretations such as many-worlds and Bohmian mechanics remain live, and in a 2025 Nature survey of more than 1,100 physicists only 36 percent chose Copenhagen. Even if the world is indeterministic, it is disputed whether randomness at the level of particles reaches the level of decisions, and whether, if it did, it would add control or only luck.
Case for an open future: The best physics we have does not say that the world is deterministic, and on its most widely held interpretation it says the opposite: outcomes of quantum events are not fixed by prior states. The neurogeneticist Kevin Mitchell argues that brains are noisy systems that exploit this indeterminacy — “causal slack” — to keep options open, and that organisms evolved to settle among those options by their own reasons and goals. Robert Kane’s event-causal libertarianism places the indeterminacy in moments of real inner conflict, where the agent’s effort settles which way the choice goes and makes the outcome theirs. On this view, determinism was never established by science; it was a nineteenth-century assumption, and the burden lies with those who say the future is closed.
Case for no room in physics: Whatever happens at the quantum scale, the brain operates at temperatures and scales where quantum effects are generally thought to wash out, so neural events behave as if deterministic; and if they did not, the added element would be chance. A choice that happens partly at random is not more under the agent’s control but less, which is the “luck objection” pressed against libertarians by compatibilists and skeptics alike: if two identical agents with identical pasts can choose differently, nothing about either agent explains the difference. Robert Sapolsky argues that biology leaves no gap through which free will could enter, and Sam Harris that neither determinism nor randomness gives anyone the control the folk notion requires. The question of physics, on this view, does not matter much to the question of freedom.

Question: Would society be worse off if people stopped believing in free will?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Belief keeps us moral / Disbelief makes us kinder
Why contested: Some psychologists and philosophers fear that if people come to believe their choices are not free, they will feel licensed to cheat, aggress and give up effort; others think the main effect would be less hatred, less retribution and more compassion. Early experiments seemed to support the first worry; later replications and a large meta-analysis did not; new studies link belief in free will both to happiness and to harsher punishment.
Case for belief keeping us moral: Kathleen Vohs and Jonathan Schooler found in 2008 that people who read a passage arguing that behavior is determined cheated more, and later studies reported more aggression and less helping. Belief in free will predicts positive affect: a 2026 study of 1,381 people in five European countries by Kathi Diel and colleagues found that people with stronger free will beliefs reported more positive feelings across the day, and exposure to pro-free-will viewpoints raised positive affect. The belief sustains the sense of agency that makes effort, self-control and hope make sense. Saul Smilansky has argued that even if libertarian free will is an illusion, the illusion does important moral work, and that dismantling it would wear away respect for persons and the idea that people can earn what they achieve.
Case for disbelief making us kinder: The cheating results have not held up: a 2020 series of five replications by Thomas Nadelhoffer and colleagues was “largely unsuccessful,” and a meta-analysis of about 145 experiments by Oliver Genschow and colleagues found little evidence that weakening belief in free will changes behavior once publication bias is accounted for. What belief in free will does predict is punitiveness: Azim Shariff and colleagues (2014) found that people who read about the neural causes of behavior recommended less retributive punishment, and the 2026 44-country study by Walton, Kirkland and Bastian found that stronger free will belief predicted more punitive responses to everyday wrongs. Sapolsky points to history: each time people came to see a condition such as epilepsy or schizophrenia as caused rather than chosen, they stopped blaming those who had it, and the world became more humane.

Question: Do ordinary people think responsibility requires an undetermined will?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Folk are incompatibilists / Folk are compatibilists
Why contested: Each side claims that common sense is on its side, and the burden of proof in the debate shifts accordingly: if ordinary people think responsibility requires an open future, compatibilism is a revision of the concept; if they do not, incompatibilism is a philosopher’s invention. Experimental philosophy has produced results that point both ways, depending on whether people are asked abstract or concrete questions and how determinism is described.
Case for folk incompatibilism: In the study by Hagop Sarkissian and colleagues (2010), majorities in the United States, India, Hong Kong and Colombia — 63 to 75 percent — said that no one can be fully morally responsible in a universe where every decision is completely caused by the past, and 65 to 85 percent said our universe is not like that. In the abstract version of Shaun Nichols and Joshua Knobe’s experiment, 86 percent gave the incompatibilist answer. When people answer as compatibilists, it is in vivid cases of terrible wrongs, where emotion pushes them to blame; Nichols and Knobe argue that this is a performance error, like other biases, and the calm abstract answer reveals the underlying theory.
Case for folk compatibilism: When determinism is described without implying that the person’s mind is bypassed — that their beliefs and desires play no role — most people judge agents in determined universes to act freely and be blameworthy; in the 2006 studies of Eddy Nahmias and colleagues, most participants judged a bank robber in a universe where a supercomputer could predict every action to be morally blameworthy. Nahmias and Dylan Murray argue that apparent incompatibilist responses come from misreading determinism as fatalism, the idea that what you decide makes no difference. The 2019 study by David Wisniewski and colleagues found that 86 percent of US respondents who agreed with a compatibilist definition of free will also agreed with an incompatibilist one, which suggests that the folk concept is not committed to either theory, and the neuroscientists surveyed in 2026 overwhelmingly combined physicalism with belief in freedom.

Question: Do blame and praise need a metaphysical foundation at all?
Kind: value
Poles: The practice stands alone / It needs real desert
Why contested: One influential line of thought rejects the question as posed. P. F. Strawson argued that holding people responsible is part of a web of attitudes — resentment, gratitude, love, indignation — that is not a theory about causation and could not be refuted by one. Others reply that if those attitudes presuppose something false, such as that people could have done otherwise in a deep sense, they cannot be justified merely by being natural.
Case for the practice standing alone: Asking whether determinism is compatible with responsibility treats responsibility as a hypothesis awaiting evidence. It is not; it is a form of life. People cannot step outside their reactive attitudes and decide to stop resenting betrayal or feeling grateful for kindness, and even if they could, the result would be a world in which people regard each other only as objects to be managed, treated or avoided. Any justification of the practice has to come from inside it, from the role it plays in human relationships. Pamela Hieronymi and others in the Strawsonian tradition argue that this is not evasion but the right account of what moral demands are. On this view the free will debate rests on a mistaken picture of what responsibility could need.
Case for real desert being needed: Some practices are natural and widespread and nonetheless unjust; that blame is entrenched does not show that it is deserved. Resentment carries a belief that the wrongdoer could and should have done otherwise, and if that belief is false the attitude is a mistake, however deeply felt. Derk Pereboom argues that much of what Strawson valued survives the loss of desert — love, gratitude in a modified form, forgiveness, moral protest — while the retributive core of blame, the idea that wrongdoers deserve to suffer, does not. Libertarians agree that the practice needs a foundation and argue that freedom from determination supplies it. Both deny that the question can be dissolved.

Question: Would giving up deserved blame and praise cost something of value?
Kind: value
Poles: Something precious lost / Little worth keeping lost
Why contested: Suppose no one deserves blame or praise in the backward-looking sense. Free will skeptics say that ordinary life would go on, and in some ways better: less hatred and vengefulness, more understanding. Their opponents say that the costs would be severe and partly hidden: the loss of the sense that achievements are earned, of the dignity of being treated as an agent, and of the grounds for proportionate punishment that protects the innocent from being treated as mere risks.
Case for something precious lost: Desert protects people as well as condemning them. If wrongdoers do not deserve punishment, what limits what society may do to them for the sake of safety? A system based only on prevention can justify detaining people who have done nothing yet, or holding offenders far longer than their acts warrant; desert sets a ceiling as well as a floor. Praise matters too: people want their achievements, kindness and courage to be credited to them, not to their luck in genes and upbringing, and C. S. Lewis argued that being punished because one deserves it is a way of being treated as a responsible person rather than a patient to be cured. Daniel Dennett argued that autonomous people are justly held responsible for what they did “because all of us depend on being able to count on them.”
Case for little worth keeping lost: What blame adds beyond protection, moral protest and the chance for reform is the infliction of suffering for its own sake, and that is precisely what cannot be justified if no one is ultimately responsible for who they are. The skeptic’s alternative, set out by Derk Pereboom and Gregg Caruso, includes a public-health model in which restrictions on dangerous people are limited by the same principles that limit quarantine — the least restrictive measure that is effective — so it does not license unlimited detention. Love, gratitude and joy at another’s goodness survive in modified forms, and life without the belief that one deserves one’s good fortune may make people more humble and more generous toward those who were less lucky. Sapolsky suggests replacing pride with gratitude for having turned out the sort of person who could do well.

Question: Does God’s foreknowledge or sovereignty leave room for human freedom?
Kind: value
Poles: Freedom requires openness / Freedom fits sovereignty
Why contested: For believers in an omniscient, all-governing God, the free will problem takes a theological form that predates modern physics: if God knows or ordains everything that will happen, can human choices be otherwise, and can people be blamed for sin? Christian, Jewish and Islamic traditions have each produced both libertarian and compatibilist answers, and the question still divides denominations.
Case for freedom requiring openness: The Catholic Church teaches that God created human beings as rational persons who “can initiate and control” their own actions, and defines freedom as the power “to act or not to act, to do this or that.” Arminian and Wesleyan Protestants, Jewish thinkers following Maimonides, and the Mu‘tazili school in Islam held or hold a similar view: human beings must have genuine alternatives, or God would be the author of sin and divine judgment would be unjust. Philosophers such as William Lane Craig and Alvin Plantinga defend libertarian freedom as central to answering the problem of evil: a world with free creatures who could choose wrongly is better than one without them.
Case for freedom fitting sovereignty: Reformed Christians, following Augustine’s later work, Luther and Calvin, hold that God ordains all that happens and that human beings are nonetheless free and responsible. The theologian John Frame, writing for The Gospel Coalition, defines the relevant freedom as “the freedom to do what you want to do,” which is “compatible with causation” — including God’s causation. Scripture, on this reading, affirms both divine sovereignty and human accountability side by side, as in the crucifixion, which it presents as both God’s plan and the free act of those who carried it out. In Islam the Ash‘ari doctrine of acquisition (kasb) holds that God creates human acts while humans acquire responsibility for them. On this view, demanding freedom from God’s governance asks the creature to be independent of the Creator, which no theist can coherently want.

## Positions

Camp: Hold people responsible, since freedom fits a caused world
Adherents: The Gospel Coalition, essay “Divine Sovereignty and Human Freedom” by the Reformed theologian John Frame, defining freedom as doing what you want, which is “compatible with causation” — https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/essay/divine-sovereignty-human-freedom/ ; Daniel Dennett (1942-2024), in “Just deserts,” his 2018 exchange with Gregg Caruso in Aeon, arguing that “autonomous people are justly held responsible for what they did” — https://aeon.co/essays/on-free-will-daniel-dennett-and-gregg-caruso-go-head-to-head ; also John Martin Fischer, Eddy Nahmias, Manuel Vargas, Pamela Hieronymi, the legal scholar Stephen Morse, and Christian List, whose Why Free Will Is Real (2019) argues that free will is a higher-level phenomenon that physical determinism does not threaten (https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/why-free-will-is-real/). Variation inside the camp: classical compatibilists, from Hobbes and Hume to Frame, define freedom as acting on one’s desires without constraint; Fischer and Mark Ravizza tie responsibility to acting from one’s own reasons-responsive mechanism, and Fischer’s “semicompatibilism” holds that responsibility is compatible with determinism even if the ability to do otherwise is not; List argues that alternative possibilities are real at the level of the agent even if physics is deterministic; followers of P. F. Strawson ground responsibility in the practice of reactive attitudes and partly reject the question as posed; revisionists such as Vargas concede that the everyday concept carries libertarian assumptions and argue that it should be revised rather than abandoned. Reformed Christian theology holds the same structure with God’s ordaining in place of physical causation. Dennett defended desert itself; some revisionists ground blame in its forward-looking role.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The question is not whether our choices have causes — of course they do, and a choice with no causes would be a random twitch, not an act of will — but whether they are ours. When you weigh reasons, consider consequences, consult your values and act on the result, without a gun to your head, a tumor in your brain or a hypnotist behind you, you are doing exactly what freedom is. Everything that matters about the difference between a free act and an unfree one — between the thief and the kleptomaniac, the adult and the small child, the person who chooses and the person who is coerced — survives the discovery that the universe runs on laws. That is why the law excuses insanity and duress but not ordinary motives, and why every human community distinguishes the deliberate from the accidental. The demand that a free choice be uncaused by the past, by contrast, cannot be met by any being and would not be worth having if it could: an act that did not flow from your character and reasons would be less yours, not more. Freedom is a natural capacity that evolution built and upbringing develops: the capacity to respond to reasons, to reflect on one’s desires, and to be moved by moral considerations. People who have it can be held responsible, and deserve the resentment, gratitude, blame and praise that responsible beings give one another. Treating them otherwise — as mechanisms to be managed — is the real affront to human dignity.
Best evidence: It is the settled majority view of the people who study the question most closely: 59 percent of philosophers at leading departments accepted or leaned toward compatibilism in 2020, as in 2009. Of 2,657 neuroscientists surveyed in 2025, 64 percent endorsed reductive physicalism yet only 17.5 percent agreed that the nervous system leaves humans no freedom, a pattern the survey’s authors describe as compatibilist. Ordinary people judge concrete cases the compatibilist way: in Nichols and Knobe’s study, 72 percent said a man in a fully determined universe who kills his family is fully morally responsible, and in Eddy Nahmias’s studies most judged a predicted bank robber blameworthy. The neuroscience that was supposed to refute free will has weakened on inspection: the readiness potential looks like accumulating noise in arbitrary choices and is absent for deliberate ones, and brain decoding predicts free choices only modestly above chance. And the law already works on compatibilist lines: the insanity defense, raised in about 1 percent of felony cases, excuses people whose capacities to understand or control their conduct are disabled, not people whose acts have causes.
Answer to the strongest objection — that if the past and the laws fixed your choice, you could not have done otherwise and so cannot deserve blame: The argument trades on an ambiguity in “could have done otherwise.” In the sense that matters for responsibility — that you had the capacity and the opportunity, and would have chosen differently had you seen the reasons differently — determined agents often could have done otherwise. Frankfurt’s cases show that the deeper sense is not needed anyway: a person who acts on her own reasons is responsible even if some hidden mechanism would have stopped her from doing anything else. The manipulation cases describe agents whose values were installed in a way that bypassed their own reasoning and history; ordinary causal histories do not do that, and to treat every cause as a manipulator is to assume the conclusion. Finally, the incompatibilist demand, followed through, makes freedom impossible for any being, determined or not, which is good reason to think it misdescribes what freedom is.

Camp: Ground responsibility in choices the past does not fix
Adherents: Catholic Church, Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraphs 1730-1731, teaching that God made the human person one “who can initiate and control his own actions” and defining freedom as the power “to act or not to act, to do this or that” — https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_one/article_3.html ; Kevin Mitchell, neurogeneticist at Trinity College Dublin and author of Free Agents: How Evolution Gave Us Free Will (2023), arguing on EconTalk in December 2025 that physics shows “inherent indeterminacy” and that if the future were fixed, “the idea that we might be exercising choice doesn’t even arise” — https://www.econtalk.org/free-will-is-real-with-kevin-mitchell/ (blocks automated fetching; loads in a browser); also the philosophers Robert Kane, Timothy O’Connor, Helen Steward, Alvin Plantinga and William Lane Craig, Arminian and Wesleyan Protestant traditions, and Jewish thought following Maimonides. Variation inside the camp: agent-causal libertarians such as O’Connor hold that the agent, as a substance, directly causes the choice; event-causal libertarians such as Kane locate indeterminism in the agent’s effortful deliberation in moments of inner conflict; naturalists such as Mitchell and Steward argue that organisms evolved to use physical indeterminacy to settle among open options by their own goals, with no appeal to a soul; theological libertarians ground freedom in the person’s creation as a rational being and see it as necessary for sin, grace and divine justice.
The case in the camp’s own voice: Every time you deliberate, you take it for granted that more than one future is open and that it is up to you which one happens. That is what choosing is. If the whole of what you will do was settled before you were born, your deliberation is a show in which the outcome was always fixed, and the sense that you are the author of your life is mistaken. Most people in every culture studied see this immediately, and they are right: you cannot deserve blame for doing what you could not have avoided, given everything that came before. The good news is that nothing in science requires the bleak conclusion. Physics on its best-supported reading is not deterministic; living things are not billiard balls but organized systems that hold their options open and settle among them for reasons of their own. Human freedom is the highest form of that capacity: the power, rooted in reason and will, to do this or that. Because people have it, they can be held responsible for what they do, they can change, and their love, achievements and repentance are genuinely their own. Compatibilism keeps the word “freedom” but gives up the thing people meant by it; skepticism at least admits what is lost. We hold that the thing is real.
Best evidence: Ordinary people across cultures hold this view when asked directly: in Sarkissian and colleagues’ 2010 study, 65 to 85 percent of students in the United States, India, Hong Kong and Colombia said our universe is not deterministic, and 63 to 75 percent said full moral responsibility is impossible in a universe that is. Belief in free will is high wherever it is measured: 82 percent of US respondents and 85 percent in Singapore in 2019, and an average of 3.72 on a five-point scale across 44 countries in 2026. Physics has not established determinism: in a 2025 survey of more than 1,100 physicists the most popular interpretation of quantum mechanics, Copenhagen, at 36 percent, holds that outcomes are not fixed in advance, and only 24 percent were confident in any interpretation. The neuroscience offered against free will is weak: the readiness potential is absent for deliberate decisions, and predictions of free choices from brain scans have peaked at 57 percent against 50 percent by chance. Among philosophers the libertarian share rose from 13.7 percent in 2009 to 18.8 percent in 2020, though the two surveys drew on somewhat different departments.
Answer to the strongest objection — that an undetermined choice is a matter of luck, not control: The objection assumes that if the past does not fix a choice, nothing does, so the outcome must be random. But on our view the agent settles it. In Kane’s account, a person torn between ambition and conscience is making an effort either way, and whichever effort succeeds, the choice is the product of that person’s own striving and reasons; the indeterminism is in the process of their willing, not outside it. On agent-causal views the agent is a cause in her own right, which is no more mysterious than causation itself. Mitchell adds that biological systems do not hand decisions to chance; they use indeterminacy to keep options open and then select among them by goals and meanings at the level of the whole organism. The luck objection proves too much: it would equally make any compatibilist choice the product of the luck of one’s genes and upbringing.

Camp: Give up blame and praise based on desert
Adherents: The Justice Without Retribution Network, housed at the University of Aberdeen School of Law and co-directed by Elizabeth Shaw, Gregg Caruso, Farah Focquaert and Derk Pereboom, which explores “the practical implications of free will scepticism” and non-retributive alternatives that “do not rely on a traditional understanding of free will” — https://www.abdn.ac.uk/law/why-us/partnerships/the-justice-without-retribution-network/ ; Robert Sapolsky, neurobiologist at Stanford University and author of Determined (2023), who says “we have no ground to praise or blame anyone in a moral sense” — https://neuroscience.stanford.edu/news/how-live-world-without-free-will ; Gregg Caruso, in “Just deserts,” Aeon, 2018: “it is never fair to treat anyone as morally responsible” in the basic desert sense — https://aeon.co/essays/on-free-will-daniel-dennett-and-gregg-caruso-go-head-to-head ; also Derk Pereboom, Galen Strawson, Sam Harris and Bruce Waller. Variation inside the camp: hard determinists such as Sapolsky hold that determinism is true and rules out free will; hard incompatibilists such as Pereboom and Caruso hold that free will of the kind desert requires is ruled out whether the world is deterministic or not, since chance would not supply control; Galen Strawson argues that ultimate responsibility is impossible in any possible world. They differ on what to keep: Pereboom retains forward-looking blame as moral protest aimed at reform and reconciliation, while Waller argues for abandoning the language of moral responsibility more fully. Not everyone who denies free will wants the belief abandoned: Saul Smilansky’s “illusionism” holds that the belief in libertarian free will is false but morally valuable, and that it may be better that it persist.
The case in the camp’s own voice: No one chose the genes they were born with, the womb they developed in, the family and culture that raised them, or the experiences that shaped their brain before they were old enough to reflect on any of it. Every choice a person makes flows from the person they had become by that moment, and that person was produced by things they did not control. Adding randomness does not help; a choice made partly by chance is not more yours. So no one deserves, in the basic sense, to suffer for what they did or to be exalted for it. This is not a counsel of despair. We can still protect people from danger, ask wrongdoers to recognize what they did and change, forgive, love, be grateful, and admire what is good. What we give up is the conviction that the people who did wrong deserve to be hurt for it and that those who did well earned their place over others — a conviction that has justified a great deal of cruelty and smugness. Each time humanity has learned the causes of something it once blamed — epilepsy, mental illness, addiction — the world has become more humane. Taking the science of behavior seriously means extending that understanding to everyone.
Best evidence: The science of behavior has steadily found causes where people once saw unconditioned choice: twin studies attribute on average 49 percent of variation in human traits to genes, and a meta-analysis of antisocial behavior attributes about 41 percent of its variation to genes and much of the rest to environments no child chooses. Neural activity predicts simple choices before people report making them, by about a third of a second in Libet’s experiments and seconds in later brain scans. The fear that disbelief in free will would make people immoral has not survived testing: a meta-analysis of about 145 experiments found little evidence that weakening belief in free will changes behavior once publication bias is accounted for, and five attempts to replicate the original cheating result were “largely unsuccessful.” What belief in free will does predict is punitiveness: across 8,917 people in 44 countries, stronger free will belief went with more punitive responses to everyday wrongs. Neurobiological evidence now appears in a growing number of US criminal cases, with more than 250 published opinions in 2012 alone, as courts confront the causes of behavior case by case.
Answer to the strongest objection — that without desert nothing limits what society may do to wrongdoers, and that life without blame and credit would be impoverished: The quarantine model answers the first. We may restrict people who pose a danger only as far as is necessary to protect others, by the least restrictive effective means, just as we may quarantine carriers of disease though they are blameless — and because they are blameless, we owe them humane conditions, treatment and a path back. That is a firmer limit than desert has provided, since retributive systems have justified some of the harshest sentences in the world. As for the rest of life, love never depended on desert; people love their children before the children have done anything. Gratitude survives as gladness that someone acted well, admiration as appreciation of what is good, and resentment can give way to protest that aims at repair rather than retaliation. What disappears is the satisfaction of seeing wrongdoers suffer because they deserve it, and we think the world can do without it.

Note for the editor: hybrid and rejecting camps were considered. Saul Smilansky’s illusionism (free will is an illusion, but one worth keeping) is a real hybrid, but no institution holds it, so it appears as a variation inside the third camp. P. F. Strawson’s view that responsibility needs no metaphysical foundation partly rejects the question, but its adherents affirm that people are responsible in a caused world, so it is noted inside the first camp; it is also a Question. Order: the first camp is the majority view among philosophers and neuroscientists and matches concrete-case intuitions; the second is closest to what most people say in the abstract and to the teaching of the largest religious bodies, and could reasonably go first if order is meant to follow the public rather than the informed.

## Crux

The question under the others is what kind of control a person must have over a choice for it to be fair to blame or praise them for it. One answer is that it is enough for the choice to come from the person’s own reasons and character, through capacities to understand, deliberate and respond to reasons that work normally and are not coerced or bypassed. The other is that the person must be its ultimate source, so that the choice is not fully settled by anything outside their control, including the past and the causes that made them who they are. On the first answer, freedom and deserved blame fit a world of causes. On the second, they need choices the past does not fix, and the dispute turns to whether such choices exist (libertarians say yes) or are impossible (skeptics say no). Almost every other disagreement on the page, from Libet’s readiness potential to whether people should keep believing in free will, matters because of how it bears on this one.

What evidence could settle: how far conscious deliberation actually shapes what people do, rather than reporting decisions made elsewhere in the brain; whether the processes behind deliberate choices are deterministic, or noisy in ways that brains use; how genes, development and circumstance constrain the capacities to respond to reasons; what ordinary people mean by free will when determinism is described accurately; and what happens to behavior, punishment and well-being when people stop believing in desert. Neuroscience, physics, behavioral genetics, experimental philosophy and social psychology can all move this part, and have: the readiness potential no longer looks like a hidden decision, and the claim that disbelief makes people cheat has not survived replication. None of it settles the question alone, because every camp can accept the same findings: compatibilists say they describe how free agents work, and skeptics say they show the causes behind every agent.

What is a value choice: whether fairness in blame requires only that people act from capacities they actually have, or requires that they be responsible for having those capacities in the first place; and how to weigh what desert gives — a limit on punishment, a sense that achievements and wrongs belong to the people who did them, the reactive attitudes that make relationships personal — against what it costs: suffering inflicted because it is deserved, and credit and condemnation handed out for differences that began in luck. No experiment can tell a reader whether being the kind of creature who weighs reasons is enough to make a choice one’s own, or whether nothing short of being one’s own first cause would be.
