# Boys & Men Falling Behind — research

## Overview

This topic covers the claim that boys and men in rich countries, and in the United States above all, are falling behind: in school, where girls now earn better grades, finish high school more often and take most college degrees; in work, where the share of prime-age men in the labor force has drifted down for seventy years and the wages of men without a degree have stalled; and in well-being, where men die about five years sooner than women, account for roughly four in five suicides and most overdose deaths, and report fewer close friends than a generation ago. It covers the disagreement over what those gaps mean, what causes them (how schools are run, the loss of well-paid work for men without degrees, the decline of marriage and fatherhood, rigid ideas about masculinity, poverty and race, or bias against males), and what, if anything, should be done: help aimed specifically at boys and men, a revival of traditional roles, efforts to change masculine norms, universal policies that ignore sex, or the removal of rules said to disadvantage males. It deliberately excludes the general debate over loneliness and community, the effect of AI on jobs and the cost of college, which have their own pages and are drawn on only where they bear on men; it also leaves aside gender identity and the separate debates over women’s rights (the pay gap, abortion, violence against women) except where they enter the comparison between the sexes.

Why now: on 18 November 2026 the American Institute for Boys and Men, the think tank founded in 2023 by Richard Reeves, formerly of the Brookings Institution, held its inaugural national summit in Washington, where California Governor Gavin Newsom said “Too many boys are falling behind in school” and reported results from the executive order on boys and men he signed in July 2025, including more than 11,000 men enrolled in a state Men’s Service Challenge and a teacher recruitment campaign aimed at men (Office of the Governor, https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/11/18/governor-newsom-addresses-crisis-facing-boys-and-men-at-national-summit-in-washington-dc/). The next day, 19 November 2026, England’s Department of Health and Social Care published a one-year progress report on the country’s first men’s health strategy, committing up to £6.3 million, with Movember and the People’s Health Trust, to community programs for men underserved by traditional services (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/mens-health-strategy-one-year-on-report/mens-health-strategy-one-year-on-report). Earlier in 2026 Virginia’s General Assembly created what its backers call the first legislatively created permanent state commission on boys and men, which first met in July (https://vaboysandmen.org/news/virginias-boys-and-men-commission-first-meeting/), while a bill to create a similar commission in Washington State failed for the fifth year running (https://wibm.substack.com/p/fifth-attempt-for-a-washington-boys-and-men-commission-falls-short-2026).

The debate is live because the facts are largely agreed and their meaning is not. Few dispute that women now outnumber men among college graduates in almost every rich country or that men die younger and more often by their own hand; but men still hold most top jobs and earn more on average, teenage girls report far more depression and anxiety than boys, and the male gaps are much wider among poor, Black and working-class men than among the affluent. So the argument is over whether this is a gender problem at all, whether naming it helps boys or fuels resentment of women, and whose remedy fits. The participants include Reeves and his institute, which want male-specific policy alongside support for women’s progress; governors and legislators of both parties, from Newsom to the bipartisan Virginia commission; the British government; social conservatives such as Senator Josh Hawley, the Heritage Foundation and the Institute for Family Studies, who trace the problem to the decline of marriage, fatherhood and confident manhood; psychologists and advocacy groups such as the American Psychological Association and Equimundo, who blame restrictive masculine norms; feminist and sociological critics such as Steven Roberts of Monash University and Jessica Calarco of the University of Wisconsin–Madison, who see a recurring “crisis” narrative that obscures class and race and risks blaming women; men’s-rights organizations such as the National Coalition for Men, who argue that schools, courts and governments discriminate against males; and, since the 2024 US election, politicians of both parties competing for the votes of young men.

Note: Women earned 58.5 percent of US bachelor’s degrees in 2021–22, up from 43.1 percent in 1969–70; they first passed half in 1981–82.
Context: In 2021–22 US colleges awarded 1,179,719 bachelor’s degrees to women and 835,316 to men. Those who see a crisis for boys treat this as the clearest single sign of it; those who doubt the crisis framing note that men still dominate the highest-paying majors, such as engineering and computer science, and that the gap is widest among Black and low-income students.

Source label: US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Population Survey, series LNU01300061 (labor force participation rate, men 25 to 54)
Source URL: https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/LNU01300061
Note: About 89 percent of American men aged 25 to 54 were in the labor force in 2025, down from about 97 percent in the mid-1950s.
Context: The labor force counts people who are working or actively looking for work, so the roughly 11 percent outside it are neither; the reasons range from illness or disability to caregiving, school and early retirement, and among men without a degree who are out of work, about half cite illness or disability (American Institute for Boys and Men, https://aibm.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/WORKINGCLASS_FORPUB.pdf). The decline was slow and steady from the 1960s to the mid-2010s, reaching a low of 88.2 percent in 2014 and 87.9 percent in the pandemic year 2020, and has recovered modestly since. Over the same period the rate for women aged 25 to 54 rose from 38.7 percent in 1954 to 77.8 percent in 2025 (BLS series LNU01300062, https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/LNU01300062), so the gap between the sexes has narrowed from both directions. Economists disagree about why (see Questions): some point to the loss of manufacturing and construction jobs, others to disability programs, opioids, criminal records, the decline of marriage and the rising appeal of leisure.

England’s Health Secretary Wes Streeting said men there are dying nearly four years earlier than women when he launched England’s first men’s health strategy in November 2025 (https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-unveils-englands-first-ever-mens-health-strategy). Women outlive men on average in every country, and Our World in Data’s review attributes the gap partly to biology (boys are more vulnerable from birth to prematurity, infections and some genetic disorders) and partly to behavior: at young ages to accidents, violence, suicide and poisonings, and at older ages to differences in smoking, drinking, drug use and occupational risk (https://ourworldindata.org/why-do-women-live-longer-than-men). The behavioral share, and how much of it reflects norms that could change, is where the debate lies.

## Questions

Question: Should policy aim help at boys and men specifically, or at everyone who is struggling?
Tag: value
Poles: Male-specific help / Universal programs
Why contested: The gaps are real on average but heavily concentrated among poor, Black and working-class males, and many of the remedies proposed (tutoring, mentoring, apprenticeships, mental health outreach) could be offered to everyone. The question is whether sex is the right lens for policy, and whether naming boys and men as a group helps or distorts.
Case for male-specific help: Programs designed for everyone have not closed gaps that have widened for decades; boys are less likely to seek help, to use counseling or to enroll in college support programs, so universal offers reach them less. Governments already run programs for women and girls in science, business and health, and a gap that ran the other way would not be met with calls for sex-neutral policy. Targeted efforts, such as recruiting men into teaching and nursing, men’s health checks, or mentoring for young men, can be designed and evaluated like any other policy, and supporters cite evidence that some programs, such as certain career academies and mentoring models, help boys much more than girls. As the American Institute for Boys and Men puts it, “Many of these challenges are structural and demand evidence-based policy solutions.”
Case for universal programs: The boys who are struggling are overwhelmingly poor, from neighborhoods with few good jobs, and often Black or Hispanic; affluent boys are doing well. Policies aimed at poverty, school quality, good jobs and mental health care would reach them without implying that sex is the problem, and without competing with programs for girls, who still face higher rates of depression, sexual violence and later earnings penalties. The sociologist Jessica Calarco argues that a focus on men makes people more skeptical of universal policies and fosters what she calls a culture of woman-blame. Targeting by sex can also be legally fraught under anti-discrimination law.

Question: Are schools built in ways that shortchange boys, or do boys fall behind for reasons outside school?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Schools fit boys poorly / Causes lie outside school
Why contested: Boys get lower grades, more suspensions and more ADHD diagnoses, and they read less well than girls in almost every country. Whether the cause is how schools teach and judge students, how boys develop, or the families and neighborhoods they come from is hard to separate with observational data.
Case that schools fit boys poorly: Boys develop self-control and verbal skills later than girls, yet schools increasingly demand both from age five, reward organization and compliance in grades, and rely on reading and writing in every subject. Studies have found that teachers grade boys lower than their test scores alone would predict, largely because of classroom behavior, and boys receive about 70 percent of out-of-school suspensions. Only about one elementary teacher in nine is a man, and some studies find students do better with a teacher of their own sex. A Danish study by Thomas Dee and Hans Henrik Sievertsen found that starting school a year later sharply reduced inattention and hyperactivity, which suggests that the timing of school, not only boys themselves, matters.
Case that causes lie outside school: Girls have outscored boys in US national reading tests since the 1970s, long before any recent change in schooling, and the gap appears in school systems that differ greatly, which points to development and to the wider world rather than to classroom design. Research by David Autor, David Figlio and colleagues on Florida siblings found that boys are more harmed than their sisters by family disadvantage, and Raj Chetty and colleagues found that boys from poor families, especially in high-poverty neighborhoods, are less likely to work as adults than girls from similarly poor families. On this view, what has changed is not school but the economy and family life around it, and gender gaps within the same income and racial group are small compared with gaps across them.

Question: Why have men pulled back from work: fewer good jobs, or changed incentives and choices?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Lost good jobs / Changed incentives and choices
Why contested: The share of prime-age American men in the labor force fell from about 97 percent in the 1950s to about 89 percent today, and the fall is concentrated among men without degrees. Economists agree on the facts and disagree about the mix of causes, which determines whether the remedy is economic policy or something else.
Case for lost good jobs: The decline tracks the disappearance of well-paid manufacturing and other blue-collar work. David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson found that places hit hardest by Chinese import competition saw men’s employment and earnings fall, and with them marriage rates, because fewer men were seen as reliable partners. Wages for men without degrees have stagnated for decades, so the reward for taking available work has fallen. Unions, which once raised pay for exactly these men, have shrunk. Rebuilding good non-college jobs through industrial policy, apprenticeships, wage subsidies or labor law would bring many men back.
Case for changed incentives and choices: Jobs have been available in many places where men have not returned to work, and the economists Ariel Binder and John Bound argue that the standard story of falling demand for less-educated men’s labor “does not reasonably explain the data” on its own (NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w25577). Mark Aguiar, Erik Hurst and colleagues estimated that better video games and recreational computing explain about half of the rise in leisure among young men since 2004. Disability benefits, opioid addiction, criminal records and the decline of marriage, which removes a reason to work, all play a part. The Heritage Foundation describes “a surge in idleness, fed by bad welfare incentives, foolish policy responses to the COVID-19 crisis, and a corrosive culture addicted to tech distractions.” Jobs alone will not fix a problem that is partly about purpose and family.

Question: Is traditional masculinity part of the problem or part of the remedy?
Tag: value
Poles: Loosen masculine norms / Renew manly virtues
Why contested: Everyone in the debate agrees that ideas about manhood shape men’s behavior. They disagree about whether the traditional script (strength, stoicism, self-reliance, providing and protecting) is what hurts men or what they have lost.
Case for loosening masculine norms: Men who hold rigid ideas about manhood are less likely to seek medical or psychological help, more likely to drink heavily, take risks and use violence, and more isolated. The American Psychological Association’s 2018 guidelines for work with boys and men say that “socialization for conforming to traditional masculinity ideology has been shown to limit males’ psychological development, constrain their behavior,” and harm mental and physical health. Equimundo’s 2025 survey found men who endorse restrictive masculine norms 6.27 times more likely to report suicidal thoughts. A wider idea of manhood, one that includes caregiving, emotional openness and friendship, would let men take up the growing jobs in health and education and share family life on equal terms.
Case for renewing manly virtues: Boys need a positive picture of what a good man is, and telling them their instincts are toxic leaves them to find one in the online manosphere. Courage, responsibility, protectiveness and the drive to provide are virtues that families and communities depend on; Senator Josh Hawley argues that the left wants to “define traditional masculinity as toxic” and that “We need the kind of men who make republics possible.” Many men find purpose in being needed, and the decline of marriage and of respected male roles has left them without it. Even some who favor male-specific policy, such as Richard Reeves, reject the language of toxic masculinity and call for a positive vision of masculinity rather than its removal.

Question: Does attention to boys and men threaten progress for women and girls?
Tag: value
Poles: Both can advance / Risk of backlash and neglect
Why contested: Public money, attention and political energy are limited, and the debate over men has become entangled with partisan politics and with online movements hostile to feminism. Whether naming men’s problems builds a broader coalition for fairness or feeds resentment is disputed.
Case that both can advance: Most Americans, 81 percent in Pew’s April 2024 survey, say women’s gains have not come at men’s expense, and most proposals for boys, such as better early education, mental health care and vocational paths, cost girls nothing. Ignoring young men’s problems hands the issue to those who do blame women; taking them seriously in a framework that also supports women’s progress, as Reeves, Newsom and England’s health strategy all say they do, is the best defense against that. Jennifer Siebel Newsom, California’s First Partner, put it this way: “Women and men face different challenges — but there are systemic barriers we can take on together.”
Case for the risk: Women still earn less, hold a small minority of top positions, do most unpaid care and face far higher rates of sexual violence. A narrative of male crisis can recast women’s progress as the cause of men’s troubles, and surveys find 52 percent of men across 29 countries agreeing that the promotion of women’s equality now discriminates against men. The sociologist Steven Roberts of Monash University argues that panics about a crisis of masculinity have recurred for 150 years and that the framing isolates gender from other structural factors. Those who share this worry add that money and goodwill spent on a male agenda are likely to come out of programs for women and girls.

Question: Who is worse off on mental health: men, who die more, or girls and women, who report more distress?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Men’s crisis is deeper / Girls’ distress is greater
Why contested: Men die by suicide about four times as often as women and die more often of overdoses, but teenage girls report persistent sadness and suicidal thoughts about twice as often as boys. Each side has a measure that favors its reading.
Case that men’s crisis is deeper: Death is the hardest outcome to argue with. About four in five American suicides are men, men die about five years younger, and overdoses, alcohol deaths and homicide all fall mostly on men. Men are less likely to recognize or report depression, so surveys of feelings understate their distress, and services built around talking about emotions reach them less. Governments from California to England now treat male suicide as a priority for that reason.
Case that girls’ distress is greater: On almost every measure of reported mental health, from depression and anxiety to self-harm and suicide attempts, girls and young women fare worse, and their reported distress rose through the 2010s. Suicide deaths reflect lethality of means, above all firearms, as much as distress, so the male excess is partly a story about guns. Framing mental health as a male crisis risks neglecting the larger number of girls and women who suffer and survive.

Question: Do schools, courts and governments treat males unfairly?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Systemic bias against males / Gaps reflect behavior and choices
Why contested: Males receive most school suspensions, most prison sentences and, historically, fewer custody awards, and there are many more public programs aimed at women and girls than at men. Whether this is bias or a response to real differences in behavior and need is hard to test, and the answer differs by institution.
Case for systemic bias: Studies of grading find teachers mark boys lower than their test scores predict; a study by the law professor Sonja Starr found men received federal sentences about 63 percent longer than women after controlling for the offense and criminal history; family courts long favored mothers in custody disputes. Public bodies fund commissions and programs for women and girls but rarely for boys and men. The National Coalition for Men, founded in 1977, describes itself as “committed to ending harmful discrimination and stereotypes against boys, men, their families and the women who love them.”
Case that gaps reflect behavior and choices: Boys misbehave in class more and men commit most violent crime, so higher rates of discipline and imprisonment largely follow differences in conduct rather than bias. Custody outcomes mostly reflect agreements between parents, and many states have moved toward equal parenting time. Men still hold most positions of power in politics, business and the courts that are said to discriminate against them, and programs for women were created to correct long-standing exclusion that has not fully ended.

Question: Should boys start school a year later than girls?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Delay boys’ start / Keep a common start
Why contested: Richard Reeves’s best-known and most controversial proposal is to start boys in school a year later by default, to match their slower development. The evidence on later school entry is mixed, and the cost and fairness of the idea are disputed.
Case for delaying boys’ start: Boys’ brains, especially the regions governing self-control and planning, mature later than girls’, so a classroom of same-age children is in practice a classroom of developmentally younger boys. Dee and Sievertsen’s Danish study found a one-year delay in school entry “dramatically reduces inattention/hyperactivity at age 7,” with effects that persisted at age 11. Families who delay kindergarten already tend to be white, affluent and raising boys, according to an NWEA analysis reported by The 74 (https://www.the74million.org/zero2eight/many-parents-talk-about-delaying-kindergarten-few-actually-do-it/), so making a later start the default, with free pre-kindergarten to fill the year, would extend an advantage the well-off already use.
Case for a common start: A later start means a year less in the labor market and a year more of childcare costs for families, which falls hardest on the poor. Studies of redshirting find mixed effects on long-run outcomes; the economist Diane Schanzenbach has called redshirting “generally not worth it,” and critics argue a default delay puts the cost on boys rather than fixing what schools ask of young children. Development varies more within each sex than between them, so a rule by sex would hold back many boys who are ready and push forward many girls who are not. Better kindergarten design, more play and later testing would help all children.

## Positions

Camp: Close boys’ and men’s gaps with targeted policy
Adherents: American Institute for Boys and Men (institution), founded by Richard Reeves, “Why We Exist,” https://aibm.org/why-we-exist/ ; Governor Gavin Newsom of California, Executive Order on boys and men, July 30, 2025, https://www.gov.ca.gov/2025/07/30/governor-newsom-issues-executive-order-to-support-young-men-and-boys-address-suicide-rates/ ; UK Department of Health and Social Care (institution), Men’s Health Strategy for England, November 2025, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-unveils-englands-first-ever-mens-health-strategy ; Virginia’s Commission on Boys and Men, created by the General Assembly in 2026, https://vaboysandmen.org/news/virginias-boys-and-men-commission-first-meeting/
Case: Too many boys and men are struggling at school, at work and in their families, and the gaps are large, persistent and growing in some places. They are not caused by feminism, and closing them does not mean rolling back women’s gains, which are among the great achievements of the past century; in Reeves’s words, as quoted in The Nation, feminism has upended patriarchy, “a specific social order that had the fatal flaw of being grossly unequal.” The causes are mostly structural: schools that ask young boys for self-control and verbal skills they develop later than girls, an economy that has stripped away the well-paid jobs men without degrees once relied on, and a culture that no longer offers a clear, positive role for men as fathers and contributors. Problems with specific causes need specific remedies: recruiting more men as teachers and counselors, giving boys more time and better vocational paths, bringing men into growing fields in health, education, administration and literacy as decades of effort brought women into science and engineering, designing men’s health services that men will actually use, and treating fathers as full parents in law and policy. A society that took girls’ underachievement seriously can do the same for boys without taking anything from girls.
Variation: The camp spans the political spectrum. Reeves and his institute stress education and work, including starting boys in school a year later; Newsom’s order centers on suicide prevention, mentoring, service and recruiting men into teaching; England’s strategy is a health program for men and boys; Virginia’s commission is bipartisan and advisory. Some in the camp put more weight on schooling and work, others on health or on fatherhood. They agree that sex-specific attention is warranted and that it is compatible with continued support for women.
Best evidence: Women now earn 58.5 percent of US bachelor’s degrees and lead men in tertiary attainment in nearly every OECD country; boys trail girls by 24 PISA points in reading; the employment rate of prime-age men without a four-year degree fell from 90 percent in 1979 to 81 percent in 2023 while that of women without a degree rose; men die by suicide at about four times the rate of women and live about five years less; and research by Autor, Figlio and colleagues and by Chetty and colleagues shows boys are more harmed than girls by the same family and neighborhood disadvantage, so a sex-blind policy aimed at poverty leaves a sex-specific gap.
Answer to objections: To those who say the problem is really class or race, this camp answers that both matter, and that the male gap appears in most racial and income groups and widens with disadvantage, so class policy alone will not close it. To those who fear a zero-sum contest with women, it answers that most proposals cost girls nothing, that 81 percent of Americans already reject the idea that women’s gains came at men’s expense, and that leaving young men’s problems unaddressed hands them to people who do blame women. To conservatives who want a return to traditional roles, it answers that the economy and women’s independence are not going back, and men need new roles that fit them, not old ones that no longer exist. To those who blame masculinity, it answers that telling boys their nature is toxic drives them away; a positive vision of masculinity works better than an attack on it.

Camp: Restore marriage, fatherhood and confident manhood
Adherents: The Heritage Foundation (institution), “Saving America by Saving the Family: A Foundation for the Next 250 Years,” Special Report No. 323, January 8, 2026, https://www.heritage.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/SR323.pdf ; Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri, National Conservatism Conference keynote, October 31, 2021, https://www.hawley.senate.gov/senator-hawley-delivers-national-conservatism-keynote-lefts-attack-men-america, and the book Manhood: The Masculine Virtues America Needs (2023) ; Aaron M. Renn, writing for the Institute for Family Studies, “Right Diagnosis, Wrong Prescription,” November 15, 2022, https://ifstudies.org/blog/right-diagnosis-wrong-prescription-richard-reeves-of-boys-and-men (the institute notes the views are the author’s)
Case: Men are floundering because the institutions that once gave them purpose, discipline and a place in the world have been allowed to decay: marriage, fatherhood, faith and a culture that honored the manly virtues of courage, responsibility and protection. Boys raised without fathers are more likely to fail in school, drift out of work and get in trouble, and the collapse of marriage among working-class Americans has removed one of the strongest reasons men had to work and settle down. A culture that treats traditional masculinity as toxic, and a welfare and benefits system that pays people not to work and penalizes marriage, have taught a generation of young men that they are not needed; screens, gaming and pornography fill the gap. As Hawley puts it, “the deconstruction of America begins with and depends on the deconstruction of American men.” The remedy is to rebuild the family: end marriage penalties in tax and welfare programs, require work, support fathers in custody and child support law, protect children from addictive technology, and call men to responsibility rather than telling them to become more like women.
Variation: Some in this camp, like Hawley, emphasize cultural renewal and a call to virtue; others, like Heritage’s analysts, emphasize policy incentives and argue that culture, not wages, explains the decline of marriage; still others, such as writers for the conservative economic journal American Compass, fault Reeves for asking men to become “junior partners” in a world shaped by women’s sensibilities, as The Nation summarized their review (https://www.thenation.com/article/society/richard-reeves-profile/), and stress rebuilding blue-collar work. They share the view that the traditional roles of husband, father and provider are worth restoring rather than replacing.
Best evidence: Heritage cites research that young men’s labor market hours fell as gaming and recreational computing improved (Aguiar, Bils, Charles and Hurst estimated a 1.5 to 3 percent decline in hours), that the share of men aged 16 to 24 who were employed fell by 10.9 points between 1999 and 2018; studies by Marianne Bertrand and Jessica Pan and by Autor and colleagues find that boys suffer more than girls from growing up in single-parent or disadvantaged families; and the American Institute for Boys and Men itself reports that only 67 percent of working-class men had married by 40 in 2022.
Answer to objections: To those who say this camp wants to roll back women’s gains, it answers that it wants strong families in which mothers and fathers both matter, and that marriage benefits women and children as well as men. To those who say the problem is economic, it answers that men’s earnings have not fallen enough to explain the collapse of marriage and work, and that the same jobs go unfilled while men stay idle. To those who blame masculine norms, it answers that the virtues they call toxic are what make men good fathers, workers and citizens, and that boys deprived of a positive model of manhood find a worse one online. To advocates of targeted programs, it answers that government cannot replace a father and that new programs will fail if the family keeps eroding.

Camp: Free men from restrictive masculine norms
Adherents: Equimundo: Center for Masculinities and Social Justice (institution), “State of American Men 2025,” June 23, 2025, https://www.equimundo.org/report-launch-state-of-american-men-2025-economic-anxiety-is-the-new-masculinity-crisis/ ; American Psychological Association (institution), “APA Guidelines for Psychological Practice with Boys and Men,” August 2018, https://www.apa.org/about/policy/boys-men-practice-guidelines.pdf ; Niobe Way, developmental psychologist at New York University, as quoted in The Nation, March 11, 2025, https://www.thenation.com/article/society/richard-reeves-profile/
Case: Men are hurting, and much of the hurt comes from the narrow script they are handed about what a man must be: never vulnerable, always in control, self-reliant to the point of isolation, a provider first and a person second. That script keeps men from seeing doctors and therapists, from keeping close friendships, from taking up caring work and from sharing fully in their children’s lives, and it makes some of them angry and violent when the script cannot be lived up to. It is economic anxiety that turns this script into a crisis: when men cannot earn what the script demands, they feel they have failed as men. The answer is neither to abandon men nor to restore the old script, but to widen it: raise boys to value emotional honesty, friendship and care; build mental health services tailored to men; engage fathers as caregivers; mentor young men; challenge the online influencers who sell them grievance; and pair this with economic policies that give every man security. Gender equality, on this view, is good for men too.
Variation: Some in this camp, such as the APA guidelines, speak in clinical terms about masculinity ideology and help-seeking; others, such as Equimundo, frame the work as part of a broader agenda of gender justice and violence prevention; Niobe Way, whose research follows boys’ close friendships through adolescence, argues that “boy culture,” not only economic structure, explains men’s struggles. Within the camp, there is debate over the term “toxic masculinity,” which some avoid as alienating.
Best evidence: Equimundo’s 2025 survey found that men who adhere to restrictive masculine norms are 6.27 times more likely to report suicidal thoughts, and men experiencing economic insecurity 16.3 times more likely; the APA’s review of decades of research links conformity to traditional masculinity ideology to worse mental and physical health; men are less likely than women to seek help for depression; and the share of men reporting no close friends rose from 3 percent in 1990 to 15 percent in 2021.
Answer to objections: To those who say this camp attacks men, it answers that it criticizes a set of expectations, not men, and that its whole aim is men’s health and happiness. To conservatives who say traditional virtues are what men need, it answers that courage, responsibility and protectiveness are welcome, but that the script also bundles in emotional suppression and dominance, which are what show up in suicide, isolation and violence statistics. To the targeted-policy camp, it answers that programs to recruit men into teaching or nursing will struggle unless the norms that keep men out of caring work change. To those who say the crisis framing is overblown, it answers that men’s suffering is real and that ignoring it leaves the field to the manosphere.

Camp: Address class and race inequality, not a male crisis
Adherents: American Association of University Women (institution), “Where the Girls Are: The Facts About Gender Equity in Education,” May 2008, reported by Education Week, https://edweek.org/ew/articles/2008/06/04/39aauw.h27.html ; Steven Roberts, professor of sociology at Monash University, “The crisis that always is but never was,” June 30, 2025, https://lens.monash.edu/2025/06/30/1387679/the-crisis-that-always-is-but-never-was-a-brief-reflection-on-150-years-of-panic-about-men-masculinity-and-social-change ; Jessica Calarco, sociologist at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, as quoted in The Nation, March 11, 2025, https://www.thenation.com/article/society/richard-reeves-profile/
Case: There is no general crisis of boys and men. There are poor boys, Black and Hispanic boys and working-class men who are struggling badly, alongside affluent boys who are doing very well, and the differences between those groups are far larger than the differences between boys and girls within them. Talk of a “crisis of masculinity” has recurred for 150 years, and each time it has isolated gender from the structural factors that actually drive outcomes: as Roberts writes, boys’ educational challenges “intersect with poverty, racism, and structural inequality more than gender alone.” Men as a group still earn more, hold most positions of power and own most wealth; girls report far more depression and face far more sexual violence. Framing the problem as one of men versus women invites the conclusion that women’s progress is to blame, a conclusion the manosphere is eager to draw. The better response is universal: reduce child poverty, improve schools in poor neighborhoods, expand mental health care, raise wages and rebuild unions, and support all young people who are struggling, boys included.
Variation: Some in this camp, such as the AAUW in 2008, focused on showing that girls’ gains had not come at boys’ expense and that gaps by race and income dwarf gaps by sex; others, such as Roberts, criticize the crisis narrative as a recurring moral panic tied to anti-feminist politics; others, such as Calarco, accept that some men are struggling but argue that a male-focused agenda weakens support for the universal programs that would help them.
Best evidence: Raj Chetty and colleagues found that the usual gender gap in employment is reversed for children who grow up in poor families, with boys from those families less likely to work as adults than their female peers, while among more affluent families men still work more than women; the AAUW reported that gender differences within racial and ethnic groups are small compared with differences across them; women’s full-time earnings remain 83 percent of men’s; a record 55 women run Fortune 500 companies, 11 percent of the list; and teenage girls report persistent sadness at nearly twice the rate of boys.
Answer to objections: To the targeted-policy camp’s point that a male gap exists within every class, it answers that the gap is small among the advantaged and large among the disadvantaged, which is a reason to fix disadvantage, and that universal programs can be designed to reach boys without singling them out. To those who point to male suicide and early death, it answers that these are real and deserve attention through health policy, gun safety and addiction treatment, none of which requires a narrative of gender crisis. To conservatives, it answers that the decline of marriage follows the decline of working-class economic security, not the other way round. To those who say ignoring men drives them to the manosphere, it answers that the manosphere feeds on grievance narratives, and a “boy crisis” is one.

Camp: End discrimination against boys and men
Adherents: National Coalition for Men (institution), Sean Kullman, “Does Governor Gavin Newsom care about boys and men or is it a political stunt?” August 6, 2025, https://ncfm.org/2025/08/news/discrimination-news/discrimination-against-men-news/ncfm-member-sean-kullman-does-governor-gavin-newsom-care-about-boys-and-men-or-is-it-a-political-stunt/ ; Christina Hoff Sommers, American Enterprise Institute, The War Against Boys (first published 2000; revised 2013), https://www.aei.org/research-products/book/the-war-against-boys/
Case: Boys and men are not only falling behind; they are being treated unfairly by the institutions that are supposed to serve them, and the unfairness is rarely named because the language of discrimination has been reserved for women. Schools discipline boys more harshly for ordinary boyish behavior and grade them below their measured ability; in the words of AEI’s description of Sommers’s book, “Under the guise of helping girls, many schools have adopted policies that penalize boys, often for simply being masculine.” Courts give men longer sentences for the same crimes and long favored mothers in custody; health systems fund women’s health offices and programs but neglect men’s; and governments run dozens of commissions and programs for women and girls but almost none for boys and men. Equal treatment means applying the same scrutiny to disadvantages that fall on males: commissions on the status of boys and men with the same standing as those for women, shared parenting as the default after divorce, equal attention to men’s health, and school practices that do not punish boys for being boys.
Variation: Some in this camp, like Sommers, write as equity feminists who support women’s legal equality and object to what they see as an ideological turn against boys; others, like the National Coalition for Men, work through litigation and advocacy on custody, sentencing, false accusations and sex-based programs. The Coalition’s writers welcome targeted efforts such as Newsom’s order but argue they fall short of a permanent commission.
Best evidence: Boys receive 70.5 percent of out-of-school suspensions and 72.8 percent of expulsions; Sonja Starr’s study of federal cases found men received sentences about 63 percent longer than women after controlling for the arrest offense and criminal history; men make up 93 percent of prisoners and 92 percent of workplace deaths; and many states and cities have permanent commissions on the status of women while Virginia’s, created in 2026, is described by its backers as the first permanent state commission for boys and men.
Answer to objections: To those who say the gaps reflect boys’ behavior and men’s crimes, this camp answers that sentencing studies that control for the offense and criminal history still find large gaps, and that some studies comparing anonymous and teacher-assigned marks find teachers favor girls, and that schools would not accept “girls misbehave” as an explanation for a disparity running the other way. To those who say men still hold power, it answers that the men at the top are not the boys at the bottom, and that a powerful few do not make the many privileged. To the class-and-race camp, it answers that, as Kullman puts it, “sex differences matter more than race” in many outcomes. To the masculinity-norms camp, it answers that blaming masculinity is itself a form of the prejudice it opposes.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others: are the gaps facing boys and men mainly the product of something about being male in today’s schools, economy and culture, so that they call for answers aimed at males (whether new opportunities, renewed traditional roles, broader masculine norms or fairer institutions), or are they mainly the product of poverty, race and the loss of working-class opportunity, which fall harder on boys but call for answers aimed at disadvantage itself?

What evidence could settle: How much of each gap remains between boys and girls, and between men and women, who grow up in the same families, neighborhoods and income levels, and why boys appear more sensitive than girls to the same disadvantage. Studies comparing brothers and sisters, such as Autor and colleagues’ in Florida, and children from families at the same income level, such as Chetty and colleagues’ national tax data, have begun to answer this, and they cut both ways: the male gap is largest among the poor, which supports the class reading, but it arises because boys fare worse than their sisters under the same conditions, which supports a sex-specific one. Randomized trials can test whether programs aimed at boys (later school entry, male teachers and mentors, career academies, men’s health outreach) do better than the same money spent on everyone, and whether changing masculine norms, encouraging marriage or changing discipline and sentencing practices alters outcomes. Better data on why men leave work, separating lost jobs from disability, addiction, criminal records and leisure, would show how far economic policy alone can go.

What no data can close: Whether sex should be a basis for public attention and spending at a time when women still earn less and hold fewer positions of power, or whether help should follow need regardless of sex; and what a good man is. Those who prize traditional roles see the decline of the husband, father and provider as a loss in itself; those who prize equality see the loosening of those roles as progress that men have not yet caught up with; those who prize individual fairness want each institution to treat each person alike, whatever the averages show. Even with the causes settled, people will weigh differently a boy’s lost chances against a girl’s, a dead man against a depressed girl, and a revival of old norms against the freedom that came from leaving them.
