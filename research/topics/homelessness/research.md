# Homelessness — research

## Overview

This topic covers what ends homelessness and what governments, especially cities, may do about the people living in it: whether to house people first without conditions (“Housing First”) or to make sobriety, treatment and accountability the route into housing (“treatment first”); whether and when cities may clear encampments and punish sleeping in public; how far addiction and serious mental illness cause homelessness or keep people in it; and whether the state should expand involuntary psychiatric care for people living on the street. The debate is centered on the United States, where the federal government has just reversed two decades of policy, with Finland, Canada and England as the comparisons each side reaches for. It deliberately excludes housing prices and housing supply as a general economic question (covered on the housing affordability page) and drug policy in general (covered on the drug policy page), except where each bears directly on who becomes homeless and what gets them housed. The question of whether homelessness is “a housing problem” at all appears here only as one camp’s answer to what ends it.

Why now: in August 2026 the federal break with Housing First reached its sharpest point and its first legal wall in the same week. On 7 August 2026 a federal district court in Rhode Island set aside the Department of Housing and Urban Development’s (HUD) fiscal 2026 funding notice for the Continuum of Care program, the main federal homelessness grant, which would have shrunk protected renewal funding for existing housing projects from about 90 percent to 60 percent and steered $1.3 billion toward transitional housing and services (National Association of Counties, 24 September 2026, https://www.naco.org/news/court-vacates-fy-2026-continuum-care-grant-changes; New York Attorney General, 7 August 2026, https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-wins-case-stopping-trump-administrations-latest-attempt). A week later, on 14 August 2026, HUD, the White House Office of National Drug Control Policy and the Department of Health and Human Services released a “Treatment First” toolkit for communities that names Housing First as a “fundamental failure point” and recommends substance-free living spaces and a five-phase path from crisis to “thriving” (NAHRO, 14 August 2026, https://www.nahro.org/news/hud-and-other-federal-agencies-launch-treatment-first-toolkit/; the toolkit itself, https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/Main/documents/Best-Practices-Toolkit.pdf). The same court had already ruled on 30 June 2026 that HUD’s earlier notices, which capped the share of grants for permanent supportive housing, violated the Administrative Procedure Act; the state attorneys general who sued said the cap would have cut that funding by about two-thirds and put roughly 170,000 people at risk of losing their homes (New York Attorney General, 30 June 2026, https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-wins-case-protecting-billions-dollars-states-fight). HUD has said it is considering an appeal. [Correction 2026-10-05: HUD did appeal; on September 16, 2026 a federal appeals court let HUD proceed with the new rules pending appeal, with applications due September 30 (National Association of Counties, https://www.naco.org/news/court-vacates-fy-2026-continuum-care-grant-changes).]

These moves follow President Trump’s July 2025 executive order “Ending Crime and Disorder on America’s Streets,” which directed agencies to end support for Housing First and harm reduction, to favor jurisdictions that enforce bans on urban camping and open drug use, and to make civil commitment of people with serious mental illness easier (White House, 24 July 2025, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/07/ending-crime-and-disorder-on-americas-streets/), and the June 2024 Supreme Court decision in City of Grants Pass v. Johnson, which held 6 to 3 that enforcing generally applicable public-camping laws is not cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment, ending a Ninth Circuit rule (Martin v. Boise) that had barred enforcement whenever a city’s homeless population exceeded its “practically available” shelter beds (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-175_19m2.pdf). The national numbers give every side something to point to. HUD’s count for January 2025, released on 29 May 2026, found 745,652 people homeless on a single night, 3 percent fewer than the 2024 record, but chronic homelessness reached a record 155,750 (HUD, 2025 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report, https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/2025-AHAR-Part-1.pdf). HUD Secretary Scott Turner read the data as proof that “the status quo of ‘housing first’ has failed” (HUD, 29 May 2026, https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037); Housing First’s defenders read the same report’s 56 percent fall in veteran homelessness since 2009 as proof of what the approach does when funded at scale.

The debate is live because it is no longer only an argument among researchers and service providers: the federal government, the largest funder, has switched sides, while Democratic governors such as Gavin Newsom in California and Kathy Hochul in New York have moved toward clearing encampments and widening involuntary treatment without abandoning Housing First. The participants are homelessness service providers and their national associations, which built the Housing First system; conservative and libertarian think tanks and recovery-oriented charities, which argue it neglected addiction; mayors and governors under pressure from residents over visible encampments; civil liberties and homeless rights lawyers, who are challenging camping bans and funding conditions in court; psychiatrists and families of people with severe mental illness, who are divided over involuntary care; housing economists and pro-building groups, who argue that the regional pattern of homelessness tracks rents and vacancy rates rather than drug use; and people who are or have been homeless.

Note: In January 2025 local counts found 745,652 people homeless on a single night in the US, 3 percent fewer than the 2024 record but 26 percent more than in 2013.
Context: The point-in-time count is a one-night snapshot taken in the last ten days of January by the 386 local Continuums of Care that cover virtually the whole country; it counts people in emergency shelters, transitional housing and places not meant for habitation, and excludes people doubled up with friends or family and people already in permanent supportive housing or rapid rehousing. HUD says unsheltered numbers are probably undercounts. The 2025 total fell by 25,828 people, mostly in emergency shelters; New York and Illinois, which had the largest increases in 2024 after a surge of migrant arrivals, had the largest decreases, and some local programs there attributed the fall partly to changes in federal immigration policy. Homelessness among single adults and chronic homelessness both reached record highs in 2025, while the number of people in families with children fell 11 percent. HUD’s own press release frames the figures differently from the report: it stresses that homelessness rose 27 percent from 2013 while Continuum of Care spending rose 111 percent and taxpayer-funded beds 151 percent (HUD, 29 May 2026, https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037). The count is also sensitive to method: in 2024, 22 local programs did not conduct an unsheltered count, and HUD notes that weather and local policy move the unsheltered figure from year to year.

Note: Of the 745,652 people counted homeless in January 2025, 266,320, or nearly 36 percent, were unsheltered.
Context: The rest stayed in emergency shelters (56 percent) or transitional housing (8 percent). Unsheltered homelessness fell 3 percent from 2024, when 274,224 people were counted outside, the highest number HUD has recorded, but it was 36 percent higher than in 2013. The share outside varies enormously by state, largely because of shelter policy and climate: in New York, whose courts and laws give a right to shelter, about 4 percent of the 145,560 people counted were unsheltered, while in California 115,583 of 181,934, or 64 percent, were (HUD, 2025 AHAR Part 1, Appendix A). Unsheltered homelessness, especially encampments, is what drives most public frustration and most of the legal fight over camping bans, but most homeless people in the US are indoors on any given night.

Note: About 22 in every 10,000 Americans were homeless on a single night in January 2025, but rates ranged from 4 per 10,000 in Mississippi to 73 in New York and the District of Columbia.
Context: The highest rates are on the West Coast and in the Northeast, in places with high rents, low rental vacancy and, in New York, Massachusetts and the District of Columbia, a legal right to shelter that brings more people indoors and into the count. Many of the lowest rates are in states with cheaper housing in the South and Appalachia, including some with high rates of drug overdose. This pattern is the core evidence for those who argue homelessness is mainly a housing market problem: the economist Gregg Colburn and data scientist Clayton Page Aldern argue that “cities with higher rents and lower rental-vacancy rates see higher per capita rates of homelessness” and that variation in poverty and mental illness does not explain the differences (Sightline Institute, 16 March 2022, https://www.sightline.org/2022/03/16/homelessness-is-a-housing-problem/). Those who emphasize addiction and policy respond that high-rate places also tend to have more permissive policies toward encampments and drug use and that migration of homeless people toward services and mild climates plays a role; a 2023 representative survey of homeless adults in California found that 90 percent lost their last housing in the state and 75 percent stayed in the same county (UCSF Benioff Homelessness and Housing Initiative, https://homelessness.ucsf.edu/sites/default/files/2025-12/CASPEH_Report_62023.pdf).

Note: The number of people counted as chronically homeless reached 155,750 in 2025, 81 percent more than in 2013, while beds in permanent supportive housing rose 44 percent over the same period to about 408,000.
Context: HUD defines a chronically homeless individual as someone with a disability who has been homeless continuously for a year or repeatedly over three years; about three in ten homeless individuals met that definition in 2025. Permanent supportive housing, the main Housing First program for this group, provides a long-term rent subsidy and voluntary services; people living in it are not counted as homeless. This pairing is read in opposite ways. Those who favor treatment first argue that more than a doubling of supportive housing beds since 2007 alongside a rise in chronic homelessness shows the model does not reduce homelessness at scale; HUD Secretary Scott Turner said the data show Housing First “has failed to meaningfully reduce homelessness” (HUD, 29 May 2026, https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037). Those who favor Housing First reply that the 400,000 people in those beds would otherwise be counted as homeless, that chronic homelessness fell 28 percent from 2007 to 2013 while supportive housing expanded, and that the later rise coincided with rents outpacing incomes and a fentanyl-driven overdose wave. HUD also revised its definition of chronic homelessness in a 2015 rule [unverified], so comparisons across that date are approximate. Thirteen percent of all beds in the national inventory are dedicated to veterans.

Label: Housing outcomes in the largest Housing First trial
Value: 62
Unit: percent of Housing First participants housed all of the time in the last six months of the trial
Viz: ranked
Series: Housing First, housed all of the time, 62 (highlight); Usual services, housed all of the time, 31; Housing First, housed none of the time, 16; Usual services, housed none of the time, 46
Data year: trial run 2009 to 2013, final report 2014
Source label: Mental Health Commission of Canada, National Final Report, Cross-Site At Home/Chez Soi Project (2014)
Source URL: https://www.mentalhealthcommission.ca/wp-content/uploads/drupal/mhcc_at_home_report_national_cross-site_eng_2_0.pdf
Note: In a randomized trial of 2,148 homeless adults with mental illness in five Canadian cities, 62 percent of those offered Housing First were stably housed in the final six months, against 31 percent of those given the usual services.
Context: At Home/Chez Soi enrolled homeless adults with serious mental illness in Vancouver, Winnipeg, Toronto, Montreal and Moncton and randomly assigned them either to Housing First (a rent subsidy for a scattered-site apartment plus a support team, with no requirement to accept treatment or be sober) or to whatever services already existed. The housing difference was large and consistent across sites. Other outcomes were more mixed, and both camps cite that: the report states that mental health and substance use problems “improved by a similar amount in both HF and TAU,” the treatment-as-usual group. Costs were partly offset: over two years, every $10 spent on Housing First saved an average of $9.60 in other services for high-need participants and $3.42 for moderate-need participants, and $21.72 for the costliest tenth. The trial is the strongest evidence that Housing First keeps people with mental illness housed; those who favor treatment first argue it says less about people whose main problem is active addiction, and that keeping people housed is not the same as helping them recover.

Label: Effect of Housing First in a federal systematic review
Value: 88
Unit: percent greater reduction in homelessness for Housing First programs than for treatment-first comparison programs (median across studies)
Viz: bar
Data year: review of studies published through 2019; finding issued June 2019
Source label: Community Preventive Services Task Force (CDC-supported), Housing First Programs finding
Source URL: https://www.thecommunityguide.org/findings/social-determinants-health-housing-first-programs.html
Note: A review of 26 studies for a CDC-supported task force found that Housing First programs cut homelessness by a median 88 percent more, and improved housing stability by 41 percent more, than treatment-first programs.
Context: The Community Preventive Services Task Force recommends permanent supportive housing with Housing First for people experiencing homelessness who have a disabling condition. The same review found reductions in hospitalization and emergency department use, and concluded that economic benefits exceed costs in the United States, but found “no apparent differences in physical health, mental health, or substance abuse” between Housing First and comparison groups for the general homeless population. A 2018 National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine committee likewise found no substantial published evidence that permanent supportive housing improves health, while finding that it keeps people housed (https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25133/permanent-supportive-housing-evaluating-the-evidence-for-improving-health-outcomes). Housing First supporters read these findings as showing the model does what it promises, ending homelessness for the person housed; those who favor treatment first read them as showing that housing alone does not treat addiction.

Note: In a representative survey of homeless adults in California, 65 percent had at some point used hard drugs at least three times a week and 35 percent were doing so currently; most who had ever used regularly started before they first became homeless.

These figures are used by both sides: the federal July 2025 executive order cites the lifetime figure (“nearly two-thirds of homeless individuals report having regularly used hard drugs”) to argue that addiction must be treated first, while the study’s authors stress that the same people overwhelmingly named housing costs as the barrier to getting back inside (see next stat).

These are participants’ own beliefs about what would have helped, not measured effects of payments; those who emphasize addiction and mental illness argue that a self-report survey cannot separate the cost of housing from the reasons people could not keep it, while those who emphasize housing costs point to the low incomes and short notice as the proximate cause.

Value: 9
Unit: percent increase in homelessness associated with a $100 increase in median monthly rent
Viz: bar
Data year: analysis of point-in-time count data, report dated July 2020 and released August 2020
Source label: US Government Accountability Office, GAO-20-433, Homelessness: Better HUD Oversight of Data Collection Could Improve Estimates of Homeless Population
Source URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-433
Note: The Government Accountability Office found that a $100 rise in median monthly rent was associated with a 9 percent increase in homelessness across the areas it studied.
Context: GAO’s econometric analysis controlled for wages, unemployment, poverty and other demographic and economic characteristics, but it is a statistical association, not an experiment. Those who emphasize individual factors do not deny that rents matter; they argue that housing costs explain how many people are at risk in a region while addiction and mental illness explain which people end up and stay on the street.

Data year: January 2025
Source label: HUD, 2025 AHAR Part 1, Exhibit A.6
Source URL: https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/2025-AHAR-Part-1.pdf
Note: Veteran homelessness has fallen 56 percent since 2009, when HUD first reported it, to about 32,500 people in January 2025.
Context: The decline coincided with a large federal push, including the HUD–Veterans Affairs Supportive Housing (HUD-VASH) voucher program, which pairs rent subsidies with VA case management on Housing First principles, and Supportive Services for Veteran Families, which funds prevention and rapid rehousing. Those who favor treatment first cite a May 2026 national study of 60,888 HUD-VASH veterans, matched to similar homeless veterans, that found their overdose death risk was about twice as high at every point over two years and that an early drop in all-cause mortality was not sustained (Tsai, Peltzman and Beydoun, American Journal of Public Health, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41679266/). The study is observational, so its authors and others debate whether housing raises overdose risk, for example through using alone, or whether the comparison groups differ in ways matching did not capture. The federal Treatment First toolkit cites it as evidence that Housing First lacks sufficient addiction treatment.

## Questions

1. Is homelessness driven mainly by the cost of housing or by addiction and mental illness?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Housing market / Personal crises
Why contested: Both are plainly involved, so the argument is about weight and about which explains what. Most homeless adults have had serious mental health or substance use problems at some point, yet regional homelessness rates track rents and vacancy rates far more closely than they track rates of addiction or mental illness. Each side has data that answers a different question: who becomes homeless, or how many do.
Best case for housing market: Places with the most addiction and mental illness are not the places with the most homelessness. West Virginia and Mississippi have some of the lowest homelessness rates in the country, while New York, Oregon, Hawaii and California have the highest, and the difference lines up with rents and vacancy. The Government Accountability Office found a $100 rise in median rent associated with a 9 percent rise in homelessness after controlling for poverty and unemployment. In California’s representative survey, the median homeless adult had $960 a month in income before losing housing, and 89 percent named cost as the barrier to getting back in. Addiction and illness decide who loses the game of musical chairs; the number of chairs decides how many lose.
Best case for personal crises: Most people with low incomes in expensive cities never become homeless; the ones who do, and especially the ones who stay on the street for years, overwhelmingly have serious addiction or mental illness. In California, 65 percent of homeless adults had used hard drugs regularly, most of them before they first became homeless, and two-thirds had current mental health symptoms; chronic homelessness, the group most visible on the street, hit a record in 2025 even as supportive housing beds grew. Cheap housing does not keep someone housed through psychosis or methamphetamine addiction, and the fentanyl era changed who is outside and how they die. Treating the individual crisis is the precondition for any housing to last.

2. Does Housing First reduce homelessness across a whole city or country, or only keep the people it houses off the street?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Scales up / Stays small
Why contested: Randomized trials show Housing First keeps far more participants housed than usual services, but the national count of chronic homelessness rose while supportive housing beds more than doubled. The disagreement is whether that gap reflects a failure of the model or a failure to fund it at the scale of the inflow into homelessness.
Best case for scales up: Where Housing First was funded at the scale of the problem, the problem shrank. Veteran homelessness fell 56 percent after the VA and HUD built a housing voucher program sized to the need; Houston cut its count by more than 60 percent with a single coordinated system; Finland cut long-term homelessness by about 70 percent after 2008. The roughly 400,000 people in supportive housing would otherwise be in the count. National numbers rose because rents rose and pandemic aid ended, not because people who were housed fell back out.
Best case for stays small: The test of a policy is whether the problem gets smaller, and after two decades as federal policy it has not: chronic homelessness rose 81 percent from 2013 to 2025 while Continuum of Care spending more than doubled. Permanent supportive housing is expensive and slow to build (nearly $600,000 a unit in Los Angeles in 2021), so it houses a few thousand while tens of thousands wait outside. Retention rates measure whether people stay in a subsidized unit, not whether they recover or become self-sufficient, and a system that prioritizes permanent housing for the few leaves too little for shelter, treatment and recovery for the many.

3. Should housing be offered without requiring sobriety or treatment?
Tag: value
Poles: Housing unconditionally / Housing with expectations
Why contested: It turns partly on evidence (which approach keeps people alive and housed) but mostly on what a home funded by the public is: a precondition for recovery that should not be withheld, or a support the public may tie to effort toward recovery. Trials comparing the two exist but mostly enrolled people with mental illness rather than people whose main problem is active addiction.
Best case for housing unconditionally: People cannot get sober on a sidewalk. Requiring sobriety first screens out exactly the people who most need housing, as Finland found when its “staircase” system stalled in the 2000s with a hard core of long-term homeless people who could not climb it. In trials, Housing First participants stay housed at about twice the rate of those in usual services, and their substance use improves about as much as under treatment-first programs, so conditioning housing buys no extra recovery while costing many people their housing. Treatment works best when chosen, and a stable home is what makes the choice possible.
Best case for housing with expectations: Addiction is a disease that thrives on low expectations, and a building full of people actively using fentanyl is not a recovery environment; a national study of 60,888 veterans found those placed in supportive housing were twice as likely to die of an overdose as matched homeless veterans. Recovery housing, contingency management and structured programs build the accountability and community that addiction erodes. Taxpayers may reasonably ask that publicly funded housing come with an expectation of working toward health and self-sufficiency, as most other public benefits do, and many people in recovery say a requirement is what got them sober.

4. May cities punish people for sleeping or camping in public places?
Tag: value
Poles: Public order / Right to rest
Why contested: The Supreme Court settled in 2024 that the Constitution generally allows it, so the question is now whether cities should, and under what conditions. It sets the interests of residents, businesses and other homeless people in safe, usable public space against the liberty and survival of people who may have nowhere better to go.
Best case for public order: Sidewalks, parks and transit belong to everyone, and encampments bring fires, violence, open drug markets and disease that fall hardest on the people living in them. Without the ability to say “not here,” outreach workers have no leverage to get people to accept shelter or treatment, and a standing camp becomes a place where people die. Clear rules, paired with real offers of shelter, are what moved people indoors in Houston and San Francisco; tolerance of permanent camps is not compassion.
Best case for right to rest: Sleeping is not a choice, and fining or jailing people for doing it outdoors when they have no home gives them a criminal record, takes their documents and medications in sweeps, and makes housing harder to reach. Shelter is often unavailable, unsafe, or barred to couples, pets or people with jobs at night; a modeling study projected that repeated displacement of people who inject drugs could contribute to 15 to 25 percent of their deaths over ten years. Enforcement moves people a few blocks rather than indoors, at high cost, and spends money that could house them.

5. Should the state be able to commit people with severe mental illness or addiction to treatment against their will?
Tag: value
Poles: Compel care / Protect liberty
Why contested: A small group of people with psychosis or severe addiction, often refusing help, account for much of the visible disorder and many of the deaths on the street. Widening involuntary treatment pits a duty to rescue people who cannot recognize their own illness against hard-won protections against abuse in psychiatric institutions, and depends on psychiatric beds that largely no longer exist.
Best case for compel care: Some people are too ill to know they are ill; leaving them to die “with their rights on,” in the phrase used by many psychiatrists and families, is neglect rather than respect for autonomy. The US has about 11 state psychiatric beds per 100,000 people, down from 339 in 1950, so many people with schizophrenia now cycle among the street, emergency rooms and jail. New York, California and other states have widened the legal standard to include people who cannot meet their basic needs because of mental illness, and courts can order treatment that restores the capacity to choose.
Best case for protect liberty: Forced treatment has a long record of abuse and little evidence of lasting benefit once people are released, and short holds tend to end in discharge back to the street with nothing changed. People avoid services they fear will lock them up. The scarce resources are voluntary treatment beds, supportive housing and outreach, which many people accept when they are actually offered; civil commitment without housing to return to is a revolving door, and expanding it risks sweeping up people whose main problem is poverty.

6. Do encampment clearances get people indoors, or just move them?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Moves people indoors / Moves people around
Why contested: Cities report falling encampment and unsheltered counts after clearance campaigns, while researchers tracking individuals find most people displaced stay nearby, lose belongings and lose contact with caseworkers. The answer may depend heavily on whether a real shelter or housing offer comes with the clearance.
Best case for moves people indoors: When clearances come with a concrete offer, many people accept: San Francisco reports shelter placements up 40 percent and unsheltered homelessness down 22 percent after combining encampment resolution with new treatment-focused beds, and Houston’s decommissioning moved most residents of closed camps into housing. Persistent camps entrench addiction and victimization, and ending them interrupts that. The aim is not punishment but making the street a less viable option than the help on offer.
Best case for moves people around: The evidence on what happens to individuals is discouraging: an August 2026 study tracking people after clearances found they “do not travel far” and are unlikely to move indoors, while losing contact with service providers. Counts fall partly because people scatter and become harder to find, not because they are housed. Sweeps destroy IDs, medications and tents that must be replaced, and they disrupt treatment for people who inject drugs, raising overdose risk.

7. Should limited money go to permanent housing or to faster, cheaper shelter and treatment beds?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Permanent homes / Beds now
Why contested: Every dollar spent on a $600,000 apartment cannot buy shelter beds, and every dollar spent on shelter does not end anyone’s homelessness. The answer depends on facts that are measurable (how many shelter stays end in housing, how many people return to the street) and on how one weighs the person housed for good against the many still outside.
Best case for permanent homes: Shelter manages homelessness without ending it; people cycle through for years at high cost, while permanent housing with support ends the episode, and most people stay housed. Rent vouchers and conversions of existing buildings can house people far more cheaply than new construction. Spending on shelter without enough permanent housing to move people into simply fills the shelters, as New York’s right-to-shelter system shows.
Best case for beds now: With more than 260,000 people sleeping outside, getting people inside tonight saves lives, and interim shelter, tiny homes, sanctioned sites and treatment beds can be opened in months rather than years. Permanent supportive housing has become so costly and slow that it cannot keep pace with inflow. A system that offers everyone a safe bed immediately, and moves them to permanent housing as they stabilize, reaches far more people than one that houses a few perfectly.

8. Should the federal government set the approach through funding conditions, or leave it to cities and states?
Tag: value
Poles: National direction / Local choice
Why contested: For two decades federal grant rules required Housing First; the current administration is using the same lever to require the opposite, and courts have so far blocked it on procedural grounds. Both sides have favored federal conditions when they controlled them.
Best case for national direction: Federal money comes with responsibility for results, and the national government sees what local programs cannot: which approaches reduce homelessness across hundreds of communities. Without common standards, cities chase homeless people across borders with bans or buses, and evidence-based practice spreads unevenly. Whichever approach is right, it should be applied consistently where federal dollars go.
Best case for local choice: Homelessness looks different in rural Oregon, Houston and Manhattan, and the people closest to it should choose the mix of housing, shelter, treatment and enforcement. Abrupt federal reversals destabilize programs that took years to build and threaten the housing of people already in them, as the 2025 and 2026 funding notices did. Cities and states should be free to experiment, and the evidence from those experiments is how the field learns.

## Positions

Ordering note for the editor: the first two camps are the poles of the federal fight; the third, the hybrid, is arguably the view most US governors, big-city mayors and the plurality of the public now hold in practice, and could reasonably be placed second. The fourth rejects the program-choice question as posed. A rights-based wing (National Homelessness Law Center, ACLU) that opposes all criminalization of homelessness is folded into the first camp, since it shares every core proposition with it; it could be split out if the editor wants a camp defined by opposition to enforcement.

### 1. Offer housing first, without preconditions

Adherents:
- National Alliance to End Homelessness (institution): defines Housing First as an approach that “does not require people experiencing homelessness to address all of their problems” before housing and “does not mandate participation in services.” https://endhomelessness.org/resource/housing-first/
- National Low Income Housing Coalition (institution): its brief “The Evidence Is Clear: Housing First Works” calls Housing First “the most effective approach to ending homelessness” and treatment-first programs “one-size-fits-all programs that create high barriers to housing.” https://nlihc.org/sites/default/files/Housing-First-Evidence.pdf
- Sam Tsemberis, psychologist and founder of Pathways to Housing, who developed the model in New York in the 1990s: “The only way to end homelessness is to provide housing” (Mother Jones, August 2025). https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2025/08/homelessness-trump-washington-dc-housing-first-sam-tsemberis/
- Rights-based wing: National Homelessness Law Center, which counts and challenges laws that penalize sleeping outside. https://homelesslaw.org/06252026statement/

Case: Homelessness is, at bottom, the lack of a home, and the fastest way to end it for any person is to give them one, with a lease, a rent subsidy and support services they can choose. Requiring people to get sober, take medication or prove “housing readiness” first screens out the very people who are most vulnerable and most expensive to leave on the street, and it asks people to do the hardest thing in their lives while sleeping in a doorway. A stable home is the platform from which people can address addiction, mental illness and work, not a reward for having done so. Housing First does not reject treatment; it offers it without making it the price of a roof. Punishing people for sleeping outside when they have nowhere to go only adds criminal records, fines and lost documents that make housing harder to reach.

Best evidence: Randomized trials, above all Canada’s At Home/Chez Soi study of 2,148 people, in which 62 percent of Housing First participants were stably housed in the final six months against 31 percent with usual services, with substantial cost offsets for the highest-need participants. A CDC-supported systematic review of 26 studies found Housing First reduced homelessness by a median 88 percent more than treatment-first programs. Real-world results at scale: a 56 percent fall in veteran homelessness since 2009 under HUD-VASH; more than 60 percent in Houston since 2011; about 70 percent in Finnish long-term homelessness from 2008 to 2024.

Answer to the strongest objection (homelessness rose nationally under Housing First, and housing alone does not treat addiction): The national rise reflects rents outpacing incomes and the end of pandemic aid, not people falling out of Housing First units; the roughly 400,000 people in supportive housing would otherwise be on the streets or in shelters, and the federal government never funded enough units or vouchers to meet the need. Housing First was never claimed to cure addiction by itself; trials show substance use improves about as much as under treatment-first programs, so conditioning housing adds homelessness without adding recovery. Overdose risk in housing calls for more on-site services and naloxone, not for evicting people back to the street, where death rates are higher still.

Variation inside the camp: some emphasize permanent supportive housing for chronically homeless people with disabilities; others rapid rehousing and prevention for families and the newly homeless (short-term rent help, one-time cash, eviction defense). The rights-based wing goes further in opposing encampment clearances outright; most service providers in the camp accept clearances only where every resident is offered housing.

### 2. Require recovery and end street camping

Adherents:
- US Department of Housing and Urban Development under Secretary Scott Turner (institution), with the Office of National Drug Control Policy and HHS: “The data is clear that the status quo of ‘housing first’ has failed to meaningfully reduce homelessness” (29 May 2026); its August 2026 toolkit calls for a “Treatment First” model with substance-free living spaces and accountability for recovery outcomes. https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037 and https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/Main/documents/Best-Practices-Toolkit.pdf
- Cicero Institute (institution): model legislation banning public camping, which it calls “a compassionate way to redirect unsheltered homeless to existing shelters that are safer and provide resources such as substance abuse treatment.” https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/issue-brief-public-camping-ban/
- Michele Steeb, senior fellow at the Discovery Institute’s Fix Homelessness initiative: Treatment First is “a policy change many of us who have spent time on the frontlines serving the homeless have been arguing for.” https://fixhomelessness.org/2026/hud-announces-treatment-first-toolkit-putting-harm-reduction-on-notice/
- President Trump’s July 2025 executive order, which ends federal support for Housing First and harm reduction and directs grants toward jurisdictions that enforce camping and drug-use bans. https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/07/ending-crime-and-disorder-on-americas-streets/

Case: The people living long-term on America’s streets are not mainly people priced out of apartments; most are struggling with addiction, serious mental illness or both, and the fentanyl and methamphetamine era has made that deadlier than ever. Handing someone in active addiction the keys to an apartment, with services optional, too often means they use alone behind a closed door, and the federal system built on that idea for twenty years has produced record chronic homelessness while spending more every year. What works is a continuum: get people off the street into safe shelter or treatment, require and reward progress toward sobriety and work, and offer recovery housing where people live among others committed to the same goal. Accountability is a form of respect. Allowing permanent encampments is not compassion; it abandons people to drugs, violence and exposure, and it takes public space from everyone else, including the poor who depend on it most.

Best evidence: HUD’s own counts: from 2013 to 2025, chronic homelessness rose 81 percent and unsheltered homelessness 36 percent while Continuum of Care spending rose 111 percent and permanent supportive housing beds 44 percent. A 2026 national study of 60,888 veterans in HUD-VASH found their overdose death risk twice that of matched homeless veterans across two years. Reviews favorable to Housing First, including the CDC-supported task force and a 2018 National Academies committee, found no improvement in substance use or measurable health outcomes compared with alternatives. In California, 65 percent of homeless adults have used hard drugs regularly, most starting before they became homeless. A 2009 review in the Milbank Quarterly by Stefan Kertesz and colleagues cautioned against generalizing Housing First trials, mostly of people with mental illness, to people with active addiction (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2881444/).

Answer to the strongest objection (randomized trials show Housing First keeps people housed far better than treatment-first programs): Housing retention measures whether someone stays in a subsidized unit, not whether they are sober, healthy, working or alive, and the trials’ comparison groups mostly received fragmented usual services, not well-run recovery programs. Retention is easy to achieve if staying in housing asks nothing of the tenant. The relevant test is whether a community’s homelessness and overdose deaths go down, and nationally they did not under Housing First. Treatment First does not mean leaving people on the street until they are sober: it means shelter, treatment and recovery housing now, with permanent housing as people stabilize.

Variation inside the camp: some emphasize treatment and recovery housing and are wary of criminal penalties beyond the threat needed to move people indoors (many recovery providers); others emphasize enforcement of camping and drug laws and expansion of civil commitment (the executive order, some state legislatures). Some, including Cicero, favor sanctioned campsites as the alternative to the street; others favor faith-based or campus-style shelters.

### 3. Offer shelter and treatment, then enforce

Adherents:
- Office of Governor Gavin Newsom, California (institution): its May 2025 model ordinance prohibits persistent camping in one place and blocking sidewalks while requiring notice and “reasonable attempts to offer shelter before clearing sites”; Newsom: “There’s nothing compassionate about letting people die on the streets.” The state also kept funding Housing First and in September 2026 made “Housing First-compliant recovery housing” eligible for state funds. https://www.gov.ca.gov/2025/05/12/governor-newsom-releases-state-model-for-cities-and-counties-to-immediately-address-encampments-with-urgency-and-dignity/ and https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/29/governor-newsom-signs-housing-legislation-cementing-californias-record-progress-to-reduce-unsheltered-homelessness-and-build-more-homes/
- Daniel Lurie, mayor of San Francisco: his “Breaking the Cycle” plan combines encampment resolution, more than 600 new treatment-focused beds and, as an added voluntary option, drug-free permanent supportive housing in which residents keep their lease protections if they relapse (City of San Francisco, 29 July 2026). https://www.sf.gov/news-mayor-lurie-signs-legislation-to-expand-drug-free-permanent-supportive-housing-building-on-progress-of-breaking-the-cycle-plan
- Kathy Hochul, governor of New York, whose fiscal 2026 budget widened the involuntary commitment standard to cover people whose mental illness leaves them unable to meet basic needs, in a state that guarantees a right to shelter (New York Governor, 9 May 2025). https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-legislation-improve-mental-health-care-and-strengthen-treatment-serious

Case: The choice between Housing First and treatment first is a false one, and so is the choice between compassion and order. Permanent housing without sobriety requirements works for many people and should stay; but it cannot be built fast enough for everyone outside tonight, and some people, especially those with untreated psychosis or severe addiction, will not come inside without a push. So cities should offer every person a real option (a shelter bed, a treatment bed, a tiny home, a hotel room or an apartment), and once that offer is made, they should be able to say that living on the sidewalk is not an option, and they should be able to compel treatment for the small number too ill to accept it. Encampments are dangerous for the people in them and corrosive to public support for any homelessness spending; clearing them with offers of help is how to keep that support.

Best evidence: San Francisco reports unsheltered homelessness down 22 percent to its lowest level in 15 years, and shelter placements up 40 percent, after combining encampment resolution with expanded treatment and shelter beds; California reports the largest numerical drop in unsheltered homelessness of any state from 2024 to 2025 (8,391 people). Houston, the most cited Housing First success, also enforces city ordinances against encampments as it moves residents into housing. The 2025 AP-NORC poll found more Americans favor removing encampments (43 percent) than oppose it (24 percent), while majorities also see housing costs as a major cause.

Answer to the strongest objection (from one side, enforcement just moves people around and raises deaths; from the other, keeping Housing First keeps a failed model): Enforcement without a real offer does move people around; that is why this camp ties enforcement to an offer of shelter or housing first, and why it is funding beds at the same time. Studies showing harm from sweeps mostly describe clearances with no place to go. To the treatment-first critique, the camp answers that unconditional permanent housing demonstrably keeps most people housed, so the fix is to add recovery-oriented options and shelter capacity, not to cut the housing that is working.

Variation inside the camp: some emphasize shelter capacity and right-to-shelter guarantees (New York); others emphasize encampment enforcement once shelter exists (California’s model ordinance); others emphasize expanded involuntary care and court-ordered treatment (New York’s 2025 law, California’s CARE Court).

### 4. Build enough homes to end the shortage

Adherents:
- YIMBY Action (institution): “We fight for more housing because we want to reduce poverty, end homelessness,” arguing that exclusionary zoning and slow permitting cause the shortage that “makes the homelessness crisis worse, as more people are priced out of their homes.” https://yimbyaction.org/solutions/
- Gregg Colburn, University of Washington, and Clayton Page Aldern, authors of Homelessness Is a Housing Problem (University of California Press, 2022): housing market conditions “offer a far more convincing account” of regional variation in homelessness than mental illness, drug use or poverty; they call for zoning reform and more housing of every kind. https://ucpress.edu/books/homelessness-is-a-housing-problem/paper and https://www.sightline.org/2022/03/16/homelessness-is-a-housing-problem/

Case: The debate between Housing First and treatment first, and the fight over encampments, is a fight over how to manage the casualties of a housing shortage. Addiction and mental illness determine which people lose their housing when there is not enough of it, but the shortage determines how many lose it; that is why West Virginia, with severe addiction, has a fraction of California’s homelessness rate. Every program, whether Housing First, treatment first or shelter, needs available apartments to move people into, and in high-cost cities with near-zero vacancy, new supportive housing costs $600,000 a unit and takes years. The lasting fix is to legalize and build far more housing of every type, market-rate and subsidized, so rents fall and vacancy rises; then the services debate becomes manageable.

Best evidence: State homelessness rates track housing costs: HUD’s 2025 count shows rates of 73 per 10,000 in New York and 46 in California against 4 in Mississippi and 11 in West Virginia. GAO found a $100 increase in median rent associated with a 9 percent increase in homelessness after controlling for poverty and unemployment. Colburn and Aldern found that measures of drug use, mental illness, poverty, weather and welfare generosity do not explain regional variation, while rents and vacancy do. Houston’s success came in a region with no conventional zoning and low rents.

Answer to the strongest objection (new supply takes decades to lower rents and does nothing for the person on the street tonight, who has needs beyond a cheap apartment): Housing supply is not a substitute for services, but it is what makes services work; a voucher is useless without a vacant unit to use it in, and Houston and Finland both had housing to place people in. Zoning reform and faster permitting cost governments little and start paying off within a few years, and the alternative, subsidizing a handful of very expensive units at a time, has demonstrably failed to keep up.

Variation inside the camp: some emphasize deregulating private construction (market-oriented economists and many YIMBY groups); others emphasize public and social housing on the Finnish or Viennese model; most also support vouchers and Housing First for those with the highest needs.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others: for the people who stay on the street longest, is the lack of a home the main thing keeping them there, so that housing must come first and the rest can follow, or is untreated addiction and mental illness the main thing, so that housing without treatment will not hold and treatment, by persuasion or by requirement, must come first?

What evidence could settle: Much of this is measurable, and the existing evidence answers only part of it. Randomized trials settle that Housing First keeps people with serious mental illness housed better than usual services, but there are few head-to-head trials of Housing First against well-run recovery housing for people whose main problem is active addiction, and the 2026 veterans study linking supported housing to higher overdose deaths is observational. Evidence could also settle what happens to people after encampment clearances when a real offer of shelter or housing comes with them, whether involuntary treatment produces lasting gains once people are released, how much of a region’s homelessness rate is explained by rents and vacancy versus drug use and mental illness, and whether communities that fund housing at the scale of need (as with veterans, Houston and Finland) keep their numbers down when funding or benefits are cut. Better counts would help too: the one-night count misses many unsheltered people and does not follow individuals over time.

What is a value choice: Even with all the evidence in, people would still disagree about what a home paid for by the public is: something owed to anyone who lacks one, as a precondition for any other progress, or a support the public may tie to effort toward recovery and work. They would still weigh differently the liberty of a person who refuses help against the duty to rescue someone too ill to choose, and the right of a person with nowhere to go to sleep in a public place against the claims of neighbors, businesses and other homeless people on that space. And they would still differ on whether success means fewer people on the street, fewer people homeless, fewer people dying, or more people sober and self-sufficient, goals that every camp shares but ranks differently.
