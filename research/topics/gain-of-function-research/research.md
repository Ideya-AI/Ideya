# Engineered Pandemics — research

## Overview

A pandemic pathogen could in principle come from a laboratory rather than from nature: from research that deliberately makes a pathogen more transmissible or more harmful, from an accident with a dangerous pathogen already under study, or from someone ordering synthetic DNA and assembling an agent from it. This topic covers the governance of those routes — which experiments governments will fund or permit, who reviews them, how high-containment laboratories are overseen, and whether the companies that sell custom DNA and the benchtop machines that make it should be required to screen what they make and who they sell it to. It deliberately leaves aside the forensic question of where SARS-CoV-2 came from, except where a camp’s argument depends on it; the general regulation of frontier AI, which has its own topic; antimicrobial resistance, which has its own topic; and pandemic preparedness as a whole, which appears here only where it is offered as an alternative to restriction.

Why now: on 28 July 2026 the US government released the United States Government Policy for Stopping High-Risk Life Sciences Research, approved by the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy on 20 July 2026 (policy text, https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/USG-Policy-for-Stopping-High-Risk-Life-Sciences-Research_July-2026.pdf). It prohibits federal funding for “dangerous gain-of-function” research outright, rather than subjecting it to case-by-case review, and it replaces the 2024 dual-use research policy, the 2012 and 2014 dual-use policies and the 2017 potential-pandemic-pathogen framework. The policy implements Executive Order 14292, signed 5 May 2025, which paused federal funding for such work and ended it for work in “countries of concern” (White House fact sheet via the American Presidency Project, https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-14292-improving-the-safety-and-security-biological-research). Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said the government was “ending federal support for dangerous gain-of-function research and replacing weak oversight with clear, enforceable safeguards” (HHS joint statement, 28 July 2026, https://www.hhs.gov/press-room/stopping-high-risk-life-sciences-research.html). The next day the American Society for Microbiology said the policy’s categories were defined so broadly that, if enforced as written, they “will impede our ability as a nation to address infectious disease threats” (https://asm.org/press-releases/2026/july/asm-statement-on-usg-high-risk-life-sciences-resea).

The debate is live on two tracks at once. On the research track, more than 40 National Institutes of Health awards were suspended under the 2025 pause, including at least 22 tuberculosis awards, and a further 172 were flagged for review — numbers that supporters read as evidence the old system was missing things and opponents read as evidence the new definitions sweep in ordinary work. On the synthesis track, a 2026 review of the law found that no country legally requires DNA synthesis companies to screen orders; the United States and the United Kingdom have issued guidance only. A bipartisan Senate bill introduced in early 2026 would make screening mandatory, and the 2024 US screening framework’s broader definition of sequences of concern takes effect on 13 October 2026. The participants include the White House and HHS, scientific societies and the virologists they represent, biosecurity institutes such as the Nuclear Threat Initiative and the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, the gene synthesis industry, and a defense-oriented camp that argues the whole fight over permissions is less important than building countermeasures.

The 1918 influenza genome sequence was published by US federal researchers in 2005; the pandemic it caused killed an estimated 50 million people. Those who favor keeping the research open note that this result is about commercial access, not about laboratory experiments, and is an argument for screening rules rather than for restricting what scientists may study.

## Questions

Question: Does making a pathogen more dangerous in a laboratory teach us things we cannot learn any other way?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Essential for preparedness / Little practical payoff
Why contested: The benefit is claimed in general terms — better surveillance, better vaccine candidates, earlier warning — but almost never traced to a specific countermeasure that would not have existed otherwise, and the counterfactual is close to untestable. Both sides are arguing about what would have happened in a world that did not occur.
Case for each pole: Those who hold the research essential point out that influenza surveillance depends on knowing which mutations to watch for, that reverse genetics is how modern influenza vaccine seed strains are made, and that the Georgetown census found about 24 percent of gain- and loss-of-function publications related to vaccine development and the most-studied pathogens to be those with the highest global disease burden. They add that the techniques cannot be cleanly separated: 29 percent of studies in that census involved both gaining and losing function, so a rule aimed at one hits the other. They argue that the demand for a named countermeasure misunderstands how basic research pays off, and that the field’s contribution to the speed of COVID-19 vaccine development is the kind of return that only shows up when a pandemic arrives. Those who see little payoff reply that the specific experiments in dispute — the ones that make a pathogen more transmissible between mammals — are a tiny fraction of virology and have not produced a vaccine, drug or surveillance trigger anyone can name. Kevin Esvelt of MIT puts the argument sharply: predicting which virus is pandemic-capable is the stated justification for these experiments, but knowing the answer does not help build universal vaccines or broad-spectrum antivirals, so the work is either a waste or a proliferation risk. On this view, the burden of proof belongs with whoever proposes to create a pathogen that does not exist.

Question: Is a laboratory accident or deliberate misuse a larger source of future pandemics than natural spillover?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Laboratory routes dominate / Nature still dominates
Why contested: Natural spillover has a long historical record to estimate from; laboratory and synthesis routes have very few observed events and a capability base that is changing fast, so the two hazards cannot be compared on the same evidence footing.
Case for each pole: Those who think the laboratory and synthesis routes now dominate argue that the natural rate is roughly fixed while the artificial one is rising: the number of high-containment laboratories went from 59 to 69 between 2021 and 2023, synthetic DNA costs a few cents a base, and AI systems now outscore expert virologists on practical laboratory troubleshooting questions in their own specialties. They note that the deliberate route has no natural ceiling — an engineered agent need not be a plausible product of evolution — and that the US select agent system logged 273 release reports in 2024 alone, a rate of containment failure that only has to go wrong once in the wrong place. Those who think nature still dominates point to the record: every pandemic of the last century whose origin is settled began in nature, the corrected Marani estimate puts a 1918-scale event at 0.11 to 0.89 percent per year from natural emergence alone, and that rate is rising with land-use change and animal agriculture. They note that none of the 2024 US releases caused an illness, let alone an outbreak, that the hard steps in making a transmissible agent remain tacit and experimental rather than informational, and that reallocating attention from spillover surveillance to laboratory paperwork trades a measured hazard for a speculative one.

Question: Can “dangerous gain-of-function research” be defined precisely enough to regulate?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Definable in practice / Inherently vague
Why contested: Every US framework since 2012 has been rewritten partly because its definition proved either too narrow to catch what worried people or too broad to administer, and the 2025 pause produced a concrete test case that both sides read as confirming their position.
Case for each pole: Those who think a workable definition exists note that the July 2026 policy names seven specific research outcomes rather than listing pathogens, which closes the gap that let work fall outside a pathogen list, and that other fields manage comparable judgments: human-subjects research and work with radioactive materials are governed by rules that require case-by-case judgment and still function. They argue the right response to ambiguity is an expert review body with published criteria, not abandoning the category. Those who think the term is inherently vague point to what happened when it was applied: NIH suspended more than 40 awards, at least 22 of them tuberculosis studies, after reviewing more than 71,000 projects, and a Berkeley researcher whose work disables genes to make the bacterium weaker found it labeled dangerous gain-of-function. The American Society for Microbiology objected that the distinction between dangerous gain-of-function and potential dangerous gain-of-function is ambiguous as written. Since a conferral of antibiotic resistance is a standard laboratory marker and “altering host range” describes much of comparative virology, they argue the definition’s breadth is not a drafting error that can be fixed but a property of the biology.

Question: Does restricting US funding reduce global risk, or relocate the work?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Sets a standard others follow / Pushes the work offshore
Why contested: The United States funds a large share of this research — Georgetown found US-affiliated researchers on roughly half of the publications it identified — so a US restriction is a real reduction in volume; but it has no authority over the rest, and the unknown is how other states and private funders respond.
Case for each pole: Those who expect a standard-setting effect note that the US was the first country to require federally funded researchers to buy synthetic DNA only from attesting providers, and that European, UK and New Zealand policymakers have been moving toward mandates; the July 2026 policy explicitly “encourages global cessation” of dangerous gain-of-function research and conditions funding on foreign institutions meeting US oversight standards, which exports the rule along the money. Where the US funds half the field, halving the field is itself risk reduction, whatever others do. Those who expect relocation note that Richard Ebright, who wants the research stopped, nevertheless says there is “no — zero — global governance” of it, and that the restriction covers federally funded work, leaving privately funded and classified work in the US and all work abroad untouched; the policy itself says in a footnote that additional statutory action would be required to reach privately funded work. They add that the international-research-of-concern provisions may cut US scientists out of collaborations in exactly the countries whose laboratories most need outside visibility, which could lower the quality of the information available about risky work rather than the amount of it.

Question: Should screening of synthetic DNA orders and benchtop synthesizers be a legal requirement?
Tag: value
Poles: Make screening mandatory / Keep it voluntary and funder-linked
Why contested: It is a choice about who bears a cost and who carries liability — a small, concentrated burden on an industry against a diffuse, uncertain public benefit — and about whether to regulate a general-purpose technology before a harm has occurred.
Case for each pole: Those who want a mandate argue that the voluntary system has a demonstrated hole: an order split into unregulated fragments got 1918 influenza material shipped by 36 of 38 companies, roughly a fifth of commercial capacity sits outside the industry consortium by its own estimate, and benchtop machines in a customer’s own laboratory are outside the ordering system entirely. They note that the industry asked to be regulated, that screening is cheap relative to the cost of a pandemic, and that a voluntary standard punishes the companies that comply by letting competitors undercut them. The Cotton–Klobuchar bill and the June 2026 open letter signed by people at Google DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic, Twist Bioscience, the Nuclear Threat Initiative and others make this case. Those who prefer the funder-linked approach argue that the US already gets most of the coverage through procurement conditions without the rigidity of statute, that a legal mandate fixes in law a sequence-matching method that AI-designed variants have already partly defeated, and that compliance costs and liability exposure fall hardest on small providers and academic core facilities, pushing synthesis toward jurisdictions with no rules at all. They note that screening catches orders, not intentions, and that a determined actor who is deterred from ordering can buy a benchtop device or use serial passaging, which needs no synthesis at all.

Question: Who should decide whether a risky experiment goes ahead — the scientific community or an independent body outside it?
Tag: value
Poles: Expert self-governance / Independent external regulator
Why contested: It is a question about legitimacy as much as competence. Reviewers without deep subject expertise make poor technical judgments; reviewers inside the field have careers and funding bound up in the answer.
Case for each pole: Those who favor keeping judgment inside the scientific community argue that only virologists can tell which experiments are genuinely risky and which merely sound alarming, and that the 2025 suspensions show what happens when they cannot: tuberculosis gene-knockout studies classified as enhancement work. Rasmussen and 77 co-authors argued in the Journal of Virology that oversight expanded across virology would misdirect resources without a commensurate gain in safety, and that the point is to include virology expertise, not to exclude other stakeholders. Those who favor an external regulator argue that the agencies that fund high-risk research currently regulate themselves. Esvelt told the Senate that the chair of the biosecurity advisory board “reports to a department whose research he ostensibly oversees, and they can dismiss him or ignore him as they please,” and urged Congress to empower an independent board including machine-learning experts, security analysts, diplomats and members of the public. Ebright’s version is sharper: assign oversight to a single federal agency that neither performs nor funds research, on the model of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, with rules that have the force of law. The July 2026 policy takes a step this way with its independent third-party review body, while leaving the funding agencies in charge of standing it up. [Correction 2026-10-05: the policy’s review body is not a step toward an external regulator — it is a single government board of federal officials and subject-matter experts established by the funding agencies themselves, so it does not meet this camp’s objection; the sentence was removed from the page.]

Question: Has AI changed biological risk enough to justify new controls now?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Already material / Still mostly speculative
Why contested: The benchmark evidence is about knowledge and troubleshooting, not about completed physical outcomes, and there is no agreed way to translate a model’s score into a change in the number of people who could cause harm.
Case for each pole: Those who say the change is already material point to the Virology Capabilities Test, where OpenAI’s o3 outperformed 94 percent of expert virologists on questions inside their own sub-specialties, and to the demonstration that open-source protein design tools could produce variants of proteins of concern that screening software missed — with about 3 percent of the most functional-looking variants still escaping after patches. They argue that tacit knowledge was the main barrier to misuse, that models are dissolving it, and that the sensible response is to harden the physical chokepoints now, while the lead time exists. Anthropic reported in September 2026 that it had blocked attempted misuse relating to biological weapons work. Those who say it is still speculative note that none of these results is a completed harm: a benchmark score measures answering questions, not acquiring equipment, growing an agent or delivering it, and the screening-evasion study was a red-team exercise whose vulnerability was patched within weeks, which is evidence the defensive system works. They argue that acting on projected capability invites controls that slow the same models now being used for vaccine and antibiotic discovery, and that the evidence of uplift in realistic end-to-end tasks remains thin.

Question: How should a small chance of an irreversible catastrophe weigh against steady, measurable research benefits?
Tag: value
Poles: Precaution comes first / Weigh expected costs and benefits
Why contested: The two sides use different decision rules, not different facts. One treats a tail outcome of civilizational scale as categorically different; the other treats it as a very large number to be multiplied by a very small one.
Case for each pole: Those who put precaution first argue that a pandemic is not an ordinary accident — it propagates, it cannot be recalled, and the harm falls on people who had no say and got no benefit. On this reading, consent and reversibility matter independently of expected value, and a research enterprise that imposes an uncompensated global risk should have to show a benefit that cannot be obtained any other way, not merely a favorable expected-value sum. The 14.9 million excess deaths of 2020 and 2021 and the roughly 13.8 trillion dollars of projected lost output are the scale of what is being risked. Those who weigh costs and benefits argue that precaution applied asymmetrically is itself a choice with a body count: tuberculosis kills over a million people a year, and the suspended awards were not hypothetical. They note that every yearly estimate of catastrophic laboratory risk is small, that the benefits of pathogen research are continuous and compounding, and that a rule which forbids whole categories regardless of the specific proposal cannot distinguish a reckless experiment from a careful one. The right instrument, on this view, is a review that weighs each case, not a prohibition that weighs none.

Question: Should some research findings be kept out of the open literature?
Tag: value
Poles: Publish openly / Restrict dissemination
Why contested: Open publication is the mechanism by which science is checked and built on, and it is also the mechanism by which a blueprint becomes available to anyone. The two functions cannot be separated after the fact.
Case for each pole: Those who favor open publication argue that secrecy does not work in a field with this many practitioners worldwide, that it removes the error-correction that makes published claims trustworthy, and that withholding information from the scientific community mostly handicaps defenders, since developers of vaccines and diagnostics need the same data. They note that classification would move the work into settings with less external scrutiny, not more. Those who favor restriction point to the concrete case: the 1918 influenza genome was published by US federal researchers in 2005 and the sequence is what made the DNA ordering experiment possible twenty years later. The July 2026 policy nudges in this direction, asking authors to describe precautions to journals and suggesting editors consider review more stringent than ordinary peer review for manuscripts describing potential dangerous gain-of-function work. Esvelt has argued that an independent board should also decide when sharing a genome sequence or an experimental outcome would do irreparable harm — and acknowledges this cuts against his own field’s norms.

## Positions

Camp: Ban federally funded enhancement of pandemic pathogens
Adherents: US Department of Health and Human Services and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, which issued the July 2026 policy prohibiting federal funding for dangerous gain-of-function research (joint statement, 28 July 2026, https://www.hhs.gov/press-room/stopping-high-risk-life-sciences-research.html); Biosafety Now, which campaigns for “comprehensive, legally-binding, national- and international-level oversight” and runs a petition to ban dangerous gain-of-function research (https://biosafetynow.org/); Richard Ebright of Rutgers University, who has called for strict oversight or an outright ban and testified to the Senate on the failures of the previous system (https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/imo/media/doc/Ebright%20Testimony%20Updated.pdf).
Case: There is a small category of experiments whose foreseeable product is a pathogen capable of starting a pandemic that does not otherwise exist — one that nature might not produce for thousands of years, or ever. No review committee can make the downside of such an experiment acceptable, because the downside is not confined to the laboratory, the institution or the country that approved it; it falls on everyone, and it cannot be undone. This is not a judgment about scientists’ competence or good faith. It is a judgment about a class of risk that the ordinary machinery of grant review was never designed to carry, and that the previous system demonstrably failed to carry: an oversight framework in which funders judge their own proposals, with no published criteria and no public record of what was approved, is not oversight. If the benefit of these particular experiments were large and specific, a hard rule would be a real loss. It is not: the same category of work is less than 0.1 percent of biomedical research by one estimate, and no vaccine, drug or surveillance trigger traces to the subset in dispute. Drawing a bright line costs the research enterprise very little and removes a risk that compounds every year as the technology spreads. Prohibition also has the virtue of being enforceable: a rule that says no is checkable, while a rule that says “weigh the risks” becomes whatever the weighing produces.
Best evidence: The 2017 potential-pandemic-pathogen framework left the decision to the funding department, published no criteria for what triggered review, and the Government Accountability Office found in January 2023 that HHS had never defined what “reasonably anticipated” meant, so the department could not be sure it was reviewing everything it should (GAO-23-105455, https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105455); a further GAO report in January 2026 found HHS still does not publicly report how it assesses and mitigates these risks (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107348). The record of containment, meanwhile, is not spotless: the Federal Select Agent Program logged 273 release reports in 2024, with 945 people put under medical surveillance or treatment. And the capability is spreading: 69 BSL-4 laboratories operating, under construction or planned across 27 countries as of 2023, only one of which has comprehensive national oversight of dual-use research.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that the definitions are so broad that ordinary work gets caught — the tuberculosis suspensions being the example. The answer is that implementation errors are correctable and the alternative is not. The July 2026 policy already separates a prohibited category from a “potential” category that goes to independent review and can be approved, so the gene-knockout studies that were wrongly flagged in 2025 have a route back. Over-inclusion in the first pass of a new rule is the predictable cost of writing one; under-inclusion in the old rule was the cost of not writing one, and it is paid once, globally, and permanently.

Camp: Narrow the restrictions and keep pathogen research moving
Adherents: The American Society for Microbiology, which said the July 2026 policy’s categories are defined so broadly that the resulting ban “will impede our ability as a nation to address infectious disease threats” (https://asm.org/press-releases/2026/july/asm-statement-on-usg-high-risk-life-sciences-resea); Angela Rasmussen of the Vaccine and Infectious Disease Organization at the University of Saskatchewan, lead author with 77 co-authors of “Virology—the path forward” in the Journal of Virology, arguing that expanding oversight across virology would misdirect resources without a commensurate gain in safety (https://doi.org/10.1128/jvi.01791-23); Treatment Action Group, which documented the suspension of at least 22 tuberculosis awards under the pause (https://www.treatmentactiongroup.org/statement/treatment-action-group-statement-on-suspension-of-tb-funding-over-white-house-gain-of-function-fictions/).
Case: “Gain of function” is not a kind of experiment; it is a description of an outcome that applies to a vast amount of ordinary biology. Disabling a gene to see what it does, adding an antibiotic-resistance marker so a modified strain can be identified, passaging a virus in cell culture, swapping a receptor to study host range — these are the basic grammar of the field, and most of them make the organism weaker, not stronger. When a rule is written against the outcome rather than against the intent and the agent, it catches the grammar. That is not a hypothetical: the 2025 pause suspended tuberculosis studies whose purpose was to make the bacterium less virulent and find new drug targets, against a disease that kills more than a million people a year. The same oversight expansion imposes costs that are real and immediate — review boards, attestations under penalty of law, five-year funding bans, twenty-four-hour reporting duties — against a benefit that is asserted rather than demonstrated. The people best placed to tell a risky experiment from a routine one are the people who do the experiments, and they have been largely excluded from writing the rules that govern them. Safety matters; the right response is better biosafety practice, better facilities and better training, not prohibiting categories of knowledge.
Best evidence: The Georgetown census found that gain- and loss-of-function work use the same techniques, that loss-of-function work is more common, and that 29 percent of studies involve both, so a rule written against one necessarily restricts the other (https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/understanding-the-global-gain-of-function-research-landscape/); it also found nearly all this research is done on BSL-2 and BSL-3 pathogens, not the exotic agents the debate pictures. Sarah Stanley of UC Berkeley described in STAT how her tuberculosis grant was halted as “dangerous gain-of-function research” when the work disables genes and uses a marker treatable by all clinically used antibiotics (https://www.statnews.com/2025/10/06/gain-of-function-research-tuberculosis-expert-nih/). The 2024 Federal Select Agent Program report records no illness, death or community transmission from any of the year’s 273 reported releases.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that the field cannot be trusted to police itself, since its incentives run toward doing the work. Rasmussen and her co-authors reply that they are not arguing for a wholly self-regulated system or for excluding other stakeholders; they are arguing that without virology expertise inside the oversight system, scarce money builds a regulatory apparatus that does not reduce risk and does make the country slower to respond to the next pandemic. On this view the demand is for a seat at the table, not for the table to be removed — and the tuberculosis episode is what the table looks like without them.

Camp: Make screening of synthetic DNA mandatory
Adherents: The International Biosecurity and Biosafety Initiative for Science, a Geneva foundation created by the Nuclear Threat Initiative that builds the free Common Mechanism screening software and convenes international sequence-screening standards (https://ibbis.bio/); Senators Tom Cotton and Amy Klobuchar, whose Biosecurity Modernization and Innovation Act of 2026 would require providers to screen orders and customers, endorsed by the National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology, the Engineering Biology Research Consortium, Twist Bioscience, Integrated DNA Technologies, Aclid and Ginkgo Bioworks (https://www.cotton.senate.gov/news/press-releases/cotton-klobuchar-introduce-bill-to-establish-federal-biotech-security-framework); the roughly eighty signatories of the June 2026 open letter organized by the Institute for Progress and the Foundation for American Innovation, from Google DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Twist Bioscience, MIT, Johns Hopkins and the Nuclear Threat Initiative (https://screendna.org/); Kevin Esvelt of MIT, whose team ran the 1918 influenza ordering test (https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/Testimony-Esvelt-2024-07-11-REVISED-2.pdf).
Case: Arguments about which experiments to permit are arguments about a few hundred funded laboratories. Synthetic DNA is the gateway for everyone else, and it is unguarded. Anyone can order the pieces of a pandemic virus today; the companies that would rather not sell them have no authority to verify who is asking, and the industry trade group has asked Congress to regulate it. Screening is the rare biosecurity measure that is cheap, technically mature, already practiced by most of the industry, and almost invisible to legitimate customers: a sequence check and a customer check, with records kept so that an investigation afterward is possible. Making it mandatory does three things voluntary screening cannot. It closes the gap left by providers outside the consortium and by benchtop machines that sit in a customer’s own laboratory. It stops punishing the companies that screen by letting those that do not undercut them. And it creates the legal basis for an authoritative customer-verification system, which is the part no company can build alone. None of this requires deciding whether any particular experiment should proceed; it is the physical chokepoint, and it holds whatever the answer to the research question turns out to be.
Best evidence: Ordering DNA fragments of the 1918 influenza virus under a pseudonym and a false affiliation, an MIT team received material from 36 of the 38 firms it approached — enough to assemble the virus three times over, and entirely legal, because US rules regulate the assembled agent and not the pieces (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-67955-3). A 2026 legal review found no country imposes a binding screening requirement, only guidance in the US and UK (https://academic.oup.com/jlb/article/13/1/lsag005/8663945). The Microsoft-led Science study showed both the vulnerability and the value of a coordinated system: AI-redesigned proteins slipped past screening tools, three of four providers shipped patches within weeks, and about 3 percent of the most functional-looking variants still got through (https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1100008). [Correction 2026-10-05: the source is the Science paper itself — Wittmann et al., “Strengthening nucleic acid biosecurity screening against generative protein design tools,” Science, https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adu8578 — not the EurekAlert press release, which does not carry the figure.]
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that screening is defeatable — by splitting an order, by a benchtop machine, by an AI-designed variant, or by serial passaging that needs no synthesis at all — so a mandate buys false confidence at real cost. The camp’s answer is that no one claims screening is a wall; it is a filter that raises the cost and the traceability of misuse, and each of the named gaps has a named fix: regulate fragments as select agents, require screening functions in benchtop devices, fund continuous red-teaming of screening tools against design software, and keep records so that an attempt leaves a trail. The alternative on offer is not a better filter but no filter, and the compliance cost — a database lookup per order — is small enough that major providers already absorb it voluntarily.

Camp: Replace self-regulation with an independent licensing body
Adherents: The Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, whose director Tom Inglesby and deputy director Anita Cicero argued with Marc Lipsitch for a policy that would “establish strong oversight and set concrete rules about whether and under what conditions this kind of high-risk scientific work can be done” (https://hsph.harvard.edu/news/opinion-new-policy-would-boost-oversight-of-risky-research); Marc Lipsitch of the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, who with colleagues argued in the Journal of Virology that strengthened independent review would both reduce the risk of a deliberate or accidental pandemic and restore trust in science (https://doi.org/10.1128/jvi.00061-24).
Case: The question is not whether this research should exist but who gets to say yes. Today the agencies that fund high-risk work also judge it, and the advisory board meant to check them reports to the department whose research it oversees. No other field with comparable stakes works this way: human-subjects research and work with radioactive materials are governed by rules with the force of law, administered by bodies that do not have a stake in the outcome. The fix is a single independent authority with published criteria, a transparent record of what it approved and why, membership that includes security analysts and members of the public as well as scientists, and jurisdiction over all such work in the country regardless of who pays for it — which is the gap that leaves privately funded and classified work outside every current rule. This is both safer and better for science. A categorical ban is brittle: it will be over-inclusive at the edges, it cannot adapt as techniques change, and it invites exactly the disputes about definitions now under way. A licensing body can say yes to a well-justified experiment with specified safeguards and no to a poorly justified one, and can be held to account for either.
Best evidence: Two GAO reviews, three years apart, found the same structural problem: HHS never defined the trigger for departmental review and still does not publish how it assesses or mitigates risk in this research (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107348). Of the 27 countries hosting BSL-4 laboratories, one has comprehensive national dual-use oversight legislation, which is what unregulated means in practice. And the swing in US policy — a 2014 pause, a 2017 framework, a 2024 policy, a 2025 pause, a 2026 prohibition — is itself the argument: rules that live in executive orders change with administrations, while a statutory regulator persists.
Answer to the strongest objection: Two objections come from opposite directions. From the research community, that another layer of review will slow work that is not dangerous; the answer is that a properly scoped body reviews a small number of proposals a year and gives the rest certainty rather than the current ambiguity, and that the 2025 suspensions happened precisely because no expert body with published criteria existed to make the call. From those who want a ban, that a licensing body will approve what it should refuse, since review boards tend toward yes; the answer is that a body with no funding stake, public members and a published record is a different animal from a program officer judging a grant, and that if it approves something foolish the record exists to hold it responsible — which is more than the present system offers.

Camp: Build defenses that work against any pathogen
Adherents: The Bipartisan Commission on Biodefense, which proposes a 100 billion dollar, ten-year Apollo Program for Biodefense to deploy countermeasures within days of a biological event, detect a novel threat at the first human or animal cluster, and make indoor spaces safe to gather in (https://biodefensecommission.org/reports/the-apollo-program-for-biodefense-winning-the-race-against-biological-threats/); Blueprint Biosecurity, a nonprofit working on pathogen-agnostic countermeasures — far-UVC and conventional germicidal ultraviolet light, reusable respirator stockpiles and air treatment (https://blueprintbiosecurity.org/).
Case: Every restriction under discussion is national, partial and reversible, and the hazard is global, growing and indifferent to who authorized it. Even a perfectly enforced US rule leaves the rest of the world’s laboratories, the private sector, and the falling cost of the underlying technology untouched. What does not depend on anyone else’s compliance is the ability to detect an outbreak early and stop transmission whatever caused it. That capability is buildable with known technology and it is the same capability for a natural spillover, a laboratory accident and a deliberate release, which means it pays off under every version of the risk estimate rather than only one. Germicidal ultraviolet light in shared indoor air, stockpiles of reusable respirators that actually fit and work, metagenomic surveillance that flags an unknown pathogen before anyone knows what to look for, and vaccine platforms that can be redirected in weeks are not speculative; they are underfunded. The argument is not that restrictions are wrong but that they are a small lever placed where it is hardest to pull, and that the same political attention and money placed on defense buys more safety per unit of controversy.
Best evidence: COVID-19 was associated with about 14.9 million excess deaths in two years and an estimated 13.8 trillion dollars in lost global output, against which the Commission’s proposed decade-long program is a small fraction of one year of that loss. The defense case does not depend on resolving the origin dispute: the measures work the same way whichever answer is right. The Commission added AI as a sixteenth technology priority after its 4 June 2026 meeting, arguing that the same systems that worry biosecurity analysts can be turned to surveillance, diagnostics, countermeasure development and microbial forensics.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that defense is a cheerful way of avoiding the hard choice, and that no realistic countermeasure program would stop a pathogen engineered to be worse than anything evolution produces. The camp accepts the limit and narrows the claim: the point is not that defenses make restriction unnecessary but that they are the part of the problem that does not require anyone else’s agreement, and that a world which can detect and contain fast is also a world in which a laboratory accident is a contained incident rather than a pandemic. On the engineered worst case specifically, the camp’s answer is that air treatment and respiratory protection are pathogen-agnostic by construction — they work on transmission, not on biology — which is exactly why they are robust to an adversary designing around a vaccine.

## Crux

The question underneath all the others is where the burden of proof belongs when the evidence is thin on both sides: must someone show that an experiment or a DNA order is dangerous before it is stopped, or must someone show it is safe before it proceeds? Every camp here accepts that some research is too risky and that some restriction is too costly. What separates them is which error they are willing to make while the evidence is incomplete — approving something that turns out to be catastrophic, or forbidding something that would have saved lives.

What evidence could settle: a large part of this is answerable in principle, and much of it is simply not measured yet. How often do high-containment laboratories have containment failures serious enough to infect someone, and what is the trend as the number of labs grows? What has the disputed subset of enhancement research actually produced — which countermeasures, which surveillance decisions, which published findings that changed practice? A registry of what was proposed, approved and rejected, and what came of it, would convert an argument about anecdotes into an argument about a record; two Government Accountability Office reports have now recommended that HHS publish something like it. On the synthesis side, the facts are more tractable still: what share of world synthesis capacity screens, how often screening catches a genuine attempt, how reliably it holds against AI-designed sequences, and whether mandates in one jurisdiction move orders to another. And the AI question — whether models materially increase the number of people who could cause a pandemic — is being measured, though current benchmarks test knowledge and troubleshooting rather than completed outcomes. None of these would be cheap or quick, but all of them are in principle knowable, and today most are not known.

What no data can close: even with all of that, two choices remain. The first is how to weigh a small probability of an irreversible, global harm against continuous, measurable benefits — whether a catastrophe that cannot be undone is just a very large number in an expected-value calculation, or a different kind of thing that a calculation is the wrong tool for. The second is consent and authority: pathogen research imposes a risk on everyone and a benefit that arrives mostly later and elsewhere, and there is no evidence that can tell us who is entitled to accept that trade on the world’s behalf — the scientists with the expertise, the funders with the money, an independent regulator, legislatures, or international bodies. People who agree exactly on the probabilities can disagree on both, and usually do.
