# Obesity Drugs — research

Slug: obesity-drugs-medicalization
Scope: GLP-1 drugs and treating obesity as a chronic disease, against diet, environment and the medicalization of weight.
Researched: 2026-11-23

## Overview

This topic is about the new generation of obesity drugs, the GLP-1 medicines such as semaglutide (sold as Wegovy and, for diabetes, Ozempic) and tirzepatide (Zepbound and, for diabetes, Mounjaro), and about the idea they have strengthened: that obesity is a chronic disease to be diagnosed and treated by doctors, often for life, rather than a matter of diet, willpower or the food environment. It covers how well the drugs work, what happens when people stop, and what is still unknown about decades of use; whether obesity should be defined as a disease at all, and if so by body mass index or by signs of illness; who should get the drugs, including adolescents; who should pay, from Medicare and Medicaid to employers and people paying cash; and the competing views that the better response is to change the food environment that produced rising obesity, or to stop treating body size as an illness. It deliberately leaves aside the general question of how drug prices relate to innovation (the drug-pricing-vs-innovation topic), who should insure health care in general (universal-healthcare) and whether aging should be treated as a disease (treating-aging-as-disease), touching each only where it bears on obesity drugs. The drugs’ use for type 2 diabetes is background, not the subject. The debate is global, since the World Health Organization counts about 940 million adults with obesity, but most of the coverage fight and most of the data are American.

The why-now event: on July 1, 2026, Medicare began paying for drugs prescribed for weight loss for the first time, through a temporary demonstration called the Medicare GLP-1 Bridge. Eligible Part D enrollees pay a $50 monthly copay for Wegovy, Foundayo (Eli Lilly’s orforglipron pill, approved April 1, 2026) or the KwikPen form of Zepbound, and the manufacturers supply the drugs at a net price of $245 a month, under terms the White House announced with Lilly and Novo Nordisk on November 6, 2025 (CMS, https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coverage/prescription-drug-coverage/medicare-glp-1-bridge ; KFF, May 11, 2026, https://www.kff.org/medicare/what-to-know-about-the-balance-model-for-glp-1s-in-medicare-and-medicaid/ ; White House fact sheet, https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/11/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-announces-major-developments-in-bringing-most-favored-nation-pricing-to-american-patients/). The statute that bars Part D from covering weight-loss drugs is unchanged: the Bridge runs outside the Part D benefit, and on April 21, 2026 CMS extended it to December 31, 2027 after dropping its plan to move the coverage into Part D plans in 2027, because too few plans agreed to take part (KFF, same page). CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz said about 600,000 people had signed up by the end of August (TechTarget, September 1, 2026, https://www.techtarget.com/healthcarepayers/news/366649638/Medicare-GLP-1-program-sees-rapid-uptake-among-seniors), and the USC Schaeffer Center reported on November 6, 2026 that more than 1 million Medicare beneficiaries had gained access (https://schaeffer.usc.edu/research/medicare-obesity-drugs-rarely-covered/). Most recently, on November 17, 2026, a JAMA Summit report written by more than 50 experts concluded that the drugs are a major opportunity but that, in the report’s words, “no single intervention will be enough,” and called for insurance coverage of lifestyle, medical and surgical care together with food policy such as marketing limits for children and school nutrition standards (AJMC, November 17, 2026, https://www.ajmc.com/view/glp-1s-alone-won-t-solve-obesity-jama-summit-report-says).

The debate is live because the drugs have moved from a niche to mass use faster than the evidence, the payers or the definitions have. About one in eight American adults said in late 2025 that they were currently taking a GLP-1 drug (KFF), self-reported obesity fell from its 2022 peak in Gallup’s tracking, cash prices dropped from more than $1,000 a month to $149 to $299 for starting doses, and generic semaglutide went on sale in India in March 2026 and Canada in May 2026 as patents expired there. Yet most people stop within a year and regain much of the weight, employers have stopped expanding coverage, and the WHO projects that fewer than one in ten people who could benefit worldwide will get the drugs by 2030. The people in the debate are obesity-medicine physicians and patient groups who want obesity covered like any chronic disease; public-health researchers, food-policy advocates and the Make America Healthy Again movement, who see the food environment as the cause; the Lancet Commission and its supporters, who want obesity diagnosed by illness rather than body mass index; fat-acceptance and Health at Every Size advocates, who reject treating body size as a disease; and insurers, fiscal conservatives and budget officials who question whether public programs can pay for treating tens of millions of people indefinitely. Drugmakers Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly, whose obesity and diabetes drugs brought Lilly alone $36.5 billion in 2025 (stat 19), fund much of the research and many of the patient and professional groups on the treatment side, a fact the other camps raise often.

- Source URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40353578/ (SURMOUNT-5); https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33567185/ (STEP 1); https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35658024/ (SURMOUNT-1)
- Note: In a 72-week head-to-head trial, people on tirzepatide lost 20.2 percent of their body weight on average and people on semaglutide 13.7 percent; people on placebo in earlier trials lost 2 to 3 percent.
- Context: These are averages, and responses vary widely. Newer drugs push further: Lilly reported a 28.7 percent average loss at 68 weeks on the highest dose of retatrutide in its first phase 3 trial (topline release, December 11, 2025) [unverified against the primary release]. Pills are now approved as well: oral semaglutide (the Wegovy pill) in December 2025 and Lilly’s orforglipron (Foundayo) on April 1, 2026.

### 4. Weight regained after stopping
- Label: Weight regained in the year after stopping semaglutide
- Value: 11.6
- Unit: percentage points of starting body weight regained, of 17.3 points lost (about two-thirds)
- Comparison rows (percent of starting body weight, STEP 1 extension, 327 participants): lost on semaglutide by week 68, 17.3; regained by week 120, one year after stopping, 11.6; net loss at week 120, 5.6; placebo net loss at week 120, 0.1
- Data year: 2022 (published; treatment ran 2018–2020)
- Source label: Wilding et al., Weight regain and cardiometabolic effects after withdrawal of semaglutide: The STEP 1 trial extension, Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, 2022
- Source URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35441470/
- Note: A year after stopping semaglutide, trial participants had regained 11.6 of the 17.3 percentage points of body weight they had lost, leaving a net loss of 5.6 percent.
- Context: In the extension, both the drug and the lifestyle program stopped at week 68, and the improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol seen on treatment moved back toward baseline for most measures. A 2026 systematic review in The BMJ by researchers at the University of Oxford (West et al., 37 studies, 9,341 participants, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41500720/) found people regained an average of 0.4 kilograms a month after stopping weight-loss drugs, faster than after behavioral weight-loss programs, and projected that cardiometabolic markers return to baseline within 1.4 years. Those who see obesity as a chronic disease read this as the expected course of any chronic condition when treatment stops, like blood pressure rising when people stop blood-pressure pills; those wary of the drugs read it as evidence that most users will need them for life, at a cost and with long-term effects no one has yet measured.

- Data year: users who started January 2018–December 2023

### 6. Heart attacks and strokes prevented in the SELECT trial
- Label: Major cardiovascular events with semaglutide against placebo in people with heart disease and overweight or obesity
- Value: 20
- Unit: percent lower risk (hazard ratio 0.80)
- Comparison rows (percent of participants with a cardiovascular death, nonfatal heart attack or nonfatal stroke, mean follow-up 39.8 months): semaglutide 2.4 mg 6.5 (569 of 8,803); placebo 8.0 (701 of 8,801)
- Data year: 2023 (published)
- Source label: Lincoff et al., Semaglutide and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Obesity without Diabetes (SELECT), New England Journal of Medicine, 2023
- Source URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37952131/
- Note: Among 17,604 people with existing heart disease and overweight or obesity but no diabetes, 6.5 percent on semaglutide and 8.0 percent on placebo had a heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death.
- Context: SELECT was the first trial to show an obesity drug reduces hard outcomes, not just weight, in people without diabetes, and it led the FDA to add a cardiovascular indication to Wegovy in 2024, which is how some Medicare plans could cover it before the Bridge. Participants needed a body mass index of 27 or more and established cardiovascular disease, so the result applies to a high-risk group, not to everyone with obesity. Adverse events led 16.6 percent of the semaglutide group and 8.2 percent of the placebo group to stop the drug permanently. Wegovy has since been approved for a serious liver disease (MASH) and Zepbound for obstructive sleep apnea. The trial was funded by Novo Nordisk.

- Comparison rows (ICER, dollars per QALY gained against lifestyle modification, at estimated 2025 net prices): tirzepatide 57,779; injectable semaglutide 66,355; oral semaglutide 75,456 (placeholder price). Commonly used US threshold, 100,000 to 150,000.

- Source label: Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, Treatments for Obesity: Final Evidence Report, December 2025

- Note: ICER found tirzepatide cost $57,779 and injectable semaglutide $66,355 per year of healthy life gained at 2025 net prices, below the $100,000 threshold it uses.

Being cost-effective is not the same as being affordable: see stat 10.

## Questions

### 1. Is obesity itself a disease, or a risk that becomes a disease only when it harms the body?
- Tag: value
- Poles: a disease itself / disease only with illness
- Why contested: Whether something is a “disease” is partly a definition, not a finding, and the definition decides who is treated and who pays. The American Medical Association voted in 2013 to call obesity a disease, over a report from its own Council on Science and Public Health that did not support the label and warned that the disease label “may worsen outcomes for others” (NPR, June 19, 2013, https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2013/06/19/193440570/ama-says-its-time-to-call-obesity-a-disease). In 2025 the Lancet Commission split obesity into clinical and preclinical forms, and obesity-medicine groups objected that this demotes obesity to a risk factor.
- A disease itself: Those who hold that obesity is a disease in its own right, including the Obesity Medicine Association and the European Association for the Study of Obesity, argue that excess fat is a chronic, progressive, relapsing condition driven by biology, in which the body defends a higher weight through appetite and metabolism; that waiting for organ damage before treating it is like waiting for a heart attack before treating high cholesterol; and that calling it a disease removes the moral blame that has kept people from care and insurers from paying. The Obesity Medicine Association warned that the Lancet framework “risks redefining obesity from a disease to merely a risk factor.”
- Disease only with illness: Those who distinguish, including the Lancet Commission and the Royal College of Physicians, which endorsed it, argue that body mass index does not measure fat or health, so labeling everyone above 30 as diseased both overdiagnoses muscular or metabolically healthy people and misses people with dangerous fat at lower BMI. The commission’s chair, Francesco Rubino, said the question of whether obesity is a disease “is flawed because it presumes an implausible all-or-nothing scenario.” Treating illness caused by excess fat as a disease, and excess fat without illness as a risk to monitor, would direct expensive drugs to the people who most need them. Weight-neutral advocates go further and reject the disease label for body size altogether (Positions, camp 4).

### 2. Should public insurance pay for obesity drugs for everyone who qualifies?
- Tag: value
- Poles: cover like diabetes / limit to the sickest
- Why contested: The drugs work and, by ICER’s analysis, are cost-effective at current net prices, but the number of eligible people is so large (92 million Americans, by ICER’s count) that full coverage would add tens of billions of dollars to federal spending. Medicare is barred by statute from covering weight-loss drugs; the Bridge is a temporary workaround, and states and employers have both added and dropped coverage.
- Cover like diabetes: Those who favor broad coverage, including the Obesity Action Coalition and obesity-medicine physicians such as Fatima Cody Stanford, argue that no other chronic disease is excluded from Medicare by law, that the exclusion dates from an era when weight-loss drugs were seen as cosmetic, and that leaving coverage to who can pay cash turns a treatment for a disease concentrated among poorer people into a benefit for the better-off. They point to the SELECT trial’s 20 percent cut in heart attacks and strokes, to ICER’s finding that the drugs are cost-effective, and to falling prices, which make coverage cheaper every year. Medicare covers many treatments that improve health while raising total spending; it does not require dialysis or cancer drugs to pay for themselves.
- Limit to the sickest: Those who favor limits, including fiscal conservatives at the Cato Institute and insurers, argue that cost-effective is not the same as affordable: ICER found fewer than 1 percent of eligible people could be treated without exceeding a sustainable budget, and the CBO projects a net federal cost of $35 billion over nine years even after health savings. Because most people stop within a year and regain weight, much of the spending buys temporary results. Coverage should go first to people at highest risk, such as those with heart disease, and wait for prices to fall further; John R. Puri argues in National Review that prices “will probably keep falling, but only if patients have to pay for the drugs themselves.” Some in this camp prefer removing barriers, such as making the drugs available over the counter, to subsidizing them.

### 3. Will the drugs pay for themselves through lower spending on heart disease, diabetes and other illness?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: largely pay back / remain a net cost
- Why contested: Long-term savings depend on how long people stay on the drugs, how much illness they prevent, how soon prices fall and how many people take them, and published models disagree by hundreds of billions of dollars (stat 11). The outcomes that would generate savings, such as fewer heart attacks, cases of diabetes and joint replacements, take years to appear.
- Largely pay back: Those who expect large savings, such as the USC Schaeffer Center, whose 2023 model projected Medicare savings of $175 billion to $245 billion over 10 years, argue that obesity drives diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease, sleep apnea, osteoarthritis and some cancers, so treating it early prevents expensive illness later; that drug prices fall sharply over time and generics arrive after patents expire; and that the drugs keep showing benefits in new conditions. Counting only near-term budget effects, they argue, ignores benefits to people’s working lives and to the health system as a whole.
- Remain a net cost: Those who expect net costs, including the CBO and the authors of the 2025 JAMA Health Forum model, find that savings from averted illness offset a small share of drug costs within a decade ($3 billion of $38 billion in CBO’s estimate). Savings accrue slowly while drug costs start at once, and if people stop and regain weight the savings never arrive. A treatment can be worth paying for without saving money, they note, but claims that it will save money should not be the reason for covering it.

### 4. Can people safely take these drugs for decades?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: safe long-term / unknown risks
- Why contested: Because weight returns when the drugs stop, most people who benefit would need to take them indefinitely, but trials of the newer drugs in obesity have lasted at most a few years. Rare harms surface only with very large numbers of users over long periods.
- Safe long-term: Those who expect long-term safety point out that GLP-1 drugs have been used for type 2 diabetes for about two decades and that liraglutide and semaglutide have years of experience in millions of patients; that the SELECT trial followed 17,604 people for more than three years with fewer heart attacks and strokes; and that regulators who examined suspected harms have mostly found them rare: the European Medicines Agency concluded in June 2025 that a sudden optic nerve condition, NAION, is a very rare side effect of semaglutide (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/prac-concludes-eye-condition-naion-very-rare-side-effect-semaglutide-medicines-ozempic-rybelsus-wegovy). Untreated obesity carries known, large risks that should be weighed against uncertain, small ones.
- Unknown risks: Those who worry about long-term use argue that taking a drug that reshapes appetite and metabolism for 30 or 40 years, starting in adolescence for some, is a different exposure from the diabetes experience; that weight lost on the drugs includes lean tissue as well as fat, a concern for older adults; that gastrointestinal side effects lead many to stop; and that the population now taking them, including people using compounded or unapproved versions, is broader and less monitored than trial participants. The 2025 MAHA report said the drugs “lack neurodevelopmental and other long term safety data” for children.

### 5. Is rising obesity mainly caused by the food environment, and could policy reverse it?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: policy can reverse / biology outweighs policy
- Why contested: Obesity rose across rich countries from about 1980, too fast for genes to explain, which points to changes in the environment, but decades of public-health campaigns have not reversed it anywhere, and the policies with the best evidence have modest effects.
- Policy can reverse: Those who put the food environment first, including Marion Nestle, Harvard evolutionary biologist Daniel Lieberman and the UK’s chief medical officer Chris Whitty, argue that what changed most since 1980 is food: cheap, heavily marketed ultra-processed products that now supply 55 percent of Americans’ calories and that, in a controlled NIH trial, led people to eat about 500 more calories a day. Countries that regulate food marketing to children and keep strong food cultures, such as France, have held obesity rates fairly stable, Whitty has argued; England’s sugar levy was followed by lower obesity among girls; and taxes, labeling, school standards and marketing limits reach everyone at low cost, including the children who would otherwise become tomorrow’s patients.
- Biology outweighs policy: Those who doubt policy can reverse obesity, including many obesity-medicine physicians, argue that once obesity develops the body defends the higher weight through hormones and appetite, which is why diets fail for most people over the long run and why drugs that act on those hormones work where willpower did not. Population policies help at the margins (the English levy’s effect was 1.6 percentage points among girls and none among boys), but no country has brought adult obesity down by policy alone, and people who already have obesity cannot wait for a better food system. Prevention and treatment, they say, are not rivals.

### 6. Should adolescents and children with obesity be prescribed these drugs?
- Tag: value
- Poles: treat early / wait for evidence
- Why contested: The American Academy of Pediatrics’ 2023 guideline says pediatricians should offer weight-loss medication to adolescents 12 and older with obesity, alongside lifestyle treatment, and Wegovy is approved from age 12; but the drugs would likely be taken for decades, and long-term data in growing children are limited. The CDC counts 19.7 percent of US children and teens ages 2 to 19 with obesity in 2017–March 2020, about 14.7 million, including 22.2 percent of those ages 12 to 19 (https://www.cdc.gov/obesity/childhood-obesity-facts/childhood-obesity-facts.html).
- Treat early: Those who favor treating adolescents, including the AAP and pediatric obesity specialists such as Fatima Cody Stanford, who treats patients as young as 12, argue that obesity in adolescence usually persists into adulthood and already causes diabetes, liver disease and depression in teenagers; that “watchful waiting” lets the disease progress; that adolescents in trials lose weight as adults do; and that withholding effective treatment from children while adults get it is unfair to the children who most need it.
- Wait for evidence: Those who favor caution, including the authors of the 2025 MAHA report, eating-disorder specialists and weight-neutral advocates, argue that children’s bodies and relationships with food are still developing, that there are no data on what decades of use starting at 12 will do, that a drug regimen may be lifelong once begun, and that putting teenagers on appetite suppressants risks disordered eating and teaches them their bodies are a disease. For children above all, they argue, the right response is the food and activity environment around them.

### 7. Does treating obesity as a disease reduce weight stigma or deepen it?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: reduces blame / reinforces stigma
- Why contested: Both sides oppose weight stigma; they disagree about whether the medical frame relieves it or feeds it, and the evidence on how labels change attitudes is thin and mixed.
- Reduces blame: Those who favor the disease frame argue that seeing obesity as biology rather than laziness is the most powerful cure for blame; that the drugs’ success shows weight is not simply a matter of willpower, as Fatima Cody Stanford says the disease process “is beyond this idea of willpower and self-control”; that patients describe relief at learning their struggle had a biological cause; and that stigma has been a reason insurers excluded treatment, so the disease label is a route to fair coverage.
- Reinforces stigma: Those who reject the frame, including the Association for Size Diversity and Health and the writer Virginia Sole-Smith, argue that calling a body type a disease tells fat people their bodies are wrong and need fixing; that a cultural push to medicate weight away is the same obsession with thinness in medical form, in Sole-Smith’s words, “Widespread use of GLP-1s for weight loss is weight loss culture”; and that people who cannot or do not want to take the drugs will be blamed even more for not using the cure. Health care, they argue, should not depend on weight loss at all.

### 8. Should the goal of treatment be lower weight or better health?
- Tag: value
- Poles: weight is the target / health is the target
- Why contested: Drugs are approved and judged mainly on weight lost, but several of their benefits, such as lower cardiovascular risk, may come partly from effects other than weight loss, and some people improve their health without losing much weight. What counts as success shapes approvals, coverage rules and what patients are told.
- Weight is the target: Those who keep weight central argue that excess fat is the cause of the illnesses that follow, so reducing it is the most direct way to prevent them; that weight is easy to measure and track; and that the size of weight loss predicts improvement in diabetes, blood pressure, sleep apnea and joint pain.
- Health is the target: Those who favor health outcomes, including the Endocrine Society, whose September 2026 scientific statement held that “improved health should guide obesity treatment, not weight loss alone” (https://www.endocrine.org/news-and-advocacy/news-room/2026/obesity-scientific-statement), the JAMA Summit report and, in a different form, weight-neutral advocates, argue that drugs should be judged by heart attacks, diabetes and function prevented, not pounds, and that a weight target can push people toward unsustainable or harmful efforts. Weight-neutral advocates would drop weight as a target altogether.

## Positions

### 1. Treat obesity as a chronic disease and cover the drugs
- Adherents: Obesity Medicine Association (institution; professional society of obesity-medicine clinicians), https://obesitymedicine.org/blog/obesity-medicine-association-raises-concerns-over-lancet-commissions-new-recommendations-on-obesity-diagnosis/ ; Obesity Action Coalition (institution; patient advocacy group), https://www.obesityaction.org/troa ; Fatima Cody Stanford (obesity medicine physician-scientist, Mass General Brigham and Harvard Medical School), https://www.ajmc.com/view/obesity-deserves-diabetes-level-coverage-fatima-cody-stanford-md . Also the European Association for the Study of Obesity (https://easo.org/easo-response-to-the-lancet-commission-report-on-obesity-diagnosis-and-management/), the Endocrine Society, which has endorsed the Treat and Reduce Obesity Act, and the sponsors of that bill in Congress (H.R. 4231 / S. 1973).
- Case in the camp’s voice: Obesity is a chronic, progressive, relapsing disease, not a failure of character. The body defends a higher weight through hormones that drive hunger and slow metabolism, which is why diet and exercise alone fail most people over the long run, and why drugs that act on those hormones work. For the first time there are treatments that produce losses of 15 to 20 percent of body weight, cut heart attacks and strokes by a fifth in people with heart disease, and are cost-effective by the standard yardstick. Yet Medicare is barred by law from covering them, most employers do not, and coverage that exists is pulled back whenever budgets tighten, so the people with the most severe obesity, who are disproportionately poorer and from minority groups, are the least likely to be treated. As Stanford puts it, “I think we need to treat obesity like every other chronic disease, for instance, diabetes,” and “coverage should look like it does for type 2 diabetes.” That means passing the Treat and Reduce Obesity Act, making the Bridge permanent, removing step-therapy and prior-authorization hurdles, and treating early rather than waiting for complications. Prevention matters, but no one tells a patient with high blood pressure to wait for a better food system.
- Variation inside the camp: The camp divides on the Lancet Commission. The Obesity Medicine Association and EASO opposed its split between clinical and preclinical obesity as a step back that delays treatment; other obesity-medicine physicians served on the commission and co-authored it, Stanford among them. Members also differ on how far coverage should go for people with lower body mass index and no complications, and on whether lifestyle-program requirements are good care or a barrier.
- Best evidence: STEP 1, SURMOUNT-1 and SURMOUNT-5 average losses of 13.7 to 20.9 percent; SELECT’s 20 percent reduction in cardiovascular events; ICER’s finding of $57,779 to $66,355 per QALY; the weight regain after stopping, read as proof that obesity is a chronic disease needing ongoing treatment; Gallup’s first recorded drop in self-reported obesity as GLP-1 use doubled; and KFF polling showing 61 percent support for Medicare coverage.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that coverage is unaffordable for tens of millions of people indefinitely): Prices are already falling fast, from more than $1,000 a month to $245 for Medicare and $149 to $299 for many cash buyers, and generics are arriving abroad; the cost estimates that frighten budget officials were made at old prices. Medicare covers expensive chronic treatments for heart disease, diabetes and cancer without demanding that they save money. Obesity already costs about $173 billion a year in medical spending, and the choice is not between paying and not paying, but between paying to treat the disease and paying for its complications.

### 2. Fix the food environment before medicating a population
- Adherents: The President’s Make America Healthy Again Commission (institution; its May 2025 MAHA Report), https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/WH-The-MAHA-Report-Assessment.pdf ; Marion Nestle (professor emerita of nutrition, food studies and public health, New York University), https://issues.org/dysfunctional-food-system-schmidt-hagenaars-forum/ ; Daniel Lieberman (evolutionary biologist, Harvard University), https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/10/who-should-take-glp-1s-harvard-thinking-podcast/ . Also England’s chief medical officer Chris Whitty, who said in March 2026 that “just relying on the drugs seems to me the wrong answer” (https://www.aol.com/news/weight-loss-jabs-alone-wrong-220006053.html), food-policy groups such as Healthy Food America and the UK’s Obesity Health Alliance, and authors such as Chris van Tulleken.
- Case in the camp’s voice: Human biology did not change around 1980; the food did. Ultra-processed products engineered to be eaten quickly and in excess now supply more than half of Americans’ calories and more than three-fifths of children’s, and they are marketed hardest to children and the poor. The drugs help individuals, and for people with severe disease they can be life-changing, but they treat the symptom of a broken food system rather than its cause, they must be taken indefinitely, and they put a large share of the population on lifelong medication paid for by public programs while the companies that made the food environment carry on. As Nestle writes, “GLP-1 drugs may help, but they cannot address dysfunctional food systems.” Lieberman puts the point in evolutionary terms: when we treat the symptoms rather than the causes of a mismatch between our bodies and our environment, the problem keeps coming back, and “the one thing that’s changed the most, of course, is our food.” The right response is population policy: taxes on sugary drinks, limits on marketing to children, clear front-of-package labels, school food standards, food assistance that favors whole foods, and research on what ultra-processing does to the body. The MAHA Report pressed for that research and warned that GLP-1 drugs in children “lack neurodevelopmental and other long term safety data.” Those measures reach every child before obesity starts, cost little, and do not depend on a drug company’s price.
- Variation inside the camp: The camp spans the political spectrum. Public-health researchers such as Nestle favor regulating the food industry; the MAHA movement emphasizes whole foods, food additives and personal and parental choice, and the administration it belongs to has itself expanded access to the drugs through the price agreements and the Bridge. Most in the camp accept the drugs for adults with severe obesity or its complications, and the disagreement is about emphasis and money; a minority, especially concerning children, would restrict use more tightly.
- Best evidence: the 55 percent share of calories from ultra-processed food and 61.9 percent among children; the NIH inpatient trial showing about 508 extra calories a day on an ultra-processed diet; the English sugar levy’s effect on girls’ obesity; the rise in obesity across rich countries since 1980, too fast for genetics; the regain of most lost weight after stopping the drugs; and WHO’s projection that fewer than 10 percent of those who could benefit worldwide will get the drugs by 2030, which means prevention must carry most of the global burden.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that food policy has been tried for decades without reversing obesity anywhere): Little of it has actually been tried at the scale of the problem: most sugary-drink taxes are too low to change behavior much, according to WHO, marketing limits are rare, and the food industry has fought each measure. Where serious measures have been applied, they have worked in the direction intended. And drugs have not reversed obesity either: they lower weight only for as long as people take them, which for most people is less than a year.

### 3. Diagnose obesity by illness, not body size
- Adherents: The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology Commission on Clinical Obesity (institution; endorsed by 76 organizations), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39824205/ ; Francesco Rubino (commission chair; chair of metabolic and bariatric surgery, King’s College London), https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/commission-proposes-major-overhaul-of-obesity-diagnosis . Also the Royal College of Physicians, which endorsed the commission’s definition (https://rcp.ac.uk/news-and-media/news-and-opinion/royal-college-of-physicians-endorses-lancet-definition-and-diagnostic-criteria-for-obesity/).
- Case in the camp’s voice: The argument over whether obesity is a disease has been stuck because both sides accept a false premise. In Rubino’s words, the question “is flawed because it presumes an implausible all-or-nothing scenario where obesity is either always a disease or never a disease. Evidence, however, shows a more nuanced reality.” Body mass index is a useful screening tool but it does not measure fat, where fat sits, or whether it is harming anyone. Some people with a high BMI have normal organ function; others with a lower BMI have dangerous visceral fat. Obesity should be confirmed with body-fat or waist measurements, and then divided: clinical obesity, where excess fat is causing organ dysfunction or limiting daily life, is a chronic disease that deserves timely, evidence-based treatment, including drugs and surgery, covered like any other; preclinical obesity is a risk state that calls for counseling, monitoring and risk reduction. This takes illness caused by fat seriously enough to treat it as a disease, refuses to label every large body as sick, and directs scarce, expensive drugs to the people they help most.
- Variation inside the camp: Supporters differ on what preclinical obesity should mean in practice: some would still offer drugs to people with preclinical obesity at high risk, while others reserve them for clinical obesity. Some, such as the Endocrine Society’s 2026 statement, share the camp’s emphasis on health outcomes over weight without taking a position on the diagnostic split.
- Best evidence: the All of Us analysis showing that clinical obesity carried a 6.11-fold hazard of new diabetes and 5.88-fold hazard of cardiovascular events, a much sharper risk signal than BMI alone; the known limits of BMI across ages, sexes and ethnic groups; ICER’s finding that fewer than 1 percent of eligible people can be treated within a sustainable budget, which makes prioritization unavoidable; and the commission’s broad endorsement, including by patient groups.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from obesity-medicine groups, that waiting for clinical obesity means waiting for damage): Preclinical obesity is not left alone: the commission calls for monitoring and, where risk is high, intervention. What changes is that a high BMI alone no longer makes someone a patient with a disease, which protects healthy people from overtreatment and protects the credibility of obesity as a disease for those who have it. Treating everyone early is not possible anyway at current budgets, so the real choice is how to prioritize, and illness is a better guide than a number on a chart.

### 4. Stop treating body size as a disease
- Adherents: Association for Size Diversity and Health (institution; holder of the Health at Every Size principles), https://asdah.org/haes/ ; Virginia Sole-Smith (journalist, author of Fat Talk and the Burnt Toast newsletter), https://virginiasolesmith.substack.com/p/do-we-need-another-thin-persons-ozempic . Also the National Association to Advance Fat Acceptance (NAAFA) and writers such as Aubrey Gordon.
- Case in the camp’s voice: Fat bodies are not diseases. Body size varies, as height does, and the health problems linked to it are shaped as much by weight stigma, weight cycling from repeated dieting, poverty and unequal care as by fat itself. Turning a body type into a lifelong medical condition, to be managed with injections that most people will stop and that make them regain, extends a culture that has always told fat people their bodies are wrong. Sole-Smith puts it plainly: “Widespread use of GLP-1s won’t eradicate weight loss culture, or our obsession with thinness. Widespread use of GLP-1s for weight loss is weight loss culture.” The drugs’ popularity has made it more acceptable, not less, to say that being fat is unacceptable. Health care should treat the actual conditions people have, such as high blood pressure, diabetes or joint pain, at whatever size they are, and should support behaviors that improve health regardless of weight change. In ASDAH’s principles, fat people’s access to compassionate and comprehensive health care “should not depend on obtaining a certain BMI, pursuing weight loss, and/or holding health as a value or pursuit.” What would most improve fat people’s health is ending discrimination in medicine, employment and insurance, not making them smaller.
- Variation inside the camp: Most in the camp defend each person’s right to choose the drugs and object to the social pressure and the medical framing, not to individual use; some fat people with diabetes or other conditions take GLP-1s and remain in the movement. Others oppose prescribing them to children outright. The camp also includes clinicians who practice weight-neutral care within mainstream medicine.
- Best evidence: the weight regain and high discontinuation rates, read as evidence that weight loss is rarely sustained; the limits of BMI as a health measure, acknowledged even by the Lancet Commission; research linking weight stigma and weight cycling to poorer health; the 2013 AMA council report that warned the disease label could worsen outcomes for some; and the industry funding behind many groups that promote the disease frame.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that obesity causes diabetes, heart disease and early death, and that the drugs demonstrably reduce them): The camp does not deny that higher weight is associated with illness or that the drugs improve some outcomes; it denies that the right response is to define a body type as sick. Each of those conditions can be treated directly, and the SELECT trial’s benefits may come partly from effects other than weight loss. A health system that treated disease rather than size would help fat people more than one that treats size as the disease, and it would not leave people who do not want, cannot afford or cannot tolerate the drugs to be blamed for staying fat.

### 5. Limit public coverage until prices fall
- Adherents: Cato Institute (institution; libertarian think tank), https://www.cato.org/blog/government-should-experiment-eliminating-patient-barriers-not-covering-ozempic (blocks automated fetch; title confirmed by search) ; John R. Puri (writer, National Review), https://www.nationalreview.com/corner/dont-ruin-glp-1-prices-by-having-the-government-cover-them/ . Also health insurers: the Alliance of Community Health Plans, whose CEO Ceci Connolly in February 2025 called blanket Medicare coverage a budget buster (as reported by Axios; paraphrase, the article blocks automated fetch) and which in November 2026 urged CMS to make any future coverage targeted and “financially sustainable for the program” (https://achp.org/wp-content/uploads/ACHP-Advancing-Sustainable-GLP-1-Coverage-for-Obesity-in-Medicare_Final.pdf); and the state Medicaid programs and employers that have dropped coverage.
- Case in the camp’s voice: These are good drugs, and adults should be free to buy them. But public programs face a simple arithmetic problem. Some 92 million Americans are eligible, most people who start stop within a year and regain most of the weight, and the health savings, by the CBO’s estimate, cover less than a tenth of the drug costs within a decade. ICER, which finds the drugs cost-effective, also finds that fewer than 1 percent of eligible people could be treated without straining what the health system can absorb, and it has issued an affordability alert. Putting the government on the hook now, at prices that are still falling, would lock in high spending and weaken the pressure that is driving prices down: cash prices fell from over $1,000 a month to $149 to $299 because patients were paying and companies competed for them. Puri argues that “the price of GLP-1 drugs will probably keep falling, but only if patients have to pay for the drugs themselves,” and that “governments should limit their appetite for providing short-term relief at long-term expense.” Where public money is spent, it should go to the people at highest risk, with evidence of lasting benefit, while governments remove barriers instead: Cato has argued for letting patients buy the drugs without a prescription.
- Variation inside the camp: Libertarians such as Cato oppose taxpayer coverage in principle and favor deregulation; insurers and many state budget officials accept coverage for high-risk patients with utilization controls but oppose broad mandates; some fiscal conservatives would accept coverage once prices fall to a level that makes the budget arithmetic work.
- Best evidence: CBO’s $35 billion net cost over 2026–2034; ICER’s affordability alert and its finding that fewer than 1 percent of eligible people could be treated within its budget threshold; the 64.8 percent one-year discontinuation rate; the JAMA Health Forum estimate of a $47.7 billion net increase in Medicare spending; and the employers (12 percent of large non-covering firms in the past year) and state Medicaid programs that dropped coverage after seeing the bills.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that withholding coverage leaves a cost-effective treatment to those who can pay): Prices have already fallen into the range many families pay for other monthly costs, and they will fall further as competitors, pills and generics arrive; coverage targeted at the sickest reaches those for whom the benefit is greatest. Public programs that promise more than they can fund end up cutting coverage abruptly, as several state Medicaid programs have, which is worse for patients than a sustainable, limited benefit.

## Crux

The question under the others: is long-term medication the right main response to obesity for most people who have it, or should it be reserved for the sickest while society addresses obesity some other way, through the food environment, a narrower definition of disease, or an end to treating body size as a problem?

The part evidence can settle is what decades of mass use actually deliver. Over the next 10 to 20 years, longer trials and large registries can show whether the drugs reduce heart attacks, diabetes, cancer and deaths in people who do not already have heart disease; how many people stay on them, and what happens to those who cycle on and off; whether rare harms or effects of starting in adolescence emerge; how much illness and spending they prevent at the prices that will actually prevail once generics arrive; and whether national obesity rates, measured rather than self-reported, fall. Separate evidence can show how much food-environment policies at full strength, such as high sugary-drink taxes and strict marketing limits, change obesity, and whether diagnosing obesity by illness rather than body mass index predicts who benefits from treatment.

The part no data can close is what kind of problem obesity is and who should carry its costs. Even with complete evidence, people will disagree about whether a body type that raises the risk of illness should be called a disease; whether it is better to change individuals’ biology with a lifelong drug or the environment that shaped them; how much public money should go to a treatment that most of the population could qualify for, and whether it should go first to the sickest or to all who qualify; whether a frame that removes personal blame also tells people their bodies are wrong; and how much weight to give to children who might be spared obesity by prevention against adults who have it now. A person who expects the drugs to prove safe and cheap might still favor putting prevention first, and a person who doubts food policy can work might still oppose medicating teenagers.
