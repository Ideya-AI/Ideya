# Fossil Fuel Phase-Out — research

Slug: fossil-fuel-phaseout
Scope: Restricting fossil fuel production rather than only curbing demand.
Researched: 2026-10-01

## Overview

Almost all climate policy built since the 1992 UN Framework Convention works on the demand side: it prices, taxes, regulates or subsidizes the burning of coal, oil and gas, and lets the supply of those fuels adjust to whatever demand remains. This topic is about the other lever — policies that restrict extraction itself. That includes refusing new exploration licences, ending leasing on public land and waters, setting dated end points for national oil, gas or coal output, withdrawing public finance and export credit from extraction projects, removing producer subsidies, and the proposal for a binding international instrument that would allocate production declines between countries the way arms-control treaties allocate warheads. It deliberately excludes the broader question of how fast the whole economy should decarbonize, which belongs to net-zero pace; it excludes carbon dioxide removal and geoengineering; and it treats consumer-side instruments such as carbon pricing or EV mandates only where they bear on the supply question.

The why-now event: from 24 to 29 April 2026, Colombia and the Netherlands co-hosted the First International Conference on Transitioning Away from Fossil Fuels in Santa Marta, Colombia — a conference convened specifically on phasing out fossil fuel production and use, and described by the European Parliament’s own briefing as the first of its kind. Fifty-seven states attended. It produced no treaty and no signed declaration, but three voluntary workstreams (national roadmaps, financial-system reform, fossil-free trade), a technical secretariat, and a commitment to a second conference in Tuvalu in 2027 co-hosted with Ireland (Carbon Brief, “Santa Marta: Key outcomes from first summit on ‘transitioning away’ from fossil fuels”; European Parliament ENVI briefing PE 786.412, April 2026). The conference existed because COP30 in Belém in November 2025 failed to agree any fossil fuel language: roughly 80 countries pushed for a phase-out roadmap and more than half the nearly 200 parties refused even non-binding text, so the Brazilian presidency moved the roadmap outside the UNFCCC consensus process. That roadmap is due to be presented at COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, 9–20 November 2026.

The debate is live for three reasons. First, the legal ground moved: on 23 July 2025 the International Court of Justice held unanimously that a state’s failure to act on climate — “including through fossil fuel production, fossil fuel consumption, the granting of fossil fuel exploration licences or the provision of fossil fuel subsidies” — may constitute an internationally wrongful act, which puts extraction policy inside international law rather than outside it. Second, the arithmetic is unforgiving: the Production Gap Report 2025 finds governments still plan to produce more than double the fossil fuels in 2030 that a median 1.5 °C pathway allows. Third, the politics are moving in both directions at once — Germany published a national roadmap to exit coal, oil and gas by 2045 on 23 September 2026, the third country to do so after France and the Netherlands, while Colombia — by a wide margin the largest producer among the treaty initiative’s endorsing states — inaugurated President Abelardo de la Espriella on 7 August 2026 on a pledge to restart exploration contracts and authorize fracking, and the United States proposed an offshore leasing program of up to 34 lease sales across 1.27 billion acres, replacing the Biden administration’s 2024–2029 plan. The people in this argument are governments of small island and Latin American states, European environment ministries, OPEC+ and other large producers, oil and gas companies and their unions, the IEA and IMF as technical arbiters, and a civil-society coalition built around the Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty Initiative.

### 3. Remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C
- Label: Remaining carbon budget, 1.5 °C
- Value: 170
- Unit: billion tonnes CO2 (GtCO2) from the start of 2026, for a 50 percent chance of holding warming to 1.5 °C
- Data year: 2025 assessment
- Source label: Global Carbon Budget 2025, Global Carbon Project
- Source URL: https://globalcarbonbudget.org/fossil-fuel-co2-emissions-hit-record-high-in-2025/
- Note: At 2025 emission rates the remaining budget for a 50 percent chance of 1.5 °C runs out in about four years.
- Context: The budget is a statistical quantity, not a cliff: it is the cumulative CO2 that can still be emitted for a stated probability of staying under a stated temperature, and the number moves as each year’s emissions are subtracted and as the science of climate sensitivity is revised. Pierre Friedlingstein, who led the 2025 assessment, said on publication that with emissions still rising, keeping warming below 1.5 °C “is no longer plausible.” The relevance to this topic is arithmetic: a budget of 170 GtCO2 is far smaller than the emissions already committed by fossil fuel infrastructure that exists or is under construction, which is the premise of the case for restricting supply rather than waiting for demand to fall.
- Visualization: range (a value on a scale between annual emissions and zero)

- Data year: study published 2022, infrastructure as of 2018–2020

### 5. Share of known reserves that must stay in the ground for 1.5 °C
- Label: Reserves that must remain unextracted by 2050
- Value: 89 (coal), 59 (fossil methane gas), 58 (oil)
- Unit: percent of reserves, for a 50 percent chance of 1.5 °C
- Comparison rows (one source, one study, one unit): coal 89 percent; gas 59 percent; oil 58 percent. The same study finds oil and gas production must fall about 3 percent a year globally to 2050.
- Data year: 2021 study, reserves as of 2018 base year
- Source label: Welsby, Price, Pye and Ekins, “Unextractable fossil fuels in a 1.5 °C world”, Nature, 2021
- Source URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03821-8 (open summary: https://ukerc.ac.uk/news/unextractable-fossil-fuels-in-a-1-5c-world/)
- Note: Nearly nine-tenths of coal reserves and about three-fifths of oil and gas reserves must stay unextracted by 2050 for a 50 percent chance of 1.5 °C.
- Context: This UCL study, published in Nature on 9 September 2021, is the paper most often cited as the quantitative foundation of “keep it in the ground.” Its authors stress that their figures are likely underestimates, because they use a budget consistent with only a coin-flip chance of 1.5 °C and assume substantial deployment of carbon dioxide removal. UKERC’s summary of the paper rounds the same results to “nearly 60 percent of global oil and fossil methane gas and 90 percent of coal.” Regional detail matters politically: the model requires Middle East oil output to halve within thirty years, US gas production to fall 90 percent, 99 percent of unconventional oil to stay in the ground, and no production at all from currently undeveloped Arctic fields. Which reserves count as unextractable in the model depends on cost, so the result tends to spare the cheapest producers and strand the most expensive — a distributional outcome several producer governments dispute as an equity matter rather than a scientific one.
- Visualization: radial (three shares) or ranked

- Note: Five countries account for just over half of all fossil fuel produced in the world.

### 9. Government fiscal support for fossil fuels
- Label: Fiscal cost of government support for fossil fuels
- Value: 0.92
- Unit: trillion US dollars, 2024
- Comparison rows (note: rows differ in source and scope — read with care): OECD bottom-up inventory, 49 economies, 2024: USD 0.92 trillion, down from USD 1.03 trillion in 2023, of which 84.2 percent supports consumption rather than production. IMF, 170 countries, 2024: explicit (fiscal) subsidies USD 725 billion, or 0.6 percent of world GDP; implicit subsidies, mainly the unpriced cost of air pollution and climate damage, USD 6.7 trillion, or 5.8 percent of world GDP.
- Data year: 2024
- Source label: OECD Inventory of Support Measures for Fossil Fuels 2025 (published December 2025); IMF Working Paper WP/25/270, “Underpriced and Overused: Fossil Fuel Subsidies Data 2025 Update” (December 2025)
- Source URL: https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/oecd-inventory-of-support-measures-for-fossil-fuels-2025_0377283f/6fff0cb7-en.pdf
- Note: Governments spent USD 0.92 trillion supporting fossil fuels in 2024 on the OECD’s fiscal measure; the IMF, counting unpriced environmental costs as well, puts the total at USD 7.4 trillion.
- Context: The two headline numbers are not alternatives for the same thing and should never be used interchangeably. The OECD counts money that actually moves or taxes actually foregone, in 49 economies; the IMF adds the gap between what fuels cost and what they would cost if priced to cover pollution, congestion and accidents, in 170 countries. On either measure most of the support goes to consumption, not production, which complicates the supply-side framing: removing it raises prices for households. The IMF paper estimates that removing explicit subsidies alone would cut CO2 emissions about 6 percent below baseline in 2035 and avoid roughly 70,000 premature air pollution deaths a year, while removing both explicit and implicit subsidies through corrective taxes would avoid 1.1 million premature deaths and cut CO2 by 46 percent — which the authors call politically difficult. The same paper finds that for every dollar spent on explicit fuel subsidies, the poorest 20 percent of households receive just 8 cents.
- Visualization: ranked (the two measures side by side), or bar

### 10. How much restricting supply actually reduces consumption
- Label: Change in global oil consumption per barrel of changed production
- Value: 0.6
- Unit: barrels of global consumption per barrel of production, central estimate
- Comparison rows (one study, one table of elasticity assumptions): central estimate 0.59; sensitivity range across demand and supply elasticities 0.08 to 0.78
- Data year: 2014 study
- Source label: Erickson and Lazarus, “Impact of the Keystone XL pipeline on global oil markets and greenhouse gas emissions”, Nature Climate Change, published online 10 August 2014
- Source URL: https://www.sei.org/mediamanager/documents/Publications/Climate/Erickson-Lazarus-2014-KeystoneXL-NatureCC.pdf
- Note: Each additional barrel produced raised global oil consumption by about 0.6 barrels in this model; the authors’ sensitivity range runs from 0.08 to 0.78.
- Context: This is the single most-argued number in the supply-side debate, because it quantifies leakage — the extent to which output withheld by one producer is replaced by another. The study was written about the Keystone XL pipeline and models the price effect of adding capacity to a market with given supply and demand elasticities; read in reverse, it implies that restricting a barrel of production removes roughly 0.6 barrels from world consumption rather than a full barrel. Advocates of supply restriction treat 0.6 as evidence the policy works: a 60 percent pass-through is comparable to the effectiveness of many demand-side measures. Critics treat the same figure as evidence of waste: 40 percent of the restricted production simply moves to another country, so the restricting country bears the full economic cost for a fraction of the climate benefit. The spread in the sensitivity table (0.08 to 0.78) is wide enough that both readings are defensible from the paper itself, and the result is specific to oil; coal and gas, which are less globally traded, behave differently.
- Visualization: range (a value on a scale from 0 to 1)

- Data year: 2025

- Value: 8.7
- Unit: million premature deaths per year
- Comparison rows (one study): China 3.9 million; India 2.5 million; 95 percent confidence interval on the global figure, −1.8 to 14.0 million

- Source label: Vohra et al., “Global mortality from outdoor fine particle pollution generated by fossil fuel combustion: Results from GEOS-Chem”, Environmental Research, 2021

- Note: This modelling study attributes 8.7 million premature deaths a year worldwide to fine particle pollution from burning fossil fuels.
- Context: Estimates of fossil-fuel-attributable mortality vary widely with the exposure-response function used, and this study’s figure is at the high end: the WHO attributes about 4.2 million deaths a year to ambient fine-particle pollution from all sources, and other peer-reviewed estimates for the fossil share are closer to 3.6 million. - Note: An arbitral tribunal ordered Italy to pay Rockhopper EUR 190 million for refusing a production concession after banning nearshore oil activity, against an investment of EUR 29.2 million.

## Questions

### 1. Does restricting production in some countries cut global emissions, or just move extraction somewhere else?
Tag: empirical
Poles: real reductions / displaced production
Why contested: Nobody disputes that some leakage occurs; the dispute is about how much, and no clean natural experiment exists because no large producer has ever restricted output for climate reasons. The modelling answer depends almost entirely on assumed supply and demand elasticities, which differ by fuel, by region and by time horizon, and the published range is wide enough to support opposite policy conclusions.
The case for real reductions: Erickson and Lazarus’s model, the most-cited estimate, finds that one barrel of changed production changes global consumption by about 0.6 barrels. Sixty percent pass-through is not leakage in the dismissive sense — it is comparable to the effectiveness attributed to many demand-side instruments, which also leak through competitiveness channels and through the rebound of fuel prices when one country consumes less. Leakage is also lower for coal and gas than for oil, because both are bulkier to trade and much of the world’s coal and gas is consumed close to where it is dug or piped. Beyond the arithmetic, those who favor supply restriction argue that the model misses the main mechanism: refusing a licence is a visible, durable, legally binding act that shifts expectations about future demand, and expectations are what drive capital allocation. A carbon tax can be repealed in a budget; a cancelled field is a decade of capital that never moved.
The case for displaced production: The same model’s sensitivity table runs from 0.08 to 0.78, and the low end is the relevant one in markets with spare capacity. OPEC+ holds several million barrels per day of idle capacity precisely so it can fill gaps; when a high-cost producer withdraws, a low-cost producer with lower per-barrel upstream emissions typically fills the space, so the climate may gain nothing and lose a little. Ecuador is the cautionary case those who doubt supply restriction cite: a national referendum in 2023 voted 59 percent to stop extraction in the Yasuní ITT block, and Human Rights Watch reported in August 2025 that extraction continued, with only a handful of the block’s roughly 240 wells closed. Even where restriction is implemented, if world demand is unchanged the barrels come from elsewhere and the restricting country has transferred the revenue, not the emissions.

### 2. Should governments stop issuing new extraction licences now, or only once replacement supply is in place?
Tag: value
Poles: stop now / stop when replaced
Why contested: Both sides accept that licensing must eventually end; they disagree about whether the risk of locking in a new thirty-year asset is worse than the risk of a supply gap. Because the two risks land on different people — the first on future generations and climate-vulnerable countries, the second on current consumers and workers — no amount of modelling resolves it.
The case for stopping now: The IEA’s own Net Zero Emissions scenario concludes that no new long-lead-time upstream oil and gas projects are needed, nor new coal mines, mine extensions or unabated coal plants. Trout and colleagues find that existing and under-construction extraction already commits 936 GtCO2, 60 percent more than the 1.5 °C budget they compare against. On that arithmetic, a new field is not filling a gap; it is adding to an overshoot. Because fields take years to sanction and decades to produce, the decision made this year determines production in the 2040s, when a 1.5 °C-compatible world has little use for it — which is also the stranded-asset argument: refusing the licence protects the investor as much as the climate.
The case for stopping when replaced: The IEA’s September 2025 analysis of 15,000 fields found that if upstream capital spending stopped, oil output would fall about 8 percent a year — roughly 5.5 million barrels a day, more than Brazil and Norway produce combined — and gas about 9 percent. Demand is not falling at anything like that rate. Those who favor waiting argue that a supply decline running ahead of demand decline does not produce a transition; it produces price spikes, which fall hardest on low-income households, discredit climate policy, and in Europe’s 2022 experience triggered a round of coal restarts and new LNG contracts that raised emissions. On this view the sequence matters more than the destination: build the replacement, then stop the licence.

### 3. Who should cut production first — the wealthiest producers or the highest-cost ones?
Tag: value
Why contested: The two principles give nearly opposite answers. Cost-efficiency says shut the most expensive barrels first, which means Canadian oil sands, Arctic and deepwater frontier projects, and leaves cheap Gulf production running. Equity says the countries that got rich from extraction and have the most alternatives should go first, which means Norway, the United States, Australia and the Gulf states, while Uganda, Senegal and Guyana develop. Neither principle is wrong; they measure different things.
Poles: richest first / costliest first
The case for richest first: This is the Fossil Fuel Treaty’s and the Santa Marta process’s framing, and it follows the common-but-differentiated-responsibilities principle written into the UNFCCC itself. The European Parliament briefing on Santa Marta notes that the Global South holds about 78 percent of global fossil fuel reserves; a cost-ranked phase-out would therefore ask the poorest holders to keep producing while rich countries decarbonize their own economies and then close the market. Across 40 heavily fossil-fuel-dependent economies, extraction revenue averages 38.6 percent of government income, so a sequence that strands them first is a sequence that defunds schools and hospitals.
The case for costliest first: A cost-ranked decline minimizes the economic loss per tonne of CO2 avoided, which means more abatement for the same money and less political backlash. It also tracks what markets will do anyway: when demand falls, the highest-cost barrel is the one that stops, so policy that fights this is spending political capital to make the transition more expensive. And the equity ranking is not as clean as it looks — Norway’s sovereign wealth fund can absorb a phase-out, but so can Saudi Arabia’s, while Canada’s oil sands employ a region that cannot. Those who favor cost-ranking argue that the right way to handle equity is cash transfers, not production quotas.

### 4. Would restricting supply cause price shocks severe enough to break public support for climate policy?
Tag: empirical
Poles: shocks break it / shocks are manageable
Why contested: Energy price spikes have measurably moved votes in the last four years, but it is hard to separate the effect of climate policy from the effects of war, pandemic recovery and monetary policy. The 2022 European gas crisis is read by one side as proof of fragility and by the other as proof that the problem was dependence, not transition.
The case that shocks break it: After Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, European prices for oil, coal and gas rose by roughly 40, 130 and 180 percent in two weeks, according to European Central Bank analysis cited in the European Parliament’s Santa Marta briefing, and the political response across Europe and North America was a round of fuel subsidies, coal restarts and new LNG contracts — the opposite of a phase-out. The OECD records that emergency support measures peaked at USD 163.9 billion in 2022 and were still USD 57.7 billion in 2024. Those who hold this view argue that supply restriction deliberately creates the conditions that produced that backlash, and that a climate policy whose first visible effect is a higher heating bill will be reversed before it bites.
The case that shocks are manageable: The same crisis produced the fastest expansion of renewables and heat pumps in European history and ended Russian pipeline dominance, and the UK Climate Change Committee concluded in 2026 that reaching net zero by 2050 would cost less than the impact of a single major fossil fuel price shock. On this reading the volatility is a property of fossil fuel markets, not of climate policy: countries that depend on imported hydrocarbons are exposed to price shocks whether or not they restrict anything, and the way out is to need less of the commodity. A scheduled, announced decline is also the opposite of a shock — the whole point of phase-out dates is to replace surprise with a timetable.

### 5. Is natural gas a transition fuel or part of what must be phased out?
Tag: empirical
Poles: bridge fuel / phase it out with the rest
Why contested: Gas emits about half the CO2 of coal per unit of electricity but leaks methane across its supply chain, and the climate verdict depends on leak rates that are genuinely uncertain and have been systematically underreported. It also depends on the counterfactual: gas replacing coal is one calculation, gas replacing renewables-plus-storage is another.
The case for the bridge: Gas-fired plants are the cheapest firm capacity available at scale today, and in systems adding large amounts of variable wind and solar, something must cover the still, dark weeks. The IEA’s Stated Policies Scenario has gas demand rising nearly 1 percent a year to 2035 before leveling off, later than oil or coal, which reflects this role. Where gas displaces coal it cuts CO2 and local air pollution immediately, and in much of Asia and Africa the realistic alternative to new gas is new coal or no electricity. Germany and Italy are cited as the reason BOGA remains small: both treat gas as a necessary transitional fuel and have stayed out of the alliance for that reason.
The case for phasing it out too: The Production Gap Report 2025 finds that governments’ planned gas production in 2030 is 92 percent above the median 1.5 °C pathway and that planned gas output to 2050 is higher than it was in the 2023 assessment — so in practice gas is not bridging to anything, it is being built as a destination. Methane accounted for 17.9 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions in 2024 on the figures cited in the European Parliament’s Santa Marta briefing, and the IEA attributes more than 35 percent of methane emissions to fossil fuel production and use. A gas plant built in 2026 has a thirty-year life, and the capital, contracts and pipelines around it generate political constituencies for keeping it running. Those who want gas treated like coal and oil argue that “bridge” has functioned as a word that defers the decision indefinitely.

### 6. What do wealthy countries owe economies that live on hydrocarbon revenue?
Tag: value
Poles: compensation owed / no entitlement
Why contested: There is no agreed principle for whether a resource a country has not yet sold is an asset it can claim compensation for, and climate finance commitments made so far are small relative to the revenue at stake and have repeatedly been missed.
The case for compensation: Across 40 fossil-fuel-dependent countries, hydrocarbons account for 38.6 percent of government revenue and 61.2 percent of exports; asking them to forgo that is asking them to accept a fiscal shock on behalf of a problem they did little to cause. Ecuador’s 2007 Yasuní-ITT proposal — leave the oil underground in exchange for half its value in international payments — raised a small fraction of its target and collapsed, which supporters of compensation read as a demonstration that the world has been willing to ask for restraint without paying for it. The Santa Marta process built one of its three workstreams around financial-architecture reform for this reason.
The case against entitlement: The counter-argument is that subsoil assets are not held in trust for the holder at any price the holder names, and that paying countries not to produce creates a straightforward incentive to acquire reserves and threaten to develop them. It also mixes up two things: a poor country’s need for development finance, which is real and independent of what is underground, and a claim on the market value of hydrocarbons, which is a claim on revenue from an activity the world is trying to end. Those who hold this view argue that the right instrument is general development finance, debt relief and concessional capital for clean energy — all of which help regardless of geology — rather than payments indexed to the oil a country would otherwise have sold.

### 7. Can a binding international agreement on production be negotiated, or do voluntary coalitions do more?
Tag: empirical
Poles: treaty is achievable / coalitions do more
Why contested: Supply-side cooperation has a plausible precedent in arms control and the Montreal Protocol and a discouraging one in every attempt to get fossil fuel language into a COP decision. Both sides can point to the same record.
The case for a treaty: Production is far more concentrated than consumption — five countries account for just over half of all fossil energy produced — so an agreement among a modest number of parties would cover most of world supply, which is not true of any demand-side instrument. Non-proliferation and the Montreal Protocol both show that production controls among a limited set of producers can work where consumption controls among billions of users cannot. The ICJ’s 2025 opinion gives such an instrument a legal foundation that did not exist before, and the treaty campaign has grown from one state in 2022 to 18 in 2026, plus 199 subnational governments and the European Parliament.
The case for coalitions: Eighteen endorsing states produce almost nothing; the Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance’s core and associate members together account for under 1 percent of world oil and gas production; and at COP30 more than half the parties refused even non-binding phase-out language, which is why the Santa Marta track exists outside the UNFCCC at all. Those who prefer coalitions argue that a treaty requiring the consent of the countries that hold the supply will never be signed by them, and that the time spent pursuing it is time not spent on measures that do not require unanimity — national end dates, export credit restrictions, subsidy removal, and the norm-building that turned “no new coal” from a slogan into the default position of most OECD governments in a decade.

### 8. Should courts enforce limits on extraction, or should elected governments decide?
Tag: value
Poles: courts should enforce / politics should decide
Why contested: Climate litigation has moved from a fringe tactic to a decisive one in several jurisdictions, and the ICJ opinion gives domestic courts new material. Whether that is the rule of law working or the displacement of a political choice is a question about democracy, not about climate.
The case for courts: The ICJ held unanimously in July 2025 that failing to act on climate, including through production, licensing and subsidies, may be an internationally wrongful act; rights that exist only when a government feels like honoring them are not rights. In the United Kingdom, consents for the Rosebank and Jackdaw fields were quashed by the Court of Session in January 2025 because the government had not assessed the emissions from burning the oil and gas, following the Supreme Court’s reasoning in Finch. Supporters argue this is not courts setting energy policy but courts requiring that the decision be made with the relevant facts in front of it — a procedural discipline, not a substantive veto.
The case for politics: Decisions that determine a country’s energy prices, industrial base and fiscal position are the paradigm case of what elections are for, and transferring them to judges removes the mechanism by which a losing side consents to the outcome. The same machinery runs the other way too: investor-state arbitration is a court-like body that has ordered Italy to pay Rockhopper EUR 190 million for refusing an oil concession, against an investment of EUR 29.2 million. Those who favor political decision-making argue that a world in which extraction policy is set by litigation will be a world in which it is set by whoever litigates best, and that the resulting policy is neither coherent nor revisable at an election.

## Positions

### Camp 1 — Stop licensing new extraction and set national end dates
Adherents:
- Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance (institution), founded by Denmark and Costa Rica at COP26 in 2021; core members commit to end new oil and gas concessions, licensing or leasing rounds and to set a Paris-aligned date for ending production. https://beyondoilandgasalliance.org/the-beyond-oil-and-gas-alliance-declaration
- German Federal Ministry for the Environment and Federal Environment Minister Carsten Schneider, who presented Germany’s national roadmap for transitioning away from fossil fuels on 23 September 2026, setting an exit from coal, oil and gas no later than 2045. https://www.bundesumweltministerium.de/fileadmin/Daten_BMU/Download_PDF/Klimaschutz/taff_roadmap_en_bf.pdf
The case, in the camp’s own voice: The cheapest barrel never to be burned is the one never drilled. Every new field is a thirty-year commitment made on the assumption that the world will fail its own climate targets, and governments should stop underwriting that bet with licences, leases and export credit. This is not expropriation: nobody’s existing wells are seized, no contract is torn up. A government simply declines to sell new rights to extract, and publishes the date on which its production ends, so that companies, workers and regions can plan. Done this way, decline comes from geology rather than confiscation — fields deplete at 5 to 8 percent a year on their own — and the state keeps the proceeds of its remaining production to fund the transition rather than paying arbitral awards afterwards. The point of going first is not to move world prices; it is to make the end of the fossil era a fact that other governments must plan around, the way “no new coal” became the default in a decade.
Best evidence: The IEA’s Net Zero Emissions scenario concludes that no new long-lead-time upstream oil and gas projects are needed, nor new coal mines, mine extensions or unabated coal plants. Trout et al. put committed emissions from existing and under-construction extraction at 936 GtCO2, about 60 percent above the 1.5 °C budget they compare against. The coal precedent is the demonstration: the Powering Past Coal Alliance grew to around 180 national, subnational and corporate members and was the main driver behind the Glasgow Climate Pact’s coal language, the first time a COP decision named a fossil fuel.
Answer to the strongest objection — that one country’s restraint is another’s market share: Pass-through is not zero. The standard estimate is that a barrel withheld removes about 0.6 barrels from world consumption, which compares well with demand-side instruments that also leak. More importantly, licensing decisions do not operate only through today’s price; they operate through expectations about future demand, which is what determines whether capital is committed at all. And the countries best placed to go first are rich ones whose public finances can absorb it — which is exactly the sequence critics say equity requires.

### Camp 2 — Cut the emissions, not the production
Adherents:
- OPEC Secretariat and Secretary General Haitham Al Ghais, whose joint statement with the Gas Exporting Countries Forum on 14 November 2025 called for adequate global investment in oil and gas, projected that oil and gas will still hold over 50 percent of the global energy mix in 2050, and put required oil industry investment at USD 18.2 trillion by 2050. https://www.opec.org/pr-detail/1518581-14-november-2025.html
- American Petroleum Institute and President and CEO Mike Sommers, whose State of American Energy 2026 keynote on 13 January 2026 argued that meeting demand requires building rather than blocking, and welcomed the restoration of onshore and offshore leasing. https://www.api.org/-/media/Files/news/2026/SOAE-2026-MJS-Keynote.pdf
Variation inside the camp: Norway holds a lower-carbon version of the same position — it keeps awarding exploration acreage, announcing APA 2026 with 70 additional blocks in May 2026, while taxing offshore CO2 and electrifying platforms, on the argument that Norwegian barrels are among the least emissions-intensive and that cutting them would hand share to higher-emitting producers. Japan’s “various pathways” framing and its investment in ammonia and hydrogen co-firing is another version.
The case, in the camp’s own voice: The objective written into the UN Framework Convention is stabilizing greenhouse gas concentrations, not choosing which energy sources people are allowed to use. Attack the emissions and you can keep the energy; attack the energy and you get shortage, price spikes and a backlash that costs you the emissions too. The practical path runs through carbon capture, methane abatement, efficiency and lower-carbon production techniques, all of which cut emissions per unit of energy without asking two billion people who still cook over biomass to wait. Demand is the thing that is actually growing: oil consumption rose through 2025, gas is rising, and an industry that stops investing while demand rises does not create a transition, it creates a crisis. Supply restriction is a policy of rationing by price, and the people it rations are not the ones proposing it.
Best evidence: Fossil fuels still supplied 86.2 percent of world energy in 2025, barely four percentage points below the 1990 share, and total energy supply passed 600 exajoules, up 1.7 percent. The IEA’s September 2025 field analysis shows that nearly 90 percent of upstream investment since 2019 has gone to offsetting decline rather than growth, and that halting it would remove 5.5 million barrels per day of oil supply each year. Europe’s 2022 experience, when prices for oil, coal and gas rose roughly 40, 130 and 180 percent in two weeks, is the camp’s illustration of what supply shortfall does to public support for climate policy.
Answer to the strongest objection — that abatement technology has never scaled and functions as a license to delay: The camp’s answer is that the comparison is unfair, because carbon capture has been starved of the policy support that wind and solar received for two decades, and because methane abatement is not speculative at all — it is leak detection and equipment replacement with a known cost and an immediate effect, which the EU’s Methane Regulation now mandates and which the industry has accepted. On the wider charge, the camp argues that the burden of proof runs the other way: a policy of restricting supply without a demonstrated replacement is also a bet on technology, just one whose failure mode is a shortage rather than a missed target.

### Camp 3 — Wind production down by binding international agreement
Adherents:
- Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty Initiative (institution), a campaign launched in 2019 for an international legal instrument resting on three pillars: ending new exploration and production, winding down existing production in line with 1.5 °C with wealthy nations moving fastest, and a just transition. It reports 18 nation-state endorsers, 199 subnational governments and more than 4,380 organizations. https://www.fossilfueltreaty.org/progress
- Government of Vanuatu, the first state to call from the floor of the UN General Assembly, on 23 September 2022, for an international mechanism to stop new fossil fuel projects and manage a global just transition; Vanuatu also led the campaign that produced the ICJ advisory opinion. https://fossilfueltreaty.org/vanuatu
The case, in the camp’s own voice: Climate agreements have spent thirty years counting emissions at the tailpipe and have never once counted the barrels at the wellhead, which is why governments can sign a net-zero pledge on Monday and award exploration blocks on Tuesday without contradiction. Production is where the problem is concentrated and where coordination is actually feasible: five countries produce just over half the world’s fossil energy, while consumption is spread across billions of people. Arms control and the Montreal Protocol both worked by constraining supply among a limited set of producers, with a fund to help the ones who could least afford it. A fossil fuel treaty would do the same — a global registry of reserves and production, a negotiated schedule of declines with the wealthiest producers moving first, and financing for the economies that depend on extraction. Voluntary pledges have produced a production gap that is wider in 2025 than it was in 2023. Only a binding instrument closes it.
Best evidence: The Production Gap Report 2025 finds governments plan to produce more than double the fossil fuels in 2030 that a median 1.5 °C pathway allows, and that government plans exceed even those governments’ own climate pledges by 35 percent in 2030. The ICJ’s unanimous advisory opinion of 23 July 2025 established that fossil fuel production, licensing and subsidies fall within the scope of state obligations and may give rise to internationally wrongful acts — a legal foundation the campaign did not have before. The movement from one endorsing state in 2022 to 18 in 2026, plus the European Parliament and the first intergovernmental conference on the subject at Santa Marta, is offered as evidence that the norm is forming.
Answer to the strongest objection — that the states that hold the supply will never sign: The camp accepts that the United States, Russia, Saudi Arabia and China are not joining soon, and answers that treaties are built by a core of first movers and widened under pressure, as the Mine Ban Treaty and the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons both were, and as the Montreal Protocol was. The function of the first phase is to create the registry, the schedule and the finance mechanism, so that the instrument exists when the political moment arrives. The alternative on offer is a thirty-year record of consensus processes that have never once named production.

### Camp 4 — Extract for development, with wealthy producers cutting first
Adherents:
- African Energy Chamber and Executive Chairman NJ Ayuk, who argues that Africa should develop its oil and gas rather than accept restriction, pointing to more than 600 million Africans without electricity and close to a billion without clean cooking. https://energychamber.org/forget-energy-transition-produce-oil-like-nothing-before/
- Government of Guyana and President Mohamed Irfaan Ali, who rejects what he calls the false choice between economic development and environmental protection and defends Guyana’s offshore production as compatible with its forest carbon record. https://jpt.spe.org/president-of-guyana-makes-case-for-oil-and-gas-in-a-balanced-energy-system-at-otc
Related positions: India has insisted at successive COPs that there be no dilution of common but differentiated responsibilities and that equity is the bedrock of any transition; the African Group of Negotiators did not take an official position on transition-away language at COP30, and its chair urged African ministers to oppose a firm commitment, citing the continent’s energy needs.
The case, in the camp’s own voice: The industrial world built its wealth on coal and oil for two centuries and is now proposing a rule, to take effect the moment poorer countries find their own, that nobody may do it again. Global production does have to fall — that is not in dispute — but the sequence is the whole question, and a sequence set by cost or by who moves first is a sequence that keeps Norway and Texas pumping while Senegal and Uganda are told their gas is a stranded asset. The Global South holds about 78 percent of world fossil fuel reserves and almost none of the historical emissions. A country with 600 million people lacking electricity is not going to accept that its first obligation is restraint. Give us the finance and the technology to leapfrog and we will; until then, the resources under our ground are the only development capital we have, and the people who should stop first are the people who can most easily afford to.
Best evidence: Across 40 heavily fossil-fuel-dependent economies, extraction revenue averages 38.6 percent of government income and fossil fuels are 61.2 percent of exports. Energy poverty is not an abstraction: the IMF’s 2025 analysis finds that for every dollar of explicit fuel subsidy the poorest 20 percent of households receive just 8 cents, which is an argument about targeting, but the underlying access gap is real, and OPEC puts the number of people relying on traditional cooking fuels at around 2.1 billion. Meanwhile the restriction coalition’s own members produce almost nothing: BOGA’s core and associate members together account for under 1 percent of world oil and gas.
Answer to the strongest objection — that new African and Caribbean production will be stranded, leaving debt and no revenue: The camp’s answer is that this risk is real and is a reason for better contract terms, faster development and domestic processing, not for abstention. A country that declines to develop its reserves is guaranteed nothing; a country that develops them quickly captures value while the market exists. The camp also notes that the stranding argument is made most insistently by institutions in countries that are still issuing licences themselves, and that the remedy proposed — trust the finance that will arrive later — has a poor record, from the Yasuní-ITT fund to the unmet climate finance pledges of the last decade.

### Camp 5 — Build the replacement faster instead of restricting supply
Adherents:
- Clean Air Task Force (institution), which advocates a technology-diverse decarbonization strategy — advanced nuclear, carbon capture, zero-carbon fuels, superhot rock geothermal — on the argument that the binding constraint is the availability and cost of clean alternatives rather than the availability of permits to extract. https://www.catf.us/about/
- Bill Gates and Breakthrough Energy, whose October 2025 memo “Three tough truths about climate” argued that temperature targets are the wrong measure of progress, that health and prosperity are the best defenses against climate change, and that the decisive variable is whether affordable zero-carbon technologies are ready to deploy at scale. https://static.poder360.com.br/2025/10/bill-gates-three-tough-truths-about-climate.pdf
The case, in the camp’s own voice: The question as posed — restrict supply or curb demand — assumes the bottleneck is willpower. It is not; it is the price and availability of the substitute. Nobody burns coal because a licence was issued; they burn it because it is the cheapest firm power they can get. When solar, batteries, geothermal and nuclear beat the alternative on cost and reliability, fossil fuels stop being extracted without anyone having to pass a law banning them, and when they do not, a ban produces either a shortage or a workaround. Fossil fuels fell only four percentage points as a share of world energy in thirty-five years, not because licensing was too easy but because the replacement was not ready or cheap enough across heat, heavy industry, shipping and firm power. Put the effort where the leverage is: clean firm generation, industrial heat, cement and steel, grid build-out, permitting reform for the new thing rather than prohibition of the old. A world that spends its political capital on refusing licences and then has nothing to switch to has achieved a gesture.
Best evidence: Clean energy investment reached about USD 2.2 trillion in 2026 against roughly USD 1.2 trillion for oil, gas and coal — a two-to-one ratio achieved by cost, not by prohibition. Global Energy Monitor’s 2026 analysis found that in China and India record wind and solar additions met almost all new demand and pushed coal generation down 0.6 percent globally even as coal capacity grew 3.5 percent: the generation number moved because the substitute was cheaper, not because anyone restricted mining. Solar costs fell about 80 percent between 2014 and 2024 and installed capacity grew tenfold between 2015 and 2025, on the IEA figures cited in the European Parliament’s Santa Marta briefing.
Answer to the strongest objection — that waiting for technology is the oldest delay tactic there is: The camp’s answer is that it is not asking anyone to wait; it is asking for the money and political attention to go to deployment and to the hard sectors instead of to licensing fights, and it points to its own record of backing nuclear, geothermal and industrial decarbonization that much of the climate movement opposed or ignored. It concedes the strongest version of the objection — that cheap clean energy adds to supply rather than displacing it unless something also pushes fossil fuels out — and its answer is that the push should be a carbon price and sector standards applied at the point of use, which are harder to evade and do not depend on which government happens to hold the reserves.

## Crux

The question underneath all the others is this: does the world’s fossil fuel problem bind on the supply side or the demand side?

Everyone in this argument agrees that fossil fuel use must eventually fall to near zero — even the producers’ case is framed as abatement and as meeting demand that exists, not as permanence. What they disagree about is which end of the system is actually holding the transition back. If the binding constraint is supply — if cheap, available, politically protected extraction is what keeps coal, oil and gas in the economy, and if capital keeps flowing to new fields because governments keep signalling that the fuels will be needed — then restricting production is the direct remedy and demand policy is pushing on a string. If the binding constraint is demand — if the world burns these fuels because nothing else does the job at the price, and extraction simply responds to that — then restricting supply raises prices without changing what people need, invites someone else to produce it, and spends the political capital that building the replacement requires.

What evidence could settle: a large part of this is empirical and testable in principle. How much does restricting production in one country reduce global consumption, by fuel and over what horizon? The published central estimate is about 0.6 barrels per barrel for oil, with a sensitivity range from 0.08 to 0.78, and no comparable published central estimate exists for coal or gas, which are traded less globally and priced more regionally. How much spare capacity can respond, and how fast? How much does a licensing ban shift expectations and therefore investment, as distinct from shifting physical output? Whether coal capacity now being built in China and India runs at high or low utilization will give a direct reading on whether supply or demand is setting coal’s trajectory. None of these questions is beyond measurement; they are underdetermined mainly because no large producer has yet restricted output for climate reasons and left a record to study.

What no data can close: the sequencing question is a choice about who bears a cost, and no elasticity resolves it. If global production must fall, somebody’s wells close first, and whether that is decided by cost (which protects the poorest consumers and the cheapest producers, many of them autocracies), by wealth and historical responsibility (which protects late developers and asks rich democracies to move), or by who happens to hold a licence when the music stops, is a distributive judgment. So is the weighting between a harm that is certain and near — higher energy bills, closed mines, a region without an industry — and a harm that is probabilistic and distant, falling largely on people who are not yet born and not represented in any legislature that will decide this. And so is the question of how much risk of being wrong a society should accept in either direction: a phase-out that proves unnecessary wastes real wealth, and a phase-out forgone that proves necessary cannot be retrieved. Those are judgments about what we owe strangers and the future, and better data would sharpen them without deciding them.
