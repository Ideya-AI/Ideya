# Surveillance Capitalism — research

## Overview

Most of the internet that people use without paying is financed by advertising, and much of that advertising is priced and aimed using personal data: what people search for, read, watch, buy, where their phones go, who they know, and what software infers about them from all of it. Google and Meta, the two largest sellers of advertising outside China, together reported about $491 billion in advertising revenue for 2025, and behind them sits a less visible industry of data brokers, ad exchanges and tracking software that collects, combines and resells personal information. The Harvard Business School scholar Shoshana Zuboff named this arrangement “surveillance capitalism” in her 2019 book: an economic logic that treats human experience as free raw material for predicting and influencing behavior. The term itself is contested; defenders of the model call the same thing “data-driven advertising” or the “ad-supported internet.” This topic covers the commercial side of the question: who owns and profits from personal data, whether the exchange of data for free services is a fair bargain, whether targeted advertising and data brokering should be permitted, restricted, banned, broken up through competition law, or turned into a market in which people are paid for their data. It deliberately excludes surveillance by governments, including police and intelligence access to commercial data, which belongs to Privacy vs. Surveillance; the use of copyrighted work to train AI models, which belongs to AI, Copyright & Training Data (personal data used for AI training is covered here where it extends the advertising model); antitrust beyond the role of data, which belongs to Antitrust & Market Power; and what platforms allow people to say, which belongs to Content Moderation & Free Speech.

Why now: rules on commercial data are being written and contested on both sides of the Atlantic at once. On 1 August 2026, California began requiring every registered data broker, 654 of them by the state’s count, to process deletion requests submitted through its Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP), and on 25 August the California Privacy Protection Agency reported that more than 500,000 Californians had signed up since the platform opened on 1 January (CalPrivacy, https://privacy.ca.gov/2026/08/half-a-million-californians-have-signed-up-for-drop-to-delete-their-personal-information-from-data-brokers/). In Congress, the SECURE Data Act (H.R. 8413), introduced on 21 April 2026 by Representative John Joyce with the backing of Energy and Commerce chair Brett Guthrie, would create a national privacy standard and preempt overlapping state laws, including California’s Delete Act; at its 3 June hearing Republicans called it a long-overdue national framework and Democrats said it would strip stronger state protections (StateScoop, https://statescoop.com/house-subcommittee-secure-data-act-preempts-state-privacy-laws-2026/); the bill had not been marked up when this was written. On 2 September 2026, US District Judge Leonie Brinkema declined to order Google to sell its AdX ad exchange after finding in 2025 that Google had illegally monopolized parts of the advertising technology market, and instead ordered conduct remedies, including that Google let publishers access and export their own data (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/2/us-judge-rejects-bid-to-break-up-googles-ad-business; US Department of Justice, 16 September 2026, https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-again-wins-substantial-relief-against-google). In Europe, the Commission’s November 2025 Digital Omnibus proposal to amend the GDPR, which would narrow the definition of personal data and let companies rely on “legitimate interest” to train AI, has stalled: more than 1,750 amendments were tabled in Parliament by July 2026, and a planned vote on the Council’s negotiating mandate was cancelled in June (European Parliament Legislative Train, updated 20 September 2026, https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-digital-package).

The debate is live because the business model is now large enough that every option has a price someone can name. Advertisers, publishers and platforms say targeting is what keeps search, email, maps, video and news free, and Meta estimated that Apple’s 2021 iOS privacy changes, led by a prompt asking users’ permission to be tracked, would cost it about $10 billion in 2022 revenue. Privacy regulators, consumer groups and a growing number of state legislatures say the same data flows feed brokers who sell location histories and lists of service members for cents a record, and that the consent people give by clicking “agree” is a fiction. Between them sit competition authorities, who see data as the source of dominant platforms’ power, and a smaller movement that wants people paid for the data they generate. The participants include the large platforms (Google, Meta, Amazon, Apple), the advertising industry and its trade groups such as the Interactive Advertising Bureau, data brokers, publishers, privacy regulators such as the European Data Protection Board and the California Privacy Protection Agency, the US Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general, consumer and digital-rights groups such as Consumer Reports, the Electronic Privacy Information Center, the Electronic Frontier Foundation and the Irish Council for Civil Liberties, scholars such as Zuboff, and legislators in both parties.

Context: California’s Delete Act of 2023 defines a data broker as a business that collects and sells personal information about consumers with whom it has no direct relationship, and requires brokers to register each year. A companion study by Johnson, Shriver and Goldberg (Management Science, 2023) of more than 27,000 top websites found that their use of web technology vendors fell 15 percent for EU residents the week after enforcement, and that concentration in the vendor market rose 17 percent as sites kept the largest vendors, especially those owned by Google and Facebook; the aggregate effects faded by the end of 2018 but persisted for advertising vendors (https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v69y2023i10p5695-5721.html). These studies are central to the claim that privacy rules entrench the largest firms. Supporters of the GDPR answer that early effects reflect weak enforcement and transitional confusion, and that the law’s purpose was to reduce data flows, not to keep website revenue constant.

## Questions

Question: Is trading personal data for free services a fair bargain?
Tag: value
Poles: Fair exchange / Exploitative bargain
Why contested: Billions of people use search, email, maps, social media and news without paying, and measured by what they say they would need to give them up, these services are worth a great deal to them. Whether that makes the data they hand over a fair price depends on whether they know what they are paying, whether they could pay any other way, and whether the costs fall on them or on others.
Case for fair exchange: Advertising has paid for free media for more than a century, from newspapers to broadcast television, and data-driven advertising is the same bargain made more efficient. The value people receive is enormous: in experiments by Brynjolfsson, Collis and Eggers, the median American said they would need $17,530 a year to give up search engines and $8,414 to give up email, and the median Facebook user needed about $48 to give it up for a month (PNAS, 2019, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6462102/). Against that, Meta earned $57.03 per user worldwide in all of 2025 (Meta 10-K). Ads that match people’s interests are more useful than random ones, and they let small businesses reach customers they could never afford to find through mass advertising. A paid internet would exclude the poor first. People who object can block ads, pay for subscriptions where offered, or use privacy-focused services, and most do not.
Case for exploitative bargain: A bargain requires that both sides know the terms, and people do not: 67 percent of Americans say they understand little or nothing about what companies do with their data and 56 percent frequently click “agree” without reading (Pew Research Center, 2023, https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/10/18/how-americans-view-data-privacy/). Reading every privacy policy would take about 201 hours a year (McDonald and Cranor, 2008). The price is hidden, open-ended and paid later: data collected for ads is resold to brokers, exposed in breaches, used to set prices or sold to whoever will pay, including buyers of service members’ records for 12 cents each (Duke University, 2023). Much of the data is about people who never agreed, such as contacts in an address book or non-users tracked across websites. And there is often no real alternative: when Meta offered Europeans a choice between tracking and a paid subscription, the European Data Protection Board said such a binary choice will in most cases not produce valid consent. That 81 percent of Americans think the risks of company data collection outweigh the benefits (Pew, 2019) is not the verdict of people who feel they got a good deal.

Question: Can individual consent meaningfully govern how companies use personal data?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Consent can work / Consent is fiction
Why contested: Nearly every privacy law, from the GDPR to US state laws, rests on giving individuals notice and a choice. Whether people can actually exercise that choice, or whether the volume of decisions, design of consent screens and complexity of data flows make it a formality, is measurable in part, and the evidence points in different directions depending on how the choice is offered.
Case for consent can work: When a choice is presented clearly and evenly, people use it. Apple’s 2021 App Tracking Transparency prompt, a single plain question, led enough users to refuse cross-app tracking that Meta estimated a $10 billion hit to its 2022 revenue (Meta earnings call, February 2022). California’s DROP platform let residents reach every registered data broker with one request, and more than 500,000 people signed up in eight months (CalPrivacy, August 2026). The failure of consent so far comes from bad design, such as walls of legal text, pre-ticked boxes and manipulative banners, which regulators can and do outlaw. Global opt-out signals set in a browser, now required in many US states, and standardized consent valid for months, as the EU’s Digital Omnibus proposes, can reduce the burden to a single setting. Abandoning consent would hand the decision to regulators who cannot know individual preferences.
Case for consent is fiction: The number of decisions is too large for any person to make well. A typical Android app in 2017 contained code from 5 distinct tracking companies (Binns et al., 2018), a single web page load can broadcast data to many bidders at once, and the ICCL estimates a person’s data is broadcast in ad auctions 747 times a day in the United States (2022). No one can evaluate where all of that goes or what can be inferred from it later. When opting out is left to individuals, almost no one does it: users who had opted out of behavioral advertising generated only 0.23 percent of US ad impressions in Johnson, Shriver and Du’s data (Marketing Science, 2020), though most people tell surveyors they object to tracking. And individual consent cannot protect people from what is inferred from others who consented, since data about one person reveals much about their family, friends and neighbors. Rules that limit what may be collected at all, regardless of consent, are the only ones that scale.

Question: How much does tracking people add to what advertising earns?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Tracking funds content / Tracking adds little
Why contested: If behavioral targeting is responsible for much of the internet’s advertising revenue, restricting it would cost publishers and users dearly; if contextual ads that do not track people earn nearly as much, the trade-off is small. Estimates vary roughly tenfold, they come from parties with stakes in the answer, and the market has been changing as browsers and phones block third-party tracking.
Case for tracking funds content: Randomized evidence shows large losses. When Google turned off cookie-based personalization for a random slice of users in 2019, the top 500 publishers’ revenue from those users fell 52 percent on average (Google, https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/disabling_third-party_cookies_publisher_revenue.pdf). Independent academics found that ads shown to people who had opted out of behavioral advertising sold for 52 percent less (Johnson, Shriver and Du, Marketing Science, 2020), and that ad prices fell 18 to 23 percent when tracking was unavailable across thousands of publishers (Laub, Miller and Skiera, 2024). After the GDPR took effect, recorded page views and revenue from EU users fell about 12 percent (Goldberg, Johnson and Shriver, 2024). The cost falls hardest on smaller and general-interest publishers, who cannot command premium prices for their context alone; France’s competition authority found in 2025 that Apple’s tracking prompt penalized smaller publishers in particular.
Case for tracking adds little: Much of the value of tracking is captured by the intermediaries who sell it, not by the publishers who produce content. A study of millions of ad transactions at a large media company found that a cookie raised the publisher’s revenue by only about 4 percent, or $0.00008 per ad (Marotta, Abhishek and Acquisti, 2019). Google’s 52 percent figure comes from a company that sells tracking-based advertising, and it measured a sudden switch in a market built around cookies, not what contextual advertising would earn once advertisers adjusted. Laub and colleagues found that a user identifier, not browsing history, provides most of the value, so the most invasive data adds the least. The Dutch public broadcaster NPO found that ads shown to users who had refused cookies earned as much or more than ads shown to tracked users, and dropped advertising cookies altogether in January 2020 (Wired, 6 August 2020, summarized by Nieman Lab, https://www.niemanlab.org/reading/a-dutch-public-broadcaster-got-rid-of-targeted-digital-ads-and-its-revenues-went-way-up/); reported revenue increases of 61 to 76 percent year on year came from its ad sales house and coincided with other changes [unverified]. And part of the money targeting moves is a transfer between advertisers competing for the same customers, not new value created.

Question: Does data-driven targeting manipulate people or mostly fail to persuade them?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Powerful manipulation / Overrated persuasion
Why contested: Zuboff’s account of surveillance capitalism holds that companies use data not only to predict behavior but to shape it. Others, including some of the model’s fiercest critics, argue the persuasive power is mostly marketing to advertisers, and that the harms of surveillance lie elsewhere. The evidence on persuasion effects is mixed and hard to obtain outside the platforms themselves.
Case for powerful manipulation: In field experiments reaching 3.5 million people, ads matched to personality traits inferred from Facebook data produced up to 40 percent more clicks and 50 percent more purchases than unmatched ads (Matz et al., PNAS, 2017, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5715760/). Platforms continually test what people see and when, and optimize for engagement and conversions with precision no traditional advertiser had. Systems that learn which emotional states make a person most likely to buy, or which messages keep them scrolling, change behavior at a scale where small individual effects add up to large social ones. Zuboff told Congress in 2022 that these are “commodity markets in human futures” and that “no targeting means that corporations can no longer control and curate information flows and social speech or shape human behavior” (House testimony, https://docs.house.gov/meetings/HA/HA00/20220216/114403/HHRG-117-HA00-Wstate-ZuboffS-20220216.pdf).
Case for overrated persuasion: Most measured effects of targeted advertising are small, and many claims of manipulation come from companies selling targeting or from consultancies such as Cambridge Analytica, whose own effectiveness was never shown. The Matz study’s design could not separate psychological matching from Facebook’s own optimization of who saw each ad, according to Eckles, Gordon and Johnson (PNAS, 2018), and economists working with Facebook’s own large randomized experiments found that the observational methods advertisers commonly use often overstate how much ads change behavior, because they count people who would have bought anyway (Gordon, Zettelmeyer and colleagues, Marketing Science, 2019). Cory Doctorow argued in How to Destroy Surveillance Capitalism (2020) that the real problem is the companies’ monopoly power rather than any ability to control minds, and that crediting them with mind control repeats their own sales pitch to advertisers. On this view, the harms of commercial surveillance are real but come from data breaches, discrimination, stalking, price setting and concentrated power, not from irresistible persuasion.

Question: Should personal data be property that people can own and sell?
Tag: value
Poles: Own and sell / A right, not a commodity
Why contested: Ownership sounds like the natural answer to “whose data is it,” and polling shows broad sympathy for being paid. But data about a person is also data about others, it is created jointly with the services that record it, and many privacy advocates fear that a price on data would turn a right into a commodity the poor would sell first.
Case for own and sell: People generate the raw material of a multi-hundred-billion-dollar industry and receive no share of it except free services whose terms they did not negotiate. Senator John Kennedy, introducing the Own Your Own Data Act in 2019, argued that “you create that content. You should own it” (https://www.kennedy.senate.gov/public/2019/3/sen-john-kennedy-r-la-introduces-social-media-data-privacy-legislation). Property rights would let people license their data on their own terms, revoke the license, and be paid. Individually, the sums may be small, but collectively they are not: RadicalxChange proposes “data coalitions” that would bargain on people’s behalf the way unions bargain for workers, recognizing data as a form of labor that now powers AI as well as advertising (https://www.radicalxchange.org/projects/the-data-freedom-act/). Privacy rules that only let people say no leave the companies with all of the value from those who say yes.
Case for a right, not a commodity: Turning data into property would legitimize the trade it is meant to fix. Cameron Kerry and John Morris of the Brookings Institution wrote that “it’s not your data; it’s not their data either,” because information about a person has value to many people with legitimate interests, and that treating it as property “may induce people to trade away their privacy rights for very little value” (Brookings, 2019, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/why-data-ownership-is-the-wrong-approach-to-protecting-privacy/). Meta’s entire worldwide revenue per user was $57.03 in 2025, so any dividend would be a few dollars a month, enough to buy consent but not to compensate for its risks. Those with the least money would sell the most. Zuboff goes further: “Every argument that begins with ‘data’ is an argument that we have already lost,” because the companies’ claim to own human experience is itself illegitimate (House testimony, 2022). European law treats data protection as a fundamental right that cannot be signed away, and most privacy scholars agree.

Question: Is the deeper problem the collection of personal data or the market power of the companies that collect it?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Surveillance is the root / Monopoly is the root
Why contested: The same few companies dominate both data collection and digital advertising, so it is hard to tell whether their data practices cause their dominance or their dominance enables their data practices. The answer determines whether privacy law or competition law is the main remedy, and the two can pull against each other.
Case for surveillance is the root: Smaller companies collect and sell data as aggressively as large ones, and some of the worst practices belong to data brokers most people have never heard of, such as Gravy Analytics, which the FTC said collected 17 billion location signals a day (FTC, 2024). Breaking up Google or Meta would produce several smaller companies with the same business model and the same incentive to extract as much data as possible. Competition can even make matters worse, as firms fight over the same advertisers by offering ever more precise targeting. Only rules that limit collection and use, applied to every firm, address the incentive itself.
Case for monopoly is the root: People tolerate invasive practices because they have nowhere else to go. Germany’s competition authority found in 2019 that Facebook could impose unlimited data collection because its users “practically cannot switch to other social networks,” and that data was “the essential factor for establishing the company’s dominant position” (Bundeskartellamt, https://www.bundeskartellamt.de/SharedDocs/Meldung/EN/Pressemitteilungen/2019/07_02_2019_Facebook.html). Alphabet, Meta and Amazon take 57.6 percent of the world’s advertising market outside China (WPP Media, 2026). [Correction 2026-10-05: 57.6 percent is WPP Media’s forecast of the share Alphabet, Meta and Amazon would take of the 2026 advertising market outside China, not a measured current share (WPP Media, This Year Next Year 2026 Midyear Forecast, 16 June 2026).] Privacy rules that impose fixed compliance costs favor the largest firms: after the GDPR, websites dropped smaller vendors and kept Google and Facebook, raising concentration 17 percent (Johnson, Shriver and Goldberg, 2023). The Electronic Frontier Foundation argues that interoperability and competition would let users “vote with their feet” for services that respect them (https://www.eff.org/wp/interoperability-and-privacy).

Question: Should rules on personal data be loosened to help companies compete in artificial intelligence?
Tag: value
Poles: Loosen for AI / Hold the line
Why contested: AI models are trained on large amounts of data, much of it about people, and governments on both sides of the Atlantic are weighing privacy rules against the fear of falling behind the United States or China. The European Commission’s November 2025 Digital Omnibus would let companies rely on “legitimate interest” to train AI and narrow what counts as personal data; the US SECURE Data Act’s sponsors frame a single national standard as a way to “compete against China.”
Case for loosen for AI: European companies spend heavily on compliance with rules written before modern AI, while competitors in the United States and China train models freely. The Commission’s own proposal holds that pseudonymized data should not count as personal data for a company that has no means to re-identify anyone, and that training AI can be a legitimate interest under conditions, without requiring each person’s consent (European Parliament Legislative Train, https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-digital-package). Consent requirements designed for advertising make little sense for training a model that learns statistical patterns rather than profiling individuals. A patchwork of state laws in the United States, 24 by mid-2026, raises costs for every company that wants to build products nationally. Clearer, lighter rules would bring investment and talent that now goes elsewhere, while protections against harmful uses of AI can be enforced directly.
Case for hold the line: The same companies that built the advertising surveillance model are now feeding the same data into AI, and Meta began using people’s conversations with its AI assistant to target ads in December 2025 (Meta, https://about.fb.com/news/2025/10/improving-your-recommendations-apps-ai-meta/). Weakening the definition of personal data would let firms treat profiling data as anonymous whenever they claim not to hold the key, a claim that is hard to verify. The European Data Protection Board and the European Data Protection Supervisor in February 2026 “strongly” urged legislators not to adopt the changes to the definition of personal data, saying they would contradict EU case law and substantially narrow the scope of protection (EDPB, https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/digital-omnibus-edpb-and-edps-support-simplification-and-competitiveness-while-raising-key_en), and the proposal drew more than 1,750 amendments in Parliament’s committees. In the United States, Consumer Reports said the SECURE Data Act would “replace several stronger state and local laws with a weak federal framework riddled with loopholes” (https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/consumer-reports-statement-on-the-introduction-of-the-secure-data-act/). Rights are hardest to restore once given up, and competitiveness has never required abandoning them.

Question: Should companies be allowed to sell personal data to third parties at all?
Tag: value
Poles: Regulate the trade / End the trade
Why contested: Data brokering supports fraud prevention, identity verification, credit decisions, people searches, marketing and research, but it also produces location histories that reveal clinic visits and lists of service members for sale. Most US law allows the trade subject to opt-outs; some advocates want sale of personal data, or at least of sensitive data, banned outright.
Case for regulate the trade: Much of what brokers do is useful and some is essential: verifying identities to stop fraud, helping lenders reach people without credit histories, measuring the reach of advertising, locating heirs and debtors. Bans would fall on these uses along with abusive ones. The better answer is targeted rules: registration and deletion rights such as California’s, bans on selling sensitive data such as precise location and health information, and enforcement against bad actors, as the FTC did in 2024 when it barred Gravy Analytics and Venntel from selling data tied to sensitive locations. A 2024 federal law already bars sales of sensitive data to foreign adversaries. The industry’s trade groups support opt-out rights for sale and targeted advertising as a national standard.
Case for end the trade: Opt-outs do not work against companies people have never heard of, and the trade’s harms are built into it. Duke researchers bought names, home addresses and other details on active-duty service members for as little as 12 cents a person, with inconsistent checks on who was buying (Sherman et al., 2023). The FTC alleged in 2024 that location data sold by brokers revealed people’s visits to medical facilities and places of worship. California’s DROP platform shows how much effort it takes to get out of 654 registered brokers, and those are only the ones that register. Consumer Reports wants federal law to prohibit selling sensitive personal information outright, and the Norwegian Consumer Council and dozens of organizations have called for a ban on the surveillance-based advertising that generates much of the data. Information gathered for one purpose should not be resold for another.

## Positions

Camp: Protect personal data as a right through comprehensive privacy law
Adherents:
- European Data Protection Board (institution), the body of EU data protection authorities, whose April 2024 opinion held that large platforms offering only a choice between consenting to behavioral advertising and paying a fee will in most cases not obtain valid consent; its chair Anu Talus said “online platforms should give users a real choice when employing ‘consent or pay’ models”: https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/news/2024/edpb-consent-or-pay-models-should-offer-real-choice_en
- Consumer Reports (institution), whose director of technology policy Justin Brookman said on 22 April 2026 that the SECURE Data Act “falls far short of protecting the privacy of American consumers” and would “replace several stronger state and local laws with a weak federal framework riddled with loopholes,” calling instead for meaningful data minimization, universal opt-out signals and a prohibition on selling sensitive personal information: https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/consumer-reports-statement-on-the-introduction-of-the-secure-data-act/
- California Privacy Protection Agency (institution), which wrote to the House Energy and Commerce Committee on 27 April 2026 in “respectful opposition” to the SECURE Data Act and urged federal legislation “setting a floor, not a ceiling” on privacy rights: https://privacy.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2026/04/CalPrivacy-Letter-HR-8413-SECURE-Data-Act.pdf
- Also in this camp: Cameron Kerry and John Morris of the Brookings Institution, who argue for a “baseline federal privacy law” instead of data ownership (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/why-data-ownership-is-the-wrong-approach-to-protecting-privacy/); noyb, the Vienna-based enforcement group led by Max Schrems; the European Data Protection Supervisor; Representative Frank Pallone and other sponsors of the bipartisan American Data Privacy and Protection Act (2022) and American Privacy Rights Act (2024), neither of which passed; the legislatures of the 24 states with comprehensive privacy laws.
Variation inside the camp: All agree that individuals should hold enforceable rights over data about them, backed by a regulator, and that the right should not depend on what a company chooses to offer. They differ on how strong the rules should be. The European model requires a legal basis for every use of personal data and, for behavioral advertising, generally consent; most US state laws let companies collect by default and give people the right to opt out of sale and targeted advertising. A stronger wing, including Consumer Reports, the Electronic Privacy Information Center and Maryland’s 2024 law, wants data minimization: limiting collection to what is reasonably necessary for the service a person asked for, whatever the person clicks. They also differ on whether a federal law should preempt the states or set a floor, and on whether individuals should be able to sue or only regulators should enforce.
Case: Personal information is part of who a person is, and how others may collect and use it should be decided by law that applies to everyone, not by terms of service written by the company that profits. The right model is the one the world’s privacy laws already share: people have the right to know what is collected about them, to see it, correct it and delete it, to refuse its sale and its use for profiling, and to have sensitive information such as health, location and biometrics handled only with their explicit permission. An independent regulator with real fines enforces it. This does not abolish advertising or data-driven services; it makes them answer to the people whose data they use. Consent alone has been abused through dark patterns and take-it-or-leave-it choices, so the best laws pair it with limits on what can be collected in the first place. Americans overwhelmingly want this: 72 percent say there should be more regulation of what companies can do with personal information, and just 7 percent say less. Twenty-four states have acted because Congress has not, and any national law should build on their protections rather than erase them.
Best evidence: Large majorities of Americans say they are concerned about company data use (81 percent), feel they have little or no control (73 percent) and want more regulation (72 percent) (Pew Research Center, 2023). When people are given a clear, one-step choice, they use it: more than 500,000 Californians signed up within eight months for a platform that reaches all 654 registered data brokers at once (CalPrivacy, August 2026), and Apple’s plain tracking prompt led enough users to refuse that Meta estimated a $10 billion revenue hit. European enforcement has produced about €7.1 billion in GDPR fines since 2018 (DLA Piper, 2026), and EU regulators and the Commission pushed Meta to commit to offering European users a free, less personalized advertising option from January 2026, after a €200 million fine under the Digital Markets Act (European Commission, https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/meta-commits-give-eu-users-choice-personalised-ads-under-dma-2025-12-08_en). Sector-specific US laws for health, credit and children’s data show that rights-based rules can coexist with large industries.
Answer to the strongest objection: Industry’s strongest objection is that privacy rules impose large costs and entrench the biggest firms, citing studies that the GDPR reduced recorded website revenue by about 12 percent and raised concentration among web vendors. Those effects are real but early and partial: the concentration effect faded within months except in advertising, the sector regulators scrutinized most, and the answer to compliance costs falling hardest on small firms is clear, uniform rules and stronger enforcement against the largest, not fewer rights. From the other side, advocates of a ban say consent is a fiction and rights-based laws merely legitimize surveillance. That is why the camp’s stronger wing insists on data minimization and bans on selling sensitive data; a comprehensive law can include those limits, and unlike an outright ban on targeted advertising, it can pass and hold up in court.

Camp: Keep data-funded free services under one light national standard
Adherents:
- Interactive Advertising Bureau (institution), the advertising industry’s main US trade group, whose head of public policy Molly Crawford said of the SECURE Data Act on 23 April 2026: “We support strong consumer privacy protections and a strong ad-supported internet. Those goals are not in conflict”: https://www.iab.com/news/iab-statement-on-the-secure-data-act/
- Mark Zuckerberg, chief executive of Meta, who wrote in January 2019 that the best way to connect everyone “is to offer services for free, which ads enable us to do,” that “people consistently tell us that if they’re going to see ads, they want them to be relevant,” and that “we don’t sell people’s data”: https://about.fb.com/news/2019/01/understanding-facebooks-business-model/
- Andrew Ferguson, chairman of the Federal Trade Commission since January 2025, who as a commissioner dissented in September 2024 from the agency’s staff report on social media data practices, writing that “targeted advertising can offer significant benefits to website operators, advertisers, and consumers,” that it “makes much of the internet possible,” and that “the pressing policy question is not targeted advertising itself”: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ferguson-statement-social-media-6b.pdf
- Also in this camp: the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, which estimated in 2019 that a federal law mirroring the GDPR would cost the US economy about $122 billion a year against about $6.5 billion for a narrower law (https://itif.org/publications/2019/08/05/costs-unnecessarily-stringent-federal-data-privacy-law/); Representatives John Joyce and Brett Guthrie, sponsors of the SECURE Data Act; Google; NetChoice; the US Chamber of Commerce; the Network Advertising Initiative and the Digital Advertising Alliance, which run the industry’s self-regulatory opt-out programs.
Variation inside the camp: Members agree that data-driven advertising is legitimate and beneficial, that it should be allowed by default, and that the United States needs one federal standard replacing the state patchwork. They differ on how much the standard should require. Trade groups such as the IAB accept opt-out rights for sale and targeted advertising and stricter rules for sensitive data and children; Ferguson supports rules against mass data sharing and sales to foreign adversaries but opposes treating targeting itself as a harm; some think tanks prefer enforcement against specific harms to any comprehensive regime. Platforms have adapted to Europe’s stricter rules in different ways; Meta, for example, offered a paid ad-free subscription in 2023 and then a less personalized free option.
Case: The internet most people use every day is free because advertising pays for it, and advertising pays for it because it works better when it reaches people likely to care. That arrangement gives a student in a poor country the same search engine, maps and email as a billionaire, and gives a small business the ability to find customers without a national advertising budget. People value these services enormously, by their own account more than $25,000 a year for search and email together, far more than any platform earns from them. Privacy matters, and real harms such as data breaches, fraud, stalking and sales to hostile governments should be punished hard. But rules should target those harms, not the ordinary use of data to show relevant ads, and they should be the same in all fifty states. Twenty-four different state laws, each with its own definitions and deadlines, burden every business that serves the whole country, and Europe’s consent-heavy model has brought cookie banners, lower publisher revenue and more concentrated markets without making Europeans feel more in control.
Best evidence: Randomized evidence shows tracking is worth a great deal to publishers: Google measured a 52 percent fall in the top 500 publishers’ revenue when cookies were disabled (2019), and independent researchers found ads for opted-out users sold for 52 percent less (Johnson, Shriver and Du, 2020). After the GDPR, recorded EU page views and website revenue fell about 12 percent (Goldberg, Johnson and Shriver, 2024), and concentration among web technology vendors rose 17 percent as sites kept Google and Facebook vendors (Johnson, Shriver and Goldberg, 2023). Consumers value free digital services at many times the revenue they generate (Brynjolfsson, Collis and Eggers, 2019). Very few Americans use the opt-outs already available: users who had opted out of behavioral advertising made up only 0.23 percent of US ad impressions in one large study (Johnson, Shriver and Du, 2020).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that people never really agreed to the bargain: they do not read the terms, cannot see where their data goes, and are surveyed as overwhelmingly worried. Worry in surveys is real, but it is not the same as preferring to pay, and when people choose with their own money or time, the great majority keep using free services. The camp agrees that hidden practices are wrong, which is why it supports clear notices, opt-out rights and strict rules on sensitive data and on brokers who sell to bad actors. What it rejects is a regime that presumes every use of data is suspect, because that regime’s costs fall on publishers, small businesses and users who would lose free services, while the largest firms, which have their own first-party data, adapt most easily.

Camp: Ban surveillance advertising and the trade in behavioral data
Adherents:
- Norwegian Consumer Council (institution), Norway’s government-funded consumer agency, whose June 2021 report concluded that the harms of surveillance-based advertising “are significant and serious enough that we believe that it is time to ban these detrimental practices,” adding that “a ban on surveillance-based advertising does not mean that one can no longer finance digital content using advertising”; it sent an open letter the same month with more than 60 organizations, including the European consumer organization BEUC: https://storage02.forbrukerradet.no/media/2021/06/20210622-final-report-time-to-ban-surveillance-based-advertising.pdf
- Shoshana Zuboff, professor emerita at Harvard Business School and author of The Age of Surveillance Capitalism, who told the House Administration Committee on 16 February 2022 that Congress should “interrupt supply by outlawing secret massive-scale extraction of human-generated data” and that “we can outlaw markets that trade in human futures,” as democracies have outlawed markets in human organs: https://docs.house.gov/meetings/HA/HA00/20220216/114403/HHRG-117-HA00-Wstate-ZuboffS-20220216.pdf
- Also in this camp: the Irish Council for Civil Liberties and its senior fellow Johnny Ryan, who campaign against real-time bidding (https://www.iccl.ie/rtb/); the Electronic Privacy Information Center; Accountable Tech and the US coalition of consumer, civil-rights and child-protection groups that called for a ban on surveillance advertising in 2021; the sponsors of the Banning Surveillance Advertising Act introduced in Congress in 2022, Representatives Anna Eshoo and Jan Schakowsky and Senator Cory Booker; the FTC staff report of September 2024 under chair Lina Khan, which recommended limiting data collection to what is necessary for a service and restricting targeted advertising (https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Social-Media-6b-Report-9-11-2024.pdf).
Variation inside the camp: Members agree that advertising based on tracking and profiling individuals should be prohibited, not merely made subject to consent, and that the trade in behavioral data that supports it should end. They differ on scope. Consumer groups such as the Norwegian Consumer Council and BEUC would ban surveillance-based ads while allowing contextual ads and ads based on interests people state themselves. Zuboff would go further and outlaw the secret extraction of behavioral data and the markets in behavioral predictions regardless of their use. Some pursue the same end through strict data minimization laws rather than an explicit ban, and they differ on whether a ban should reach a platform’s use of activity on its own service, not just tracking across other sites and apps.
Case: Surveillance advertising is not a fair trade that a few bad actors abuse; its harms are built into its design. To target ads at individuals, companies must track them across the web and the physical world, assemble profiles of their habits, health, politics and weaknesses, and broadcast fragments of those profiles to thousands of companies in every ad auction, data that then leaks to brokers, scammers, stalkers and foreign governments. Consent cannot fix this, because no one can read 201 hours of policies a year or understand where their data goes once it enters the system, and because profiles of people who say yes reveal much about those who say no. Privacy rights that each person must exercise one company at a time leave the burden on the weakest party. The alternative already exists: contextual advertising, which places ads next to relevant content, funded media for a century and does not require knowing who is reading. Some publishers that switched to it have done as well as or better than before. Markets that are inherently dangerous are banned outright in democracies, not managed through consent forms.
Best evidence: Real-time bidding exposes a person’s online activity and location an estimated 747 times a day in the United States and 376 times in Europe (ICCL, 2022). A single broker claimed to collect 17 billion location signals a day from about a billion devices, and the FTC alleged its data revealed visits to medical facilities and places of worship (FTC, 2024). Researchers bought identified data on US service members for as little as 12 cents a person (Duke University, 2023). Only one in ten Norwegians were positive toward companies collecting personal data about them online and one in five found ads based on personal information acceptable (YouGov survey for the Norwegian Consumer Council, 2021); 81 percent of Americans think the risks of company data collection outweigh the benefits (Pew, 2019). Estimates of what tracking adds for publishers can be as low as about 4 percent of ad revenue (Marotta, Abhishek and Acquisti, 2019), and the Dutch public broadcaster NPO dropped advertising cookies in 2020 after finding untracked ads earned as much.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that banning targeted ads would gut the revenue that keeps the internet free, citing studies of 50 percent revenue losses. Those estimates measure an abrupt switch inside a market built for tracking, often by firms that sell tracking; once all advertisers face the same rules, the money that now goes to profiling would go to context and audiences instead, and much of the value of tracking is captured by intermediaries rather than publishers. Advertising budgets exist because companies need to sell products; a ban changes how ads are placed, not whether businesses advertise. The second objection, that a ban is paternalistic when people could consent, assumes a meaningful consent that the evidence shows does not exist at this scale.

Camp: Break the dominant platforms’ hold on data through competition law
Adherents:
- Bundeskartellamt (institution), Germany’s Federal Cartel Office, which in February 2019 barred Facebook from combining users’ data from its other services and outside websites without voluntary consent; its president Andreas Mundt said “today data are a decisive factor in competition. In the case of Facebook they are the essential factor for establishing the company’s dominant position,” and that because “Facebook users practically cannot switch to other social networks,” the company could no longer “force its users to agree to the practically unrestricted collection and assigning of non-Facebook data to their Facebook user accounts”; the EU Court of Justice confirmed in July 2023 (case C-252/21) that competition authorities may take data protection rules into account: https://www.bundeskartellamt.de/SharedDocs/Meldung/EN/Pressemitteilungen/2019/07_02_2019_Facebook.html
- Electronic Frontier Foundation (institution), whose 2021 paper by Bennett Cyphers and Cory Doctorow, Privacy Without Monopoly, argues that “the problems of corporate concentration and privacy on the Internet are inextricably linked” and that competition and interoperability would let users “vote with their feet” for services that respect their privacy: https://www.eff.org/wp/interoperability-and-privacy
- Also in this camp: Cory Doctorow, whose 2020 book How to Destroy Surveillance Capitalism argues against Zuboff that monopoly, not data-driven mind control, is the core problem; the Open Markets Institute, which welcomed the 2025 ruling that Google monopolized ad technology (https://www.openmarketsinstitute.org/publications/federal-court-confirms-googles-monopoly-over-digital-advertising-market); the US Department of Justice Antitrust Division, which sought the sale of Google’s AdX exchange; the European Commission in its enforcement of the Digital Markets Act, which requires dominant gatekeepers to obtain consent before combining personal data across services and fined Meta €200 million in April 2025; former FTC chair Lina Khan.
Variation inside the camp: Members agree that the market power of a few platforms is what lets them impose invasive data terms, and that competition law is the main tool to change that. They differ on remedies. Some want breakups, such as separating Google’s ad exchange from its other ad businesses, which the Justice Department sought and the court declined in September 2026. Some want conduct rules, such as interoperability and data portability so users can leave with their contacts and history, or bans on combining data across a firm’s services, as in the German Facebook case and the EU’s Digital Markets Act. Some want dominant firms to share data with rivals, which privacy advocates warn could spread personal data further; the EFF wing insists interoperability come with strict privacy rules. Many members also support privacy legislation; what sets them apart is the claim that without competition, privacy rules will be captured or entrenched by the largest firms.
Case: People accept invasive data terms because the services that impose them have no real competitors. Nobody can take their friends, photos and messages to another social network, nobody can easily avoid Google’s search or ad technology, and so no company has to compete on privacy. The dominance is self-reinforcing: the more data a firm controls, the better it targets ads, the more advertisers it draws, and the more it can spend acquiring rivals before they become threats. Alphabet, Meta and Amazon take 57.6 percent of the world’s advertising revenue outside China. [Correction 2026-10-05: 57.6 percent is WPP Media’s forecast of the share Alphabet, Meta and Amazon would take of the 2026 advertising market outside China, not a measured current share (WPP Media, This Year Next Year 2026 Midyear Forecast, 16 June 2026).] Privacy rules alone cannot fix this and can make it worse, because the largest firms absorb compliance costs and hold the first-party data that rules leave untouched, as the rise in concentration after the GDPR showed. The cure is to restore competition: break up integrated ad businesses, ban self-preferencing, require interoperability so people can leave, and stop dominant firms from pooling data across everything they own. In a competitive market, privacy becomes something firms compete to offer.
Best evidence: A US federal court found in April 2025 that Google illegally monopolized the publisher ad server and ad exchange markets, and in September 2026 ordered Google to let publishers access and export their data and barred its advertiser tools from bidding preferentially into its own exchange (US Department of Justice, https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-again-wins-substantial-relief-against-google). Germany’s competition authority found Facebook’s data combination an abuse of dominance, and the EU Court of Justice upheld its power to consider data protection rules in competition cases in 2023. After the GDPR, web vendor concentration rose 17 percent as sites kept Google- and Facebook-owned vendors (Johnson, Shriver and Goldberg, 2023). Under the Digital Markets Act, Meta was fined €200 million and agreed to offer EU users a less personalized ad option, the first such free choice on its networks.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, made by Zuboff and others in the ban camp, is that breaking up the giants would only create many smaller surveillance companies with the same business model. But the worst excesses come from scale and lock-in: a small firm that abuses users loses them, and a market with real exit options gives privacy-respecting services a chance to grow, as encrypted messaging and privacy-focused search engines have shown on a small scale. The camp does not oppose privacy rules; it argues they work only when users can walk away. The industry objection, that the platforms’ scale is what makes free services possible, assumes their size reflects efficiency rather than acquisitions and exclusionary conduct that courts have now found unlawful.

Camp: Give people ownership of their data and a share of its value
Adherents:
- RadicalxChange Foundation (institution), whose proposed Data Freedom Act would create “data coalitions” to “collectively bargain with tech companies on behalf of individuals, negotiating terms, conditions, and payment for the use of their members’ data,” and which “re-imagines data as a form of digital labor”: https://www.radicalxchange.org/projects/the-data-freedom-act/
- Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana, who introduced the Own Your Own Data Act in March 2019 to give each person an exclusive property right in the data they generate online, saying “You create that content. You should own it” and “These social media companies should not own the data that you create”: https://www.kennedy.senate.gov/public/2019/3/sen-john-kennedy-r-la-introduces-social-media-data-privacy-legislation
- Also in this camp: Andrew Yang, whose Data Dividend Project, launched in 2020, sought to establish data as a property right and pay people for its use (https://singularityhub.com/2020/06/28/your-personal-data-is-worth-money-and-andrew-yang-wants-to-get-you-paid/); the computer scientist Jaron Lanier and the economist Glen Weyl, proponents of “data dignity” and “data as labor” (https://www.radicalxchange.org/blog/posts/2019-1-5-ydij2t/); related in part, Senators Josh Hawley and Richard Blumenthal, whose 2025 AI Accountability and Personal Data Protection Act would let individuals sue anyone who uses their personal data or creative work without express prior consent (https://www.hawley.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Hawley-AI-Accountability-and-Personal-Data-Protection-Act.pdf).
Variation inside the camp: Members agree that the people whose data powers the digital economy should share in its value and control its use on their own terms. They differ on the mechanism. Kennedy’s bill and the Data Dividend Project would give individuals property rights to license or withhold their data; Lanier, Weyl and RadicalxChange argue individuals have too little bargaining power alone and propose collective “data unions” or coalitions that negotiate prices and terms; others prefer a tax or levy on data use paid out as a dividend to everyone. The rise of AI, which trains on vast amounts of human-generated data, has given the idea new force and new allies among writers and artists, though the creative-work side of that debate belongs to a separate topic.
Case: The digital economy runs on something people produce every day, their clicks, searches, posts, photos, movements and conversations, and the people who produce it are the only party not paid. Privacy laws give people the right to say no, but most people want the services and say yes, and then the companies keep all the value. Recognizing data as property or as labor changes the bargain: people could license their data for specific uses, revoke the license, and be paid, and companies would have to compete for access rather than taking it by default. Individually the sums are modest, but organized through coalitions the bargaining power is real, the same way workers gained power by organizing. This approach respects people’s choices instead of banning what many of them want, and it turns the question from whether data is collected to who benefits from it. As AI turns human-generated data into the most valuable input in the economy, leaving its producers with nothing is both unjust and politically unsustainable.
Best evidence: Meta alone earned $57.03 per user worldwide in 2025 (Meta 10-K), and Google earned $294.7 billion from advertising (Alphabet 10-K), money generated from what users do. People already treat their data as theirs: 73 percent of Americans say they have little or no control over what companies collect about them (Pew, 2023). Collective action works where individual choice fails: unions and collective licensing bodies in music have long negotiated payment for uses no individual could police. The success of California’s one-stop deletion platform shows that pooled, standardized requests can give individuals real leverage over hundreds of companies at once.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, made by privacy advocates such as Brookings’ Kerry and Morris and by Zuboff, is that putting a price on data would legitimize the trade and lead people, especially poor people, to sell their privacy for pennies. The camp answers that people are already giving their data away for nothing, so a price is an improvement, and that collective bargaining, not individual sales, is what prevents a race to the bottom. Ownership can coexist with privacy rights: inalienable protections for sensitive data and bans on harmful uses can sit alongside the right to be paid for the rest. The objection that data about one person is also about others is real, which is why the camp’s stronger proposals vest bargaining in groups rather than in isolated individuals.

## Crux

Can the commercial use of personal data be made to answer to the people it describes, through their own choices, through competition or through payment, or do its harms come from the collection itself, so that some uses must be off-limits whatever people agree to?

What evidence could settle: how much behavioral targeting adds to what advertising earns, and how much of that reaches publishers rather than intermediaries, once advertisers have adjusted to limits rather than in an abrupt switch; whether people given a clear, low-effort choice actually exercise it, as the uptake of Apple’s tracking prompt and California’s deletion platform suggest, or whether most never do; how often data collected for advertising ends up in harmful uses such as stalking, fraud, discrimination, sales to hostile buyers or breaches; whether targeted advertising changes behavior much more than untargeted advertising does; and whether privacy rules, competition remedies or data markets have in practice reduced data collection or merely shifted it toward the largest firms.

What no data can close: whether privacy is something each person may trade for convenience or money, or a condition a society must protect even from people’s own choices, because information about one person reveals others and because the bargain is struck between parties of unequal power; and how much weight to give the value of free services and the advertisers and publishers who depend on them against the harms that fall on people who never chose to be tracked.
