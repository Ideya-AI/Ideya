# Climate Tipping Points — research

## Overview

Climate tipping points are thresholds in the Earth system beyond which a part of it, pushed by warming, keeps changing under its own momentum: an ice sheet that keeps shrinking because its surface has dropped into warmer air, a rainforest that dries itself out as it loses the trees that recycle its rain, an ocean circulation that weakens because the fresher, lighter water it carries north no longer sinks. The IPCC defines a tipping point as “a critical threshold beyond which a system reorganizes, often abruptly and/or irreversibly” (as quoted by Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipping_points_in_the_climate_system). The most-cited assessment, Armstrong McKay and colleagues in Science in 2022, lists 16 candidate “tipping elements” (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abn7950): the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, the marine basins of East Antarctica, mountain glaciers, permafrost, warm-water coral reefs, the Amazon and boreal forests, the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) and the North Atlantic subpolar gyre, and the West African monsoon among them. The topic covers where those thresholds are, how close warming has come to them, how fast each system would change once past one, whether a temporary overshoot can be reversed in time, and how, or whether, their risk should shape climate targets and policy. It includes “positive tipping points” in technology and society only as a strategy some camps propose for avoiding the physical ones. It deliberately leaves the general pace of emissions cuts to Net-Zero Pace, carbon removal to Carbon Dioxide Removal, the governance of solar geoengineering in general to its own topic, societal breakdown to Civilizational Collapse, and fire seasons to Wildfires, touching each only where a camp’s case about tipping depends on it.

Why now: on 13 August 2026 researchers at Utrecht University reported in Nature Climate Change that the Atlantic overturning circulation’s tipping threshold depends on how fast the world warms, not only on how much. In their climate model the AMOC stayed stable up to 5.5°C of warming when carbon dioxide rose slowly, but collapsed at about 2°C when it rose at rates comparable to today’s (van Westen, Börner and Dijkstra, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-026-02730-w). It was the latest in a year of findings on the same systems. In October 2025 the Global Tipping Points Report, written by 160 scientists from 87 institutions, concluded that warm-water coral reefs are passing their thermal tipping point, the first Earth-system tipping point it judged to have been reached (Stockholm Resilience Centre, https://www.stockholmresilience.org/research/research-stories/2025-10-13-world-reaches-first-climate-tipping-point---widespread-mortality-of-coral-reefs.html). In April 2026 a study in Science Advances that corrected models against observations projected the AMOC would weaken by about half by 2100 under middle-of-the-road emissions (Portmann et al., https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adx4298). In May 2026 a Nature study found that deforestation lowers the Amazon’s warming threshold to about 1.5 to 1.9°C (Wunderling et al., https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10456-0). In June 2026 NOAA said the fourth global coral bleaching event, which put 84 percent of the world’s reef area under bleaching-level heat stress, had likely ended (https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/worlds-fourth-mass-coral-bleaching-event-likely-ended-2025). And on 17 September 2026 Bjorn Lomborg argued that the Great Barrier Reef “refuses to die on schedule” and that doomsday forecasts cost science its credibility (Business Day, https://www.businessday.co.za/opinion/2026-09-17-bjorn-lomborg-the-great-barrier-reef-refuses-to-die-on-schedule/).

The debate is live because the world is about to cross the warming level at which several tipping points become plausible. Warming reached 1.39°C in 2025 (Forster et al., https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/3889/2026/), the World Meteorological Organization puts a 75 percent chance on the 2026–2030 average exceeding 1.5°C, and current policies point to about 2.6°C by 2100. Scientists largely agree that some thresholds lie within that range; they disagree about how sharp and how fast the changes would be, whether “tipping point” is the right way to describe them, and how much a low-probability, irreversible loss should weigh against present costs. The participants are the Earth-system scientists behind the Global Tipping Points Reports (University of Exeter, Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, Stockholm Resilience Centre), who want warming held below 2°C and brought back toward 1°C; other climate scientists and analysts (Robert Kopp and colleagues, the Breakthrough Institute) who think the tipping-point frame confuses the public and the policy; the authors of the US Department of Energy’s 2025 climate review and commentators such as Bjorn Lomborg, who hold that tipping risks do not justify major changes to the cost of carbon; a research program, led by the UK’s Advanced Research and Invention Agency, that is testing ways to cool the climate or thicken sea ice to buy time; and a smaller movement, Deep Adaptation, that treats societal collapse as already under way.

This number matters here because the tipping-threshold estimates are expressed on the same scale: current warming already exceeds the central estimate for coral reefs and sits inside the lower end of the ranges for Greenland, West Antarctica, permafrost and the subpolar gyre.

Context: The review’s authors use the IPCC’s probability language: “as likely as not” means a 33 to 66 percent chance and “likely” means above 66 percent. At 1.5°C one of the three is warm-water coral reefs, which they call “virtually certain” (above 99 percent) to tip because the top of their threshold range is 1.5°C; the other two could be any combination of the North Atlantic subpolar gyre, land permafrost and the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets. At 2°C mountain glaciers, boreal forests and the AMOC join the list. “Tip” here means the threshold is crossed for long enough to commit the system to change, not that the change is complete; for ice sheets it would unfold over thousands of years. The review concludes that warming must peak below 2°C, return below 1.5°C within this century, and settle around 1°C thereafter to limit tipping risks. The same review notes that current policies point toward about 2.6°C.

Note: From January 2023 to September 2025, about 84.4 percent of the world’s coral reef area experienced heat stress strong enough to cause bleaching, and mass bleaching was documented in at least 83 countries and territories.
Context: This was the fourth global bleaching event on record (the others were in 1998, 2010 and 2014–2017) and the largest, beating the previous record of 68.2 percent. NOAA confirmed the event in April 2024 and said in June 2026 that it had likely ended in mid-2025. Heat stress is not the same as death: bleached corals can recover if the water cools in time, and mortality varied widely from reef to reef. The Global Tipping Points Report 2025 used this event, together with warming of about 1.4°C against a central coral threshold of 1.2°C, to conclude that reefs are passing their tipping point and that functional reefs at a meaningful scale require warming to fall back toward 1°C. Others read the same record as severe, recurring damage that reefs can partly recover from, pointing to reefs such as the Great Barrier Reef, where coral cover has swung sharply but has not collapsed.

Context: The question this figure answers is whether each element’s threshold is crossed at some point in the warming path, not whether the element finishes tipping this century; for ice sheets the consequences would unfold over centuries to millennia. The authors found the Barents Sea ice, Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, abrupt permafrost thaw and coral reefs triggered with more than 90 percent probability under this pathway, while the AMOC stayed below 50 percent and East Antarctica below 10 percent. The same paper also found that carbon released by Amazon dieback and permafrost thaw adds relatively little warming: on average about one tenth of human-caused warming or less, raising the average triggering probability by at most 3 percentage points. That second finding is cited by those who argue tipping points will not cause runaway warming; the first, by those who argue current policy is unsafe. Lower-emissions pathways cut the risk substantially.

Label: Projected weakening of the Atlantic overturning circulation by 2100
Value: 51
Unit: percent weakening from 1850–1900 strength (medium-emissions scenario SSP2-4.5)
Viz: ranked
Series: Climate models alone (CMIP6 average) 32; Models constrained by observations 51 (highlight)
Data year: 2026
Source label: Portmann et al., Observational constraints project a ~50% AMOC weakening by the end of this century, Science Advances 12 (2026)
Source URL: https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adx4298
Note: Correcting climate models against real-world observations of Atlantic temperature and salinity, a 2026 study projects the Atlantic overturning circulation will weaken by 51 ± 8 percent by 2100 under middle-of-the-road emissions, against 32 ± 37 percent in the uncorrected models.
Context: The AMOC is the system of currents that carries warm surface water north through the Atlantic and returns cold water south at depth; it keeps northwestern Europe mild and shapes monsoon rainfall in Africa and Asia. A weakening is not a collapse, but a weaker circulation is closer to the point where it could stop sustaining itself. The IPCC’s 2021 assessment said the AMOC is “very likely to weaken over the 21st century” but gave “medium confidence that there will not be an abrupt collapse before 2100.” Direct measurements by the RAPID array since 2004 show a decline of about 1 sverdrup per decade (Carbon Brief). Estimates of collapse risk vary widely: a 2023 statistical study projected a transition most likely around 2057, with a 95 percent interval of 2037 to 2109 after a 2025 correction; a 2025 multi-model study in Nature found collapse this century unlikely; and a June 2026 preprint by Holden, Lenton and colleagues, not yet peer reviewed, put a 10 to 23 percent probability on collapse already being committed, depending on Greenland meltwater assumptions.

Viz: ranked

Data year: 2026
Source label: van Westen, Börner and Dijkstra, Failure to track a stable AMOC state under rapid climate change, Nature Climate Change (13 August 2026)
Source URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-026-02730-w
Note: In a climate model, the Atlantic overturning circulation stayed stable up to 5.5°C of warming when carbon dioxide rose slowly, but collapsed at about 2°C when it rose at a rate comparable to today’s, suggesting the speed of warming matters as much as its level.
Context: The paper’s abstract starts from the consensus central threshold of 4°C (range 1.4 to 8°C) and argues “such a threshold may not be meaningful because AMOC stability depends on the rate of radiative forcing change, not a set temperature.” Under slow warming the whole depth of the ocean has time to adjust, with more evaporation and less sea ice counteracting the freshening that weakens the circulation; under fast warming it does not. The press materials put the critical rate at around 0.3°C per decade, close to the current 0.27°C per decade. It is one model, and the authors’ earlier work has been among the more alarmed in the field; its policy implication, that limiting the rate of warming matters alongside peak temperature, is new to the debate. It also cuts against the idea that a short overshoot of a temperature target is safe for fast-responding systems.

Data year: 2024 (analysis of land-cover data to about 2020)
Source label: Flores et al., Critical transitions in the Amazon forest system, Nature 626 (2024)
Source URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06970-0
Note: The Amazon biome has lost about 13 percent of its original forest to deforestation, or 15 percent counting young secondary forest, beyond the 10 percent the authors propose as a safe limit and approaching the 20 percent at which one model found forest loss accelerating.
Context: The Amazon recycles much of its own rain: moisture evaporated by trees in the east falls again further west, so clearing forest dries the forest downwind. Past a point, that feedback could turn large areas into degraded forest or savanna. Flores and colleagues, 24 authors, estimated that 10 to 47 percent of Amazonian forests will be exposed to compounding disturbances from heat, drought, deforestation and fire by 2050, and proposed safe boundaries of 1.5°C of warming and 10 percent cumulative deforestation, which would require ending large-scale clearing and restoring at least 5 percent of the biome. Beyond the forest cleared outright, about 38 percent of the remaining forest has been degraded by logging, fire, edge effects and drought, and the southeastern Amazon has already become a net source of carbon. Annual clearing in the Brazilian Amazon has more than halved since President Lula da Silva returned to office in 2023, falling to about 5,800 square kilometers in 2025 (Hannah Ritchie, https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/amazon-deforestation-2025), which is part of why the rate of clearing, not just its total, is central to the debate.

Context: The study combines a model of how moisture travels across the Amazon basin with a model of forest stability, so that drying in one place can push forest downwind past its own threshold. Most of the simulated transitions come from these knock-on effects, which the authors describe as “long-ranging and self-propelling cascades on scales of hundreds to thousands of kilometres.” It is why the Amazon threshold in the consensus table (3.5°C) and in this study (1.5 to 1.9°C) differ so much: the lower number assumes deforestation continues. In the scenario used, deforestation rises from about 18 percent of the basin in 2020 to 35 percent in 2050. The authors conclude that warming should be kept below 1.5°C, deforestation halted and degraded forests restored.

Context: Ice-sheet tipping is the slowest and the largest of the tipping elements. Greenland’s threshold is uncertain (0.8 to 3.0°C, central 1.5°C) because the feedback is gradual: as the ice surface melts and lowers, it sits in warmer air and melts faster. West Antarctica’s risk comes from marine ice-sheet instability, in which ice resting on bedrock below sea level retreats faster once warm ocean water gets beneath it. The West Antarctic figure (3.3 meters, over thousands of years) is from Wikipedia’s summary of the literature rather than NSIDC, which gives only whole-ice-sheet totals. Crossing a threshold commits the world to this rise only over centuries to millennia, which is why the debate turns on whether those commitments should be weighed like near-term harms. The IPCC’s 2021 assessment found “limited evidence for low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes” that would strongly increase Antarctic ice loss for centuries under high emissions.

Context: The social cost of carbon is the estimated damage from emitting one more tonne of CO2, used to weigh climate policy against its costs. In this model it rose from about $52 to $65 per tonne. Two elements slightly lower it in the model: the AMOC slowdown, because a weaker circulation cools Europe and offsets some warming damage there, and the Arctic sea-ice albedo feedback. The authors call the result “a probable underestimate,” because the literature they synthesized does not cover all tipping points and the main specification leaves out non-market damages such as ecosystems and health; other economists argued in PNAS that such models understate tipping damages by far more. The US Department of Energy’s 2025 climate review cited the same study to conclude that tipping points do “not justify major revisions” to the social cost of carbon, noting that the IPCC considers the methane-hydrate scenario very unlikely. The same number is read, from each side, as a floor or as a ceiling.

## Questions

Question: Are the major tipping thresholds within reach of this century’s warming?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Already within reach / Mostly beyond reach
Why contested: The threshold estimates carry ranges several degrees wide, and they rest on a mix of paleoclimate records, models that disagree with one another, and expert judgment. Small shifts in where the lower bound sits change the reading from “several tipping points are being crossed now” to “most are unlikely this century.”
Case for “already within reach”: Warming of about 1.4°C already exceeds the central estimate for coral reefs and sits inside the lower end of the ranges for Greenland, West Antarctica, abrupt permafrost thaw and the North Atlantic subpolar gyre. Current policies lead to about 2.6°C, a level at which the February 2026 review finds eight elements likely to tip if warming stabilizes at 3°C and five as likely as not at 2°C. New studies keep moving thresholds down: deforestation lowers the Amazon’s to 1.5 to 1.9°C, and the AMOC may collapse at about 2°C if warming stays fast. Observations back the models: record coral bleaching, a weakening Atlantic circulation, ice sheets losing hundreds of billions of tonnes a year, and a tundra that now emits more carbon than it stores.
Case for “mostly beyond reach”: The central estimates for the systems with the largest global consequences, the AMOC (4°C), the Amazon without deforestation (3.5°C) and East Antarctica (3°C and above), lie beyond current-policy warming, and the IPCC holds medium confidence that the AMOC will not collapse abruptly this century. A 2025 multi-model study in Nature found AMOC collapse unlikely before 2100 even under extreme forcing, and the lower thresholds come from single models, statistical extrapolation or preprints. Several of the systems said to be tipping, such as coral reefs, have been damaged by heat but continue to recover in places, and ice sheets that cross a threshold take centuries to millennia to respond, leaving time for warming to be reversed.

Question: Is “tipping point” the right way to describe how these systems change?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real thresholds / Gradual slopes
Why contested: The term comes from dynamical systems, where a threshold separates two stable states and a small push can flip the system. Critics argue that many of the listed elements respond in proportion to warming, over long timescales, and that the word implies speed, finality and a single line that the physics does not show.
Case for “real thresholds”: Paleoclimate records show abrupt shifts, such as the rapid coolings and warmings of the last ice age linked to changes in the Atlantic circulation and the greening and drying of the Sahara. The feedbacks behind the thresholds are well understood physics: melt lowers an ice surface into warmer air, forest loss reduces the rain that sustains the rest of the forest, fresher water reduces the sinking that drives ocean circulation. Early-warning statistics, such as rising variance and slower recovery from disturbances, have been detected in the Amazon, Greenland and the AMOC. Calling these gradual would hide the fact that, once past a threshold, some changes continue even if warming stops.
Case for “gradual slopes”: Kopp and nine coauthors argued in Nature Climate Change in December 2024 that the framing oversimplifies “the diverse dynamics of complex natural and human systems” and confuses policy benchmarks such as 1.5°C with properties of the climate. Many elements, including permafrost, mountain glaciers and summer sea ice, respond roughly in proportion to warming, and even the threshold elements change over decades to millennia. The Breakthrough Institute’s Seaver Wang wrote that the literature “has not identified any approaching global tipping point after which runaway climate change intensifies beyond humanity’s ability to arrest it.” Talking about climate damage by the degree is more accurate and, on this view, more useful.

Question: Can a temporary overshoot of 1.5°C be reversed before tipping takes hold?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Overshoot recoverable / Overshoot too risky
Why contested: Warming is now expected to pass 1.5°C, so whether a return is still possible matters for every target. Some systems respond slowly enough that a brief overshoot may do no lasting harm; others do not, and the answer depends on how long and how high the overshoot goes, and on carbon removal at a scale that does not yet exist.
Case for “overshoot recoverable”: Ice sheets and some other slow elements take centuries to millennia to respond, so crossing their thresholds briefly does not commit them to collapse; the February 2026 review states that “tipping does not always occur immediately” and that a short enough overshoot can avoid it. Warming largely stops within a few years of reaching net-zero CO2, and net-negative emissions can then bring temperatures down. Planning for a managed overshoot and a return, rather than pretending 1.5°C can still be held, gives policy a target it can meet.
Case for “overshoot too risky”: Fast elements such as coral reefs and possibly the subpolar gyre and the AMOC can tip within the overshoot itself, and the August 2026 AMOC study found collapse depends on the speed of warming, so a fast rise past a threshold can trigger it even if temperatures later fall. A return depends on removing hundreds of billions of tonnes of CO2, which is not yet possible at scale, and some processes, such as the loss of ice or forest, feed back on warming. A 2025 study found the overshoot behavior of tipping elements “still highly uncertain,” which argues for minimizing the overshoot rather than planning on it.

Question: Should the risk of tipping points set the level of climate targets?
Tag: value
Poles: Thresholds set targets / Costs set targets
Why contested: Tipping points turn a smooth damage curve into one with cliffs, and cliffs argue for precaution. Whether to steer by the lowest plausible threshold, or by expected costs and benefits that already include tipping as one risk, is a choice about how to act under deep uncertainty.
Case for “thresholds set targets”: When a loss is irreversible on any human timescale, such as several meters of sea-level rise or the loss of the Amazon, the ordinary logic of trading damages against costs breaks down, because the damages cannot be undone once the costs are known. The tipping-point scientists argue that warming must peak below 2°C, return below 1.5°C within this century and settle near 1°C, because that is what the thresholds imply. Economic models that add tipping points still raise the cost of carbon, with a long tail in which it more than doubles, and the models themselves leave out much of what would be lost.
Case for “costs set targets”: Climate policy has real costs, borne disproportionately by poorer people now, and the thresholds are too uncertain to calculate a line from. The US Department of Energy’s 2025 review argued that because such events may not be predictable, the best stance is to “maximize resilience,” and that tipping points do “not justify major revisions” to the social cost of carbon. A target set by the lowest plausible threshold is already unreachable for coral reefs, which risks discrediting targets generally. Weighing tipping as one risk among many, with costs and benefits, avoids spending on the wrong things in a panic.

Question: Do tipping-point warnings help or hurt climate action?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Warnings motivate / Warnings paralyze
Why contested: This is a question for social science, and the evidence on fear appeals, urgency and fatalism in climate communication is mixed. Both sides cite the same history of missed deadlines.
Case for “warnings motivate”: The Paris Agreement’s 1.5°C goal, the “climate emergency” declarations and the net-zero targets that followed were driven partly by the argument that there are lines not to cross. Irreversible, concrete losses, such as the Amazon or the Gulf Stream, are what make distant risks feel real to the public and to finance ministers. In November 2025 Iceland’s government placed a possible AMOC collapse before its National Security Council as a national security risk, a sign that the warnings reach decision-makers (US News/Reuters, https://www.usnews.com/news/world/articles/2025-11-12/iceland-sees-security-risk-existential-threat-in-atlantic-ocean-currents-possible-collapse). Hiding what scientists see to avoid alarm would be its own distortion.
Case for “warnings paralyze”: Kopp and colleagues argue that the deep uncertainty and abstractness of tipping points “render them ineffective for triggering action and setting governance goals.” Seaver Wang argued that if the planet has “only seven years” left, any policy that takes longer is by definition pointless, which pushes people toward either denial or fatalism. Predictions that did not come true, such as early obituaries for the Great Barrier Reef, are used to discredit climate science more broadly. Surveys of young people find high levels of climate anxiety, which some researchers link to doom framing.

Question: Should governments research ways to cool the climate or hold back specific tipping points directly?
Tag: value
Poles: Research interventions / Rule them out
Why contested: Solar geoengineering, marine cloud brightening, sea-ice thickening and seabed curtains to shield glaciers might buy time for specific systems, but they carry their own risks, could distract from emissions cuts, and raise questions of who decides for the whole planet.
Case for “research interventions”: If some tipping points are already being crossed and emissions cuts cannot reverse warming quickly, the only way to protect those systems in time may be to act on them directly, and decisions about such tools should rest on evidence rather than guesswork. The UK’s Advanced Research and Invention Agency is funding £56.8 million of research, including small outdoor experiments, to test whether approaches to delay or avert tipping points are feasible and safe. John Moore and colleagues argue that refusing to research interventions has its own moral hazard, leaving the world with no options if a collapse begins.
Case for “rule them out”: Forty-two polar scientists concluded in September 2025 that five proposed polar geoengineering concepts “fail to meet the essential criteria required for them to be considered responsible approaches,” being environmentally dangerous and unworkable at the needed scale. Solar geoengineering would have to be maintained for decades or centuries, could shift monsoons and rainfall, and would face “termination shock” if stopped. Researching it lends legitimacy and may weaken the pressure to cut emissions, and there is no governance system to decide on deploying it. A group of scholars and governments favors a non-use agreement.

Question: Would tipping in one system trigger tipping in others?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Cascades likely / Mostly independent
Why contested: Tipping elements are linked: Greenland meltwater weakens the AMOC, a weaker AMOC shifts rainfall over the Amazon and West Africa, and Amazon and permafrost carbon adds warming. Whether these links make a chain reaction likely, or whether their effects are small next to direct human emissions, is not settled.
Case for “cascades likely”: The 2018 “Hothouse Earth” paper argued that linked feedbacks could push the planet onto a hotter path that no longer depends on human emissions, and network studies since have found that interactions lower the effective thresholds of many elements. The May 2026 Amazon study found that knock-on drying spreads forest loss over hundreds to thousands of kilometers, a cascade within one system. The June 2026 AMOC preprint found that the probability of committed collapse depends on how much Greenland is melting, a direct cross-system link.
Case for “mostly independent”: The largest links operate over centuries, and a 2025 study found that carbon released by Amazon dieback and permafrost thaw adds a median warming an order of magnitude smaller than human-caused warming, raising average triggering probabilities by at most 3 percentage points. Some links are stabilizing rather than destabilizing: a weaker AMOC cools the North Atlantic, which may slow Greenland melt. The climate scientist Andrew Dessler wrote in March 2026 that a true runaway greenhouse “is simply not in the cards on any time horizon we care about,” and a 2023 review led by Seaver Wang found that emissions pathways and climate model uncertainties may matter more than tipping elements in determining multi-century warming.

Question: Can “positive tipping points” in technology and society move fast enough to matter?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Self-reinforcing change / Slow and reversible
Why contested: Supporters argue clean technologies spread by the same kind of reinforcing feedbacks as tipping in nature, so small policy pushes can trigger large shifts. Critics say adoption depends on subsidies, politics and infrastructure that can stall or reverse.
Case for “self-reinforcing change”: Solar and wind are now the cheapest new power in most of the world, and one in four new cars sold in 2025 was electric, 55 percent in China, trends that accelerated faster than most forecasts. The Global Tipping Points Report 2025 said positive tipping points have been crossed in solar and wind worldwide and in electric vehicles, batteries and heat pumps in leading markets, and a September 2026 Exeter and Earthshot Prize report identified 51 solutions that could reach self-propelling change within five years. Targeted mandates and cost parity can tip whole markets, which is faster than persuading every consumer.
Case for “slow and reversible”: Global emissions are still rising, and current policies still lead to about 2.6°C. Electric car sales in the United States stayed just under 10 percent and dropped when tax credits ended, showing adoption can depend on subsidies that can be withdrawn. Steel, cement, aviation, shipping and farming have no clean option at cost parity. Social systems have politics, and a shift that looks self-reinforcing in one market can stall in another.

## Positions

Camp: Cut emissions fast enough to stay below tipping thresholds
Adherents: University of Exeter Global Systems Institute and the Global Tipping Points Report 2025, 160 authors from 87 institutions, which concludes that Earth is passing its first tipping point and calls for halving emissions by 2030, fossil-fuel phase-out mandates and net zero by 2050 (https://global-tipping-points.org/); Paul Ritchie, Norman Steinert, Nico Wunderling and 25 coauthors from Exeter, the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research and CICERO, who conclude that “global warming must peak below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels, return to below 1.5 °C as quickly as possible (i.e. within this century), and to around 1 °C thereafter to limit tipping point risks” (Environmental Research Letters, February 2026, https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae3cad); Timothy Lenton, who told New Scientist of the AMOC preprint, “Let’s do everything in our power to get to net-zero as quickly as possible to try to keep this probability down at the 10 percent level” (Yale Environment 360, https://e360.yale.edu/digest/amoc-collapse-10-percent).
Case: The Earth system has thresholds, and we are at them. The best estimates put coral reefs past theirs and Greenland, West Antarctica, permafrost and the subpolar gyre within range at today’s 1.4°C. Each of these, once tipped, commits the world to losses that no later policy can reverse: meters of sea-level rise, the end of reefs that feed hundreds of millions, the drying of the Amazon, a colder, stormier Europe and failed monsoons. The asymmetry is the argument. If we cut emissions faster than needed, we pay a cost we can measure and recover from; if we cut too slowly, we lose things that are gone for thousands of years. So the thresholds, not a cost-benefit curve, should set the targets: peak below 2°C, return below 1.5°C as fast as possible, and aim for about 1°C in the long run. That means halving emissions this decade, ending deforestation, and building the capacity to draw carbon down. There is reason for hope as well as alarm: the same reinforcing feedbacks that make the Earth tip also make clean technologies spread, and solar, wind and electric vehicles have already passed their own tipping points in leading markets. Policy can trigger more of these.
Best evidence: Current warming already exceeds the central coral threshold of 1.2°C, and NOAA recorded bleaching-level heat stress on 84.4 percent of reef area in 2023–2025. A 2025 study found a 62 percent average probability of triggering tipping elements under current-policy warming, with nine above 50 percent. Observations match the model warnings: a weakening Atlantic circulation, Greenland losing about 266 billion tonnes of ice a year, a tundra that has become a carbon source, and an Amazon whose southeast now emits more carbon than it absorbs. The 2026 studies keep moving thresholds lower, not higher.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that the thresholds are too uncertain to build targets on and that alarm breeds fatalism. Uncertainty cuts both ways: the ranges extend below the central estimates as far as above them, and when the downside is irreversible, uncertainty is a reason for more caution, not less. Most tipping would unfold slowly, but that is what makes the next decade decisive, because it is when the commitments are made. As for fatalism, the message is not that it is too late; every tenth of a degree avoided lowers the odds, and positive tipping points show that fast change is possible.
Variation: Some in this camp emphasize the rate of warming as well as its peak, following the August 2026 AMOC study; some put most weight on positive social and technological tipping points, as in the September 2026 Exeter and Earthshot Prize report (https://news.exeter.ac.uk/faculty-of-environment-science-and-economy/the-new-tipping-points-playbook-the-earthshot-prize-and-the-university-of-exeter-reveal-how-to-make-environmental-repair-unstoppable/); and a minority go further, arguing that some tipping is already committed and that targeted research on interventions should proceed alongside cuts, which overlaps with the interventions camp below.

Camp: Weigh tipping risks by expected cost and build resilience
Adherents: The US Department of Energy’s Climate Working Group (John Christy, Judith Curry, Steven Koonin, Ross McKitrick and Roy Spencer), whose July 2025 report concluded that “consideration of potential tipping points does not justify major revisions to SCC estimates” and that, if unpredictable tipping points exist, “the most appropriate stance for economic policy is to maximize resilience” (https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2025-07/DOE_Critical_Review_of_Impacts_of_GHG_Emissions_on_the_US_Climate_July_2025.pdf); Bjorn Lomborg, president of the Copenhagen Consensus Center, who wrote in September 2026 that the Great Barrier Reef “refuses to die on schedule” and that “crying wolf has a well-known ending” (Business Day, https://www.businessday.co.za/opinion/2026-09-17-bjorn-lomborg-the-great-barrier-reef-refuses-to-die-on-schedule/).
Case: Climate change is real and some of its risks are serious, but policy should be set by weighing costs against benefits, and tipping points do not change that arithmetic as much as the headlines suggest. When economists add tipping points to their models, the social cost of carbon rises by about a quarter, not by orders of magnitude, and most of that comes from scenarios the IPCC itself judges very unlikely. What gets called a tipping point is a moving target, from 10 systems to more than 25, and the thresholds are guesses with ranges several degrees wide. The IPCC has medium confidence that the Atlantic circulation will not collapse this century and found no tipping point in Arctic sea ice. Meanwhile the policies justified by emergency language are expensive, fall hardest on poor people and poor countries, and are often ineffective. Predictions of imminent collapse, such as the obituaries for the Great Barrier Reef, keep failing, and each failure costs science credibility. If there are abrupt changes we cannot predict, the sensible answer is to make societies richer and more resilient, adapt, and invest in cheaper clean energy through innovation, not to organize everything around thresholds nobody can locate.
Best evidence: Dietz and colleagues’ PNAS model found tipping points raise the expected social cost of carbon by 24.5 percent. The IPCC’s 2021 assessment gives medium confidence against an abrupt AMOC collapse before 2100, and a 2025 multi-model study in Nature found collapse unlikely this century. The Great Barrier Reef recorded some of its highest coral cover in four decades in 2022–2024 after earlier forecasts of its death, and AIMS reports cover close to its 40-year average in 2026 despite the 2024 bleaching. A 2025 study found carbon feedbacks from tipping add only about a tenth as much warming as human emissions.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that a cost-benefit model cannot price irreversible catastrophe, so it understates the risk. But the alternative, steering by the lowest plausible threshold, gives no stopping rule and already demands the impossible for coral reefs. Deep uncertainty is not a license to assume the worst; it is a reason to buy resilience that pays off whatever happens, such as wealth, sea defenses, crop research and cheaper energy, rather than to bet everything on precise thresholds that the science cannot pin down.
Variation: The camp runs from those who doubt that warming will be large, such as some Climate Working Group authors, to those, like Lomborg, who accept mainstream warming projections but argue for a moderate carbon price and heavy spending on clean-energy research. Critics note that the Climate Working Group report was rebutted by more than 85 climate scientists as “biased, full of errors, and not fit to inform policymaking” (https://essopenarchive.org/doi/full/10.22541/essoar.175745244.41950365/v2) and that a federal judge ruled in 2026 that the group had been convened in violation of the Federal Advisory Committee Act, though the report was left in the record (C&EN, https://cen.acs.org/policy/litigation/doe-climate-working-group-illegal/104/web/2026/02).

Camp: Describe climate risk in degrees, not tipping points
Adherents: Robert Kopp, Elisabeth Gilmore, Michael Oppenheimer and seven coauthors, several of them IPCC authors, who argued in Nature Climate Change in December 2024 that the tipping-point framing oversimplifies complex systems and conveys “urgency without fostering a meaningful basis for climate action,” and called for “clearer, more specific language” (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-024-02196-8); the Breakthrough Institute, whose climate director Seaver Wang wrote that “the climate science literature has not identified any approaching global tipping point after which runaway climate change intensifies beyond humanity’s ability to arrest it” (https://thebreakthrough.org/journal/climate-change-banned-words/climate-tipping-point-real).
Case: We take climate risk seriously, including the chance of large, hard-to-reverse changes, which is exactly why we want to describe it accurately. Most of what is called a tipping point is not a cliff: permafrost thaws in proportion to warming, glaciers shrink steadily, and even the ice sheets that could pass a threshold would take centuries to millennia to respond. The word implies a single line, a sudden fall and a point of no return, and people hear it as “it’s too late” or as a deadline that, once missed, discredits the science. It also confuses policy targets like 1.5°C, which are choices, with properties of the climate system. The truth is that every tenth of a degree matters, damage rises steadily with warming, and some changes accelerate past uncertain thresholds. That argues for cutting emissions as fast as practical and for adapting, without fatalism or false precision. Talk about specific impacts, their probabilities and their timescales, and people can plan around them.
Best evidence: The threshold ranges span several degrees, and the timescales run from a decade for coral to 10,000 years for Greenland, so a single “tipping point” label covers very different kinds of change. Social science reviewed by Kopp and colleagues finds that abstract, deeply uncertain risks are poor at motivating action or setting governance goals. The 2025 study that finds high triggering probabilities also finds that tipping-driven carbon feedbacks add only about a tenth as much warming as human emissions. Wang’s 2023 review of ten tipping elements found emissions pathways and model uncertainty may matter more than tipping elements for multi-century warming.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that softening the language hides real thresholds and removes the urgency that drives action. We do not deny thresholds; we ask that each be described for what it is, with its probability and timescale. Urgency built on a claimed cliff collapses when the date passes, while urgency built on steadily rising damage lasts. We support fast emissions cuts; we disagree that a contested metaphor is the best way to win them.

Camp: Research interventions that could hold off specific tipping points
Adherents: The UK’s Advanced Research and Invention Agency, whose £56.8 million Exploring Climate Cooling program funds research, including a limited number of small, controlled outdoor experiments, on whether approaches to “delay, or avert, climate tipping points” are feasible and safe (https://www.aria.org.uk/exploring-climate-cooling/); John Moore, Marc Macias-Fauria and Michael Wolovick, who argue for a “harm-reduction paradigm” in which scientists research Arctic interventions alongside decarbonization and ask, “how should decisions be made — through evidence or guesswork?” (Frontiers in Science, September 2025, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/science/articles/10.3389/fsci.2025.1657323/full); the Centre for Climate Repair at Cambridge, founded by the former UK chief scientific adviser Sir David King, whose mission is to “reduce emissions, remove greenhouse gases and refreeze the Arctic. Each is essential; none is sufficient on its own” (https://www.climaterepair.cam.ac.uk/mission).
Case: Emissions cuts are essential, but they cannot cool the planet on the timescale some tipping points demand. CO2 stays in the air for centuries, warming will overshoot 1.5°C, and coral reefs are already past their threshold. If the Arctic, the Greenland ice sheet or the Atlantic circulation are close to tipping, the only tools that could act in time are ones that act on those systems directly: brightening clouds over the Arctic, thickening sea ice, shielding glaciers from warm ocean water, or reflecting a little sunlight. None of them is ready, and some may prove unworkable or dangerous. That is the reason to research them now, carefully and openly, so that if a crisis comes, decisions rest on evidence rather than on desperation. Refusing to study them does not make them go away; it only guarantees that any future use would be uninformed.
Best evidence: The thresholds for coral reefs, and possibly for Greenland, West Antarctica and the subpolar gyre, lie at or below today’s warming, and the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget is about three years of emissions, so overshoot is now expected. Temperatures fall only slowly after net-zero, and carbon removal at the scale needed does not yet exist. The 2026 AMOC study finds the rate of warming decisive, which is something only a fast-acting intervention could change once emissions have peaked.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection, made by 42 polar scientists in Frontiers in Science, is that polar geoengineering concepts are “environmentally dangerous,” unworkable at scale and a distraction from emissions cuts. We agree that most concepts may fail and that none should be deployed without evidence and consent, above all from Arctic peoples; that is what research is for. The moral hazard runs both ways: if research is never done, the world faces a collapse with no options, which Moore and colleagues call “the moral hazard of non-research.” Research is funded alongside decarbonization, never instead of it.
Variation: Proposals range from local, reversible measures (sea-ice thickening, marine cloud brightening over the Arctic, underwater curtains at glacier fronts) to global solar radiation modification. Most supporters, ARIA among them, fund research only and set no deployment plan; the Centre for Climate Repair frames its work as preparing for eventual deployment decisions, which it says “should not be taken unilaterally” and “demand anticipatory governance.”

Camp: Prepare communities for societal collapse
Adherents: The Deep Adaptation Forum, which exists to foster “mutual support and collaboration in the process of anticipating, observing, and experiencing societal disruption and collapse” (https://www.deepadaptation.info/); Jem Bendell, author of the 2018 paper Deep Adaptation and the 2023 book Breaking Together, who writes that “societal collapse is no longer a distant possibility but a lived reality for many” (https://jembendell.com/2025/10/08/summary-of-breaking-together/).
Case: The official story, that we can still keep warming to safe levels if we try harder, has been told for thirty years while emissions kept rising. Self-reinforcing feedbacks in the Arctic, in permafrost and in the oceans are under way, and they are converging with crises in energy, food, finance and politics. Honesty requires admitting that some form of societal breakdown is likely or already happening in many places, and that mitigation, while still worth doing, will not prevent it. The task then is to prepare: build local resilience, food and mutual aid, let go of what cannot be kept, and resist the authoritarian responses that crises invite. Facing this is not giving up. It frees people to act on what matters, with dignity and compassion, instead of on a hope that keeps being disappointed.
Best evidence: Emissions have kept rising after three decades of climate diplomacy, the 1.5°C budget is nearly spent, and current policies lead to about 2.6°C. Tipping elements such as coral reefs are already passing their thresholds, and the Arctic tundra has become a carbon source. Record heat, crop failures, fires and floods are already destabilizing vulnerable regions.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that the science does not support the claim that collapse is inevitable, and that saying so spreads despair and undermines action, as climate scientists argued in a 2020 critique of the original paper (openDemocracy, https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/oureconomy/faulty-science-doomism-and-flawed-conclusions-deep-adaptation/). Bendell revised the paper in 2020 to say collapse cannot be proven certain, and the case does not depend on certainty: preparing for breakdown is prudent if it is likely, and the preparations, stronger communities and local resilience, are good whether or not it comes. Despair comes from false hope colliding with reality, not from honest acknowledgment.

## Crux

The question under the others: does crossing a tipping threshold in this century commit the world to large, irreversible losses that a degree-by-degree weighing of costs and benefits would fail to capture?

If it does, thresholds should set the targets, overshoot is dangerous, research on direct interventions looks prudent, and the tipping-point frame is accurate rather than alarmist. If it does not, because the thresholds are far, the changes slow, or the losses recoverable, then tipping is one risk among many, best handled by the same steady cuts, adaptation and resilience as the rest of climate change, and the word itself may mislead.

What evidence could settle: where the thresholds actually lie and how wide their ranges really are; how fast each system responds once past one, and whether a brief overshoot can be reversed before it takes hold; whether the speed of warming, not only its level, sets thresholds, as the 2026 AMOC study suggests; how much tipping in one system raises the odds in others and how much extra warming carbon feedbacks add; and whether the early-warning signals now reported in the Atlantic circulation, the Amazon and Greenland are real or statistical artifacts. Continued observation of the AMOC, ice-sheet mass, Amazon rainfall and reef recovery will narrow these over the coming decade, though some answers may arrive only after the thresholds have been crossed.

What no data can close: how much weight to give a loss that is unlikely but irreversible, and that falls mostly on people who are far away or not yet born, against costs that are certain and borne now. Whether to act on the lowest plausible threshold or on the expected value, whether to use a vivid frame that may motivate or a precise one that may not, and whether to research tools that could protect a system but carry their own risks, are choices about precaution, fairness across generations and what counts as responsible under deep uncertainty. People who agree on every number can still answer them differently.
