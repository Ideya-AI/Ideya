# Immigration & Borders — research

## Overview

Immigration and borders, as this topic uses the terms, is the question of how many people a state lets in, through which doors and on what conditions, and what it does about people who arrive or stay without permission. It covers the size and mix of legal immigration (work, family, study and refugee resettlement), the right to claim asylum at or inside a border, border enforcement, detention and deportation, and the underlying question of whether people have any right to move and settle where they choose, or whether each political community may decide who joins it. It deliberately excludes what happens after admission — integration, assimilation, language, multiculturalism and national identity — which belongs to the related topic on assimilation and multiculturalism, and it touches citizenship rules such as birthright citizenship only where they bear on who is admitted. Most of the evidence comes from the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom and Canada, where the fight is most visible and the data best, with global figures for scale.

Why now: on 12 June 2026 the European Union’s Pact on Migration and Asylum, a package of ten laws adopted in 2024, began to apply in every member state. It brings mandatory screening and registration of irregular arrivals, a fast-track procedure held at the border for applicants judged unlikely to qualify, faster returns, and a standing “solidarity” mechanism under which member states either accept relocated asylum seekers or pay (European Commission, https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/new-migration-and-asylum-rules-enter-application-what-changing-2026-06-12_en). Five days later, on 17 June 2026, the European Parliament approved a new Return Regulation by 418 votes to 218, with 30 abstentions, which allows rejected applicants to be sent to “return hubs” in countries outside the EU and extends entry bans to up to 20 years (RTÉ, https://www.rte.ie/news/politics/2026/0617/1578882-eu-international-protection/). On 2 September 2026 Amnesty International reported that Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands were negotiating such hubs in Rwanda and Uganda (https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/09/migrant-return-hubs-risk-becoming-human-rights-black-hole/). In the United States the turn has been sharper still. The Census Bureau reported in January 2026 that net international migration fell from 2.7 million in the year to mid-2024 to 1.3 million in the year to mid-2025 and projected about 321,000 for the year after (https://census.gov/newsroom/blogs/random-samplings/2026/01/historic-decline-in-net-international-migration.html), and economists at Brookings and the American Enterprise Institute estimated net migration for calendar 2025 at between −295,000 and −10,000, the first negative year in at least half a century (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/macroeconomic-implications-of-immigration-flows-in-2025-and-2026-january-2026-update/).

The debate is live because the rich democracies have just run the experiment both ways. Between 2021 and 2024 the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and much of Europe admitted people at or near record rates: the UK’s net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year to March 2023, US Border Patrol encounters at the southwest border topped 2 million a year in fiscal 2022 and 2023, and Canada’s population grew faster than at any time since the 1950s. Voters reacted, and governments of left and right have since cut hard, so that border crossings, asylum claims and net migration are now falling almost everywhere at once. The same period gave each side new evidence. Restrictionists point to how fast crossings fell once policy changed, which undercuts the claim that migration cannot be controlled. Supporters of immigration point to the economic and fiscal gains estimated for the surge years, and to the shrinking workforces that aging societies now face. Human rights groups point to deaths on the routes, to detention at record levels in the United States, and to plans for offshore processing and return hubs in Europe. The participants include national governments and parties across the spectrum, from the Trump administration and Reform UK to Denmark’s Social Democrats and Italy’s Giorgia Meloni; employers and business groups; unions; immigrant and refugee advocates, churches and human rights organizations; the UN refugee and migration agencies; restriction-minded think tanks such as the Center for Immigration Studies and pro-immigration ones such as the Cato Institute; economists who study wages and public finances; and moral philosophers who ask whether borders can be justified at all.

The headline figure is therefore a reminder of scale in both directions: movement across borders is large in absolute terms and has grown fast in some destinations, yet the great majority of people live and die in the country of their birth.

Context: Net international migration is arrivals minus departures, counting everyone regardless of legal status. The bureau attributes the decline to fewer arrivals (fewer refugees and far fewer people released at the border) and more departures, and notes that its estimates are “trending toward negative net migration,” which would be the first time in more than 50 years. The White House describes 2025 as the first year of negative net migration in at least half a century and says more than 605,000 people were deported and 1.9 million “self-deported” since January 2025 (https://www.whitehouse.gov/priorities/border-immigration/); the self-deportation figure is the administration’s own estimate and independent researchers have said it is too early to verify. Because the Census Bureau’s January release projected, rather than measured, the fall below zero, the size of the reversal will be settled only by later data.

Note: The Border Patrol recorded about 238,000 encounters at the US-Mexico border in fiscal 2025, the fewest since 1970 and roughly a tenth of the record 2.2 million in fiscal 2022.
Context: This series counts Border Patrol apprehensions between ports of entry, and from fiscal 2020 also the pandemic-era Title 42 expulsions, together called “encounters”; it excludes people who presented themselves at official ports of entry, including those using the CBP One appointment app in 2023 and 2024. A single person caught several times counts several times, which inflated the 2021 and 2022 totals when expelled migrants retried quickly. The collapse happened in two steps: a June 2024 rule restricting asylum between ports cut crossings by roughly half, and the Trump administration’s January 2025 measures (suspending asylum access at the border, ending releases, deploying troops and reinstating “Remain in Mexico”) cut them to under 10,000 a month from February 2025 onward. About 72 percent of fiscal 2025 apprehensions occurred in the 111 days before the change of administration. CBP’s own release gives the fiscal 2025 total as 237,565, against 201,780 in fiscal 1970 (https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/national-media-release/lowest-fiscal-year-border-patrol-apprehensions-1970); the small difference from Pew’s figure reflects data revisions. For restrictionists this is the proof that the border can be controlled by policy; for critics, the question is what became of the people who would have crossed, and at what cost to the right of asylum.

Context: More than half of the world’s forcibly displaced never cross a border: 68.7 million were internally displaced within their own countries. Of the 41.6 million refugees, 28.5 million were under UNHCR’s mandate, 6 million were Palestine refugees under UNRWA’s mandate and 7.2 million were other people in need of international protection, mostly Venezuelans. The total fell about 4 percent in 2025, driven by the largest wave of returns UNHCR has recorded — about 4.4 million refugees and 10.3 million internally displaced people went home, many of them to Syria and some under pressure, as with Afghans expelled from Iran and Pakistan. Colombia, Germany and Türkiye were the largest hosts, with 2.8, 2.7 and 2.4 million. The rich-world asylum debate therefore concerns a minority of the world’s displaced, which each side reads differently: as proof that rich countries do little, or as proof that protection is best provided near home. UNHCR’s own site blocked automated retrieval; these figures were confirmed against coverage of the report by Al Jazeera (https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/6/11/one-in-70-people-worldwide-is-forcibly-displaced-unhcr) and ABC News (https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/118-million-people-displaced-conflict-persecution-year-133774985). Note: In June 2026, 29 percent of US adults said immigration should be decreased, down from 55 percent in 2024; 35 percent wanted it kept at the present level and 31 percent wanted it increased.
Context: The question is Gallup’s long-running “should immigration be kept at its present level, increased or decreased?” The swing from 55 percent wanting less in 2024 to 30 percent in 2025 and 29 percent in 2026 is one of the fastest reversals in the series, and it tracks events: the desire for less immigration climbed as border crossings surged after 2021 and fell back once they dropped. Political scientists call this “thermostatic” opinion, in which the public leans against whatever direction policy is moving. A second Gallup question, whether immigration is “a good thing or a bad thing” for the country, stood at 73 percent good in 2026, down from a record 79 percent in 2025, with Republicans falling 14 points to 50 percent. On specific policies, 75 percent favored a path to citizenship for immigrants in the country illegally and 81 percent for those brought as children, while 58 percent opposed deporting all of them. The figures show broad support for immigration in the abstract combined with intense concern when the system appears out of control, a combination every camp claims as its mandate.

Context: The UK is the clearest case of a rich democracy that ran net migration at record levels and then reversed it. Source label: Michael Clemens, “Economics and Emigration: Trillion-Dollar Bills on the Sidewalk?”, Journal of Economic Perspectives 25(3), 2011, Table 1
Source URL: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.25.3.83
Note: Four economic models estimate that eliminating all barriers to migration would raise world output by between 67 and 147 percent, far more than removing all remaining barriers to trade or capital flows, which they put at a few percent at most.
Context: The logic is that the same worker is several times more productive, and paid several times more, in a rich country than in a poor one, so moving labor to where it is most productive raises total output. Clemens’s survey found the estimated gains from removing migration barriers “often in the range of 50–150 percent of world GDP,” against less than a few percent for trade and capital, and argued that even the emigration of under 5 percent of poor countries’ populations would yield more than abolishing all trade barriers. The estimates are from stylized models, and the gains from complete elimination require “epic movements of people” — at least half the population of poor countries moving to rich ones. They assume that institutions, productivity and social trust in destination countries would survive such a movement unchanged, which is precisely what critics such as Paul Collier and George Borjas dispute. An ungated version of the paper is at https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/132541/file_Clemens_Economics_and_Emigration_FINAL.pdf.

Press reports put the OBR’s lifetime figures at a £151,000 net cost for a low-wage migrant and a £925,000 net contribution for a high-wage one [unverified; read from chart data by the press, not stated in the OBR text].

Suspect counts are not convictions, and comparisons across countries are fragile.

## Questions

Question: Does immigration hold down the wages and job prospects of the least-skilled workers already in the country?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Wages suffer / Wages hold
Why contested: Everyone agrees that immigration raises total output and that most native workers are unaffected or gain in the long run; the fight is over the workers who compete most directly with new arrivals, and the answer depends on which natural experiment, which comparison group and which time horizon an economist trusts. The National Academies’ 2016 review found “little to no” long-run effect on native wages overall, with any losses concentrated on earlier immigrants and people without a high school diploma, which leaves the size of those losses open.
Case for “Wages suffer”: Supply and demand still apply to labor. When a large number of workers with a particular skill arrive at once, employers can fill jobs at lower pay, and the people who feel it are those doing the same work: earlier immigrants, workers without a high school diploma, and in the United States disproportionately Black and Hispanic men. George Borjas’s reanalysis of the 1980 Mariel boatlift, which added about 7 percent to Miami’s workforce in a few months, found steep wage declines for non-Hispanic men without a high school education, and his national studies comparing workers across skill groups find that a 10 percent increase in supply lowers wages in that group by several percent. Studies that find no effect often compare cities, but workers and capital move between cities, spreading the impact nationally and hiding it. Employers who lobby for more visas are telling the truth about their interests: they want more workers at lower cost.
Case for “Wages hold”: Immigrants are also consumers who create demand, entrepreneurs who start firms and workers who complement natives rather than replace them, pushing native workers into better-paid jobs that require language and local knowledge. David Card’s original study of the Mariel boatlift found no measurable effect on Miami wages, Giovanni Peri and Vasil Yasenov found the same using a synthetic comparison group, and critics showed that Borjas’s result depended on a very small sample whose makeup changed when the survey’s methods changed. Across many studies the long-run effect on average native wages is near zero or positive, and the losses for the least skilled, where found, are small compared with other forces such as automation and trade. Restricting immigration to protect a small group’s wages is an expensive way to help them compared with direct transfers or training.

Question: Do immigrants pay more in taxes than they cost in public spending?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Net contributors / Net cost
Why contested: The answer depends on who is admitted, how long they stay, whether their children are counted, which level of government is measured, and how shared costs such as defense and interest on the debt are allocated. Estimates range from strongly positive for skilled workers to strongly negative for low-skilled arrivals and their families, and advocates on each side can choose a methodology that favors their conclusion.
Case for “Net contributors”: Immigrants usually arrive as working-age adults whose schooling another country has paid for, so they begin contributing almost at once. The Congressional Budget Office projected that the 2022–2026 US immigration surge would cut federal deficits by about $0.9 trillion over 2024 to 2034. Britain’s Office for Budget Responsibility found that a migrant arriving at 25 on average earnings becomes a net contributor in their second year, while a native-born 25-year-old does not do so until their forties. The children of immigrants are among the strongest fiscal contributors of any group in the National Academies’ estimates. In aging societies with pay-as-you-go pensions and more retirees per worker every year, working-age arrivals are the cheapest way to keep the numbers adding up, and the US population would begin shrinking in 2030 without them, on CBO’s projection.
Case for “Net cost”: Averages hide the distribution. Immigrants with little education earn less, pay less tax and draw more on means-tested programs, schools and emergency care over their lives, and the OBR found that a low-wage migrant becomes a net cost relative to the average resident by their early forties. Studies in Denmark and the Netherlands that follow immigrants over their whole lives find large lifetime net costs for arrivals from some regions, mostly through lower employment. CBO’s estimate counts only the federal budget, while the costs of schooling, housing and health care fall on states and cities, as the shelter crises in New York, Chicago and Denver showed. Immigrants also age, so the demographic benefit is a one-time bonus that requires ever more immigration to sustain. Selection, not volume, determines whether immigration pays.

Question: How much weight should a country give the interests of people who want to come, compared with those of its own citizens?
Tag: value
Poles: Citizens first / Equal moral weight
Why contested: The gains from migration go overwhelmingly to the migrants themselves, whose incomes can rise several times over, while the costs, where they exist, fall on residents. A policy judged by its effect on citizens alone and one judged by its effect on everyone affected can reach opposite answers from the same evidence, and no evidence can say which yardstick is right.
Case for “Citizens first”: A government is the agent of its own people. Citizens fund it, obey its laws and are bound to one another by obligations of mutual aid that they do not have to the whole world, just as parents rightly favor their own children without denying that other children matter. A welfare state, public schools and a shared labor market are collective goods built by one community, and that community may decide whom to admit to them. Foreign aid, trade and help to refugees in their region can do more for the world’s poor per dollar than admitting a relatively small number of them to rich countries. A government that ranks the interests of foreigners equally with its own citizens’ loses the consent on which any immigration system depends.
Case for “Equal moral weight”: Where someone is born is a matter of luck, and it is the single largest determinant of their lifetime income. A worker in Haiti or Nigeria can multiply their earnings by moving, which no aid program comes close to matching, and the people turned away bear the heaviest costs of the policy while having no vote on it. Even if states may give their citizens some priority, they may not treat enormous gains to foreigners as worth nothing when weighed against small costs to citizens, any more than a town may bar newcomers to protect local wages. Rich countries can admit many more people while compensating those residents who lose, and the fact that they don’t is a choice, not a necessity.

Question: Can enforcement reduce irregular migration durably, or does it only divert people to other routes and greater dangers?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Deterrence works / Pressure diverts
Why contested: The United States cut crossings at its southern border by about 90 percent in 2025, and the EU cut crossings on routes where it struck deals with transit countries, which looks like decisive evidence. But the push factors of war, poverty and climate remain, the long-run effects are unknown, and each side reads the same episodes differently: as proof of control, or as displacement that hides costs elsewhere.
Case for “Deterrence works”: The events of 2024 and 2025 settled the old claim that irregular migration is beyond the control of governments. Border Patrol encounters at the southwest border fell from over 2 million in fiscal 2022 and 2023 to about 238,000 in fiscal 2025, the fewest since 1970, once asylum at the border was suspended, releases ended and returns became swift and predictable. Migrants and smugglers respond to the odds of getting in and staying; when those odds fall, most do not set out, which also saves lives on the journey. Europe’s experience is similar: crossings fell where transit countries cooperated. What drove the surges was not the state of the world but the signal that arrival meant admission.
Case for “Pressure diverts”: Falls in crossings on one route are often followed by rises on another: when the Western Balkans and Atlantic routes closed, crossings from Libya to Crete tripled. The people deterred have not stopped needing protection; many are stranded in Mexico, Libya or Tunisia in conditions human rights groups document as abusive, and the policies depend on paying governments with poor records to stop them. Deaths on the routes remain near record levels. US crossings also fell under the previous administration after its 2024 asylum restriction, and they rose and fell in waves long before, so policy is one factor among many. Enforcement that relies on detention and suspending asylum has costs in money, law and human rights that the crossing numbers do not show, and whether the fall lasts beyond one administration is not yet known.

Question: Should anyone who reaches a country’s territory be able to claim asylum there, or should states choose whom to protect, with claims processed elsewhere?
Tag: value
Poles: Territorial asylum / Offshore selection
Why contested: The 1951 Refugee Convention and human rights law forbid returning anyone to persecution, and in practice have meant that a person who reaches a country’s soil can claim protection there. Governments across Europe, Britain, Australia and the United States now argue that this rewards those able to pay smugglers and makes numbers uncontrollable, and are building offshore processing, return hubs and safe-country rules. Opponents say these hollow out the right itself. Both sides agree persecuted people deserve protection; they disagree about who chooses them and where.
Case for “Territorial asylum”: The right to seek asylum exists precisely because persecuted people cannot wait in an orderly queue: they flee with what they have, and most have no legal way to travel. A state that turns them back without a hearing, or sends them to a third country with a poor human rights record, risks returning them to danger, which is exactly what the post-war legal order was built to prevent. Offshore schemes have been expensive failures: Britain’s Rwanda plan cost hundreds of millions of pounds and removed almost no one before it was scrapped, and Italy’s centers in Albania were largely blocked by courts. Of the world’s refugees, about two-thirds live in countries neighboring their own; rich countries receiving a small fraction of them can afford fair, fast hearings at home.
Case for “Offshore selection”: The current system selects for the young, the male and those able to pay smugglers, not for the most vulnerable, and it lets smugglers rather than voters decide how many arrive. In Europe about half of asylum applications come from nationalities with low recognition rates, and most rejected applicants are never returned, so the asylum channel has become a route for economic migration that undermines public support for protection itself. Processing claims outside the territory, returning rejected applicants to third countries and resettling refugees selected abroad would break the smugglers’ business model and let democracies be generous on their own terms. The European Convention on Human Rights has been interpreted far beyond what its drafters intended, which is why 46 Council of Europe governments adopted a declaration in May 2026 affirming their “undeniable sovereign right” over immigration.

Question: Does immigration increase crime?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Raises crime / No added crime
Why contested: In the United States most studies find immigrants, including those in the country illegally, are incarcerated at lower rates than the native-born, while in Germany, Sweden and other European countries foreign nationals and recent asylum seekers are overrepresented among crime suspects. High-profile crimes by people who should have been deported drive politics on both continents. The data are hard to compare: legal status is often imputed, suspects are not convicts, and immigrant populations are younger and more male than natives.
Case for “Raises crime”: European police statistics are direct evidence: in Germany’s 2025 figures non-German nationals were 42.9 percent of suspects in violent crime while making up about one in six residents, and Scandinavian registry studies find overrepresentation that persists after adjusting for age and income. Every crime committed by someone who entered or stayed unlawfully was preventable by enforcing the law, which is why cases such as the 2024 murder of Laken Riley in Georgia by a man who had been released at the border, and the 2024 and 2025 knife attacks by asylum seekers in Solingen, Mannheim and Aschaffenburg in Germany, carry such political weight. American studies showing low immigrant incarceration rely on imputed legal status in survey data that undercount illegal immigrants and on a population from which deportation removes offenders.
Case for “No added crime”: In the United States, where the data are best, immigrants of every status are less likely to be incarcerated than native-born Americans: by Cato’s 2024 estimate, 674 per 100,000 for illegal immigrants and 303 for legal immigrants, against 1,195 for the native-born. Texas arrest data, which record status directly, show the same pattern, and cities that received large numbers of immigrants did not see crime rise faster than others. European overrepresentation reflects the age, sex, poverty and trauma profile of recent asylum seekers, along with reporting bias; it is a reason for integration policy and targeted deportation of offenders, not for lower admission overall. Anecdotes about individual crimes, however terrible, say nothing about rates.

Question: Does large-scale immigration strain housing and public services faster than it adds to them?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Strains capacity / Builds capacity
Why contested: New arrivals need homes, school places and doctors at once, while the taxes they pay and the workers they supply to construction and health care add capacity only over time and only if governments invest. Canada, Australia and Britain all saw rapid population growth coincide with housing crises after 2022, and their governments cut immigration in response, but disentangling migration from interest rates and a decade of underbuilding is hard.
Case for “Strains capacity”: Housing supply is slow to respond, so a sudden rise in population pushes up rents before any new homes can be built. Canada’s population grew by more than 3 percent in 2023, the fastest since the 1950s, largely through temporary residents, and rents and homelessness rose sharply; the government itself now says it cut temporary arrivals to ease pressure on housing and services. In Britain net migration of close to a million a year added the population of a large city annually while housebuilding stayed flat. Public services are funded with a lag and planned for the existing population, so the people who wait longer for a doctor or a school place are residents in the places receiving the most newcomers.
Case for “Builds capacity”: Housing shortages are caused by restrictive zoning and slow permitting, which predate the recent surges; immigrants make up a large share of construction workers, so cutting immigration shrinks the workforce that builds homes. In the United States about a quarter of construction workers are immigrants, and home builders warned in 2025 that deportations were slowing projects. Hospitals and care homes in every rich country depend on foreign-born nurses, doctors and carers, so restricting immigration lengthens waiting lists rather than shortening them. Blaming immigrants for shortages lets governments avoid the harder task of building.

Question: Is preserving a country’s culture and social cohesion a legitimate reason to limit how many people it admits?
Tag: value
Poles: Legitimate ground / Illegitimate ground
Why contested: Many citizens say the pace of change in their communities, not the economics, is what worries them, and some scholars on the left and right take that seriously. Others hold that limiting admission on cultural grounds slides into discrimination by origin, religion or race. This is a question about reasons for restricting admission; how newcomers are integrated after arrival is a separate topic.
Case for “Legitimate ground”: A nation is not only a labor market but a community with shared language, norms and memories, and social trust is what makes a welfare state, democratic compromise and safe streets possible. Economists such as Paul Collier have argued that rapid immigration can outpace integration and erode that trust, and that the costs fall on the working-class neighborhoods that receive the most newcomers. Citizens are entitled to decide the pace at which their society changes, just as they decide other large questions collectively. Ignoring these concerns, or dismissing them as bigotry, does not make them go away; it hands them to parties that will act on them less carefully.
Case for “Illegitimate ground”: Culture has never been static, and every wave of immigrants, from the Irish and Italians in the United States to Poles in Britain, was once said to threaten cohesion before becoming part of the nation. Evidence that diversity lowers trust is weak and inconsistent, and the effect fades as neighbors get to know each other. Choosing whom to admit by culture in practice means choosing by religion, ethnicity or national origin, which liberal democracies rejected when they abolished race-based quotas such as the US national-origins system in 1965. The state’s role is to treat people as individuals, judging them by their conduct and not by the group they come from.

Question: Do states have a moral right to exclude peaceful people who want to move and settle, or is freedom of movement a basic right?
Tag: value
Poles: Right to exclude / Right to move
Why contested: International law recognizes a right to leave any country but not a right to enter one other than one’s own, and nearly every state claims the power to decide who enters. A long line of philosophers and economists argues this is indefensible, while most citizens and governments treat it as self-evident. The answer determines whether the burden of justification lies on those who want to admit more people or on those who want to keep them out.
Case for “Right to exclude”: Self-determination means a political community decides its own membership, as clubs, families and nations always have; the philosopher Michael Walzer called the power to admit and exclude “at the core of communal independence.” Without it, a country cannot plan its public services, sustain its welfare state or keep promises to its citizens, and democratic majorities in every country have made clear they want that power exercised. Open borders on today’s scale of global inequality, with around 900 million adults saying they would like to move, would transform receiving societies faster than institutions could adapt, which could destroy the very conditions that make them attractive.
Case for “Right to move”: No one chooses where they are born, and restricting peaceful movement by birthplace resembles feudal privilege, as the philosopher Joseph Carens put it: a status inherited at birth that determines life chances. We regard freedom to move within a country as a basic liberty, and the reasons that justify it — the freedom to pursue work, love and safety — apply equally across borders. Governments need a strong reason to use force to stop a willing worker from renting from a willing landlord and working for a willing employer, and the usual reasons (wages, fiscal cost, culture) are either empirically weak or could be addressed by less drastic means, such as charging fees, limiting welfare eligibility or controlling for security. The economic gains are estimated at up to double world output, the largest available improvement in the lives of the world’s poor.

## Positions

Camp: Cut immigration and enforce the border
Adherents: The Trump administration, whose stated priority is “restoring operational control of the southern border,” ending releases and mass deportation, and which counts negative net migration in 2025 as an achievement (White House, https://www.whitehouse.gov/priorities/border-immigration/). The Center for Immigration Studies and its executive director Mark Krikorian, which describes its outlook as “low-immigration, pro-immigrant” and favors far lower overall numbers (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Immigration_Studies). NumbersUSA, which calls itself “America’s largest grassroots immigration-control organization” (https://numbersusa.org/). In Britain, the Labour government’s May 2025 white paper “Restoring Control over the Immigration System,” which set out to cut net migration substantially (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoring_Control_over_the_Immigration_System), and Reform UK, which proposes much deeper cuts.
Case: A country that cannot decide who enters is not fully self-governing. For a decade, immigration in the United States and Britain ran far above what voters were ever asked to approve, through asylum claims that were mostly not about persecution, visa routes that were abused, and a border that was effectively open. The benefits went to the migrants themselves and to employers who wanted cheaper labor; the costs fell on workers competing for jobs at the bottom, on renters, on overstretched schools and hospitals, and on the working-class communities that absorbed the most change. Immigration should be reduced to a level the public consents to, selected for the skills the country needs, and enforced so that the law means what it says: people who enter or stay illegally should be removed, and employers who hire them punished. That is not hostility to immigrants; it is the condition for treating well the immigrants a country does admit.
Best evidence: Policy works. US Border Patrol encounters at the southwest border fell from over 2 million a year to about 238,000 in fiscal 2025, the lowest since 1970, and EU crossings fell sharply on routes where controls were tightened, disproving the claim that migration is beyond government control. Fiscal studies from Britain’s Office for Budget Responsibility and from Denmark and the Netherlands show that low-wage immigration imposes net costs over a lifetime. Public opinion supports the direction of travel: in Britain two-thirds of adults said asylum numbers were too high in 2026, and in the EU a majority feel negatively about immigration from outside it.
Answer to objections: To the charge that cutting immigration will shrink the workforce and slow growth, the camp answers that what matters to citizens is income per person, not the size of the economy; that labor shortages are a signal to raise wages, train residents and invest in automation, as Japan has done; and that growth built on ever more low-wage workers depresses productivity. To the charge of cruelty, it answers that enforcing laws a democratic legislature passed is not cruel, that deterrence at the border saves lives on the routes, and that chaos rather than control is what fuels resentment against immigrants.
Variation: The camp spans the right and part of the left. Some want moderate reductions and tight selection for skills (Britain’s Labour government, Canada’s cuts to temporary residents); some want net migration near zero, a long pause or large-scale deportation (the Trump administration, Reform UK, Germany’s Alternative for Germany); and some come from the labor left, such as Denmark’s Social Democrats under Mette Frederiksen, who argue that high immigration undermines wages and the welfare state (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_policy_of_Mette_Frederiksen). They differ over legal immigration, over deportation of long-settled people and over whether to leave the European Convention on Human Rights.

Camp: Expand legal immigration
Adherents: The Cato Institute, whose director of immigration studies David Bier argues that illegal immigration is a policy choice that can be corrected by opening legal channels (“Legal Immigration Will Resolve America’s Real Border Problems,” https://www.cato.org/publications/policy-analysis/legal-immigration-will-resolve-americas-real-border-problems). Century Initiative, a Canadian organization campaigning for a population of 100 million by 2100 through higher immigration, which it calls “the population we need for Canada to remain a prosperous and diverse society” (https://www.centuryinitiative.ca/). FWD.us, the technology-industry-backed advocacy group (https://www.fwd.us/). Economists such as Giovanni Peri and Michael Clemens, and business groups such as the US Chamber of Commerce.
Case: Immigrants make the countries that receive them richer, younger and more inventive. They start businesses at higher rates than natives, fill jobs that aging populations cannot, pay for pensions and health care for retirees, and win a disproportionate share of patents and Nobel prizes. The reason so many people cross borders illegally is that the legal system offers almost no way in for ordinary workers: the United States has not substantially reformed its legal immigration system since 1990, its employment-based green card backlog exceeds a million people, and for some nationalities the wait is measured in decades. Expand work visas, green cards and sponsorship routes, and most people will choose the legal door over the smuggler. A country that makes legal immigration easy can enforce its laws against the few who still cheat.
Best evidence: When the United States let Americans sponsor Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans and Venezuelans through a legal parole program in 2023, irregular crossings by those nationalities fell sharply, which Cato’s analysts put at about 85 percent. The Bracero guest-worker program of the 1950s coincided with a collapse in illegal crossings from Mexico. CBO projected that the recent surge would cut federal deficits by about $0.9 trillion over a decade and add $8.9 trillion to GDP, and the National Academies found little to no long-run effect on native wages. With deaths set to exceed births in the United States from 2030, on CBO’s projection, and already exceeding them across much of Europe and East Asia, immigration is the only near-term source of labor-force growth.
Answer to objections: To the charge that voters rejected high immigration, the camp answers that they rejected disorder — a border that looked out of control — not legal immigration, and points to Gallup: once crossings fell, the share of Americans wanting less immigration dropped from 55 percent to about 30 percent, and 73 percent still call immigration a good thing. To the charge that immigration strains housing and services, it answers that the fault lies with zoning and underinvestment, and that immigrants build the homes and staff the hospitals; the cure is to plan and build for growth, not to stop it.
Variation: Some advocates want high skilled immigration above all (points systems, more H-1B visas, green cards for graduates); others want large low-skilled and seasonal worker programs, private refugee sponsorship, or legalization for the long-settled unauthorized population. Libertarian members of the camp overlap with the free-movement camp below; business and progressive members often do not.

Camp: Close irregular routes and open legal ones
Adherents: The George W. Bush Presidential Center, which argues that robust border security must go with robust legal immigration and recommends that “Congress should expand legal immigration across industries and skill levels” while targeting the problems at the border (https://www.bushcenter.org/publications/policy-recommendation-immigration). Italy’s prime minister Giorgia Meloni, whose government has pursued offshore return centers in Albania and third-country “repatriation hubs” (a stance she restated in a joint statement with Denmark’s Mette Frederiksen on 10 August 2026, https://www.ilfoglio.it/en/politics/2026/08/10/news/the-meloni-frederiksen-alliance-on-immigration-we-need-migrant-repatriation-centres-in-third-countries--404490) while approving 497,550 work permits for non-EU workers over 2026 to 2028, Italy’s largest labor-migration quota (https://www.infomigrants.net/en/post/68411/explainer-italys-20262028-flow-decree-decreto-flussi). The European Union’s own Pact on Migration and Asylum and its Talent Pool proposal, which pair tighter border procedures with legal labor pathways, embody the same bargain.
Case: Public support for immigration depends on the public believing the state is in control of it. Canada sustained high, popular immigration for decades because almost all of it was selected and planned, not because Canadians were uniquely welcoming. The way to a generous, economically useful system is to close the irregular route decisively — swift decisions at the border, real returns of those with no right to stay, cooperation with transit countries, and no reward for paying smugglers — and at the same time to open legal doors wide enough for the workers the economy needs and for refugees selected from abroad. Enforcement without legal routes drives migration underground and starves industries of labor; legal routes without enforcement lose the consent that keeps them open.
Best evidence: The pattern of public opinion: Gallup’s measure of Americans wanting less immigration rose while the border looked chaotic and fell once crossings dropped, and in Britain the public is far more hostile to asylum arrivals by small boat than to students or skilled workers. Italy has shown that a government elected on a hard line against irregular arrivals can simultaneously run the largest legal work-visa program in its history. Canada’s experience after 2022 shows the reverse lesson: when poorly controlled temporary streams swelled, support for immigration fell even for the planned permanent program.
Answer to objections: To human rights groups who say offshore processing and return hubs violate the right to asylum, the camp answers that its schemes include legal safeguards, that even UNHCR has said return hubs can be a legitimate complement when rejected applicants cannot be returned home, and that a system most of whose rejected applicants never leave has already lost public trust. To restrictionists who say legal expansion simply raises the numbers, it answers that the level of legal immigration should be set openly by elected governments, which is exactly the control restrictionists say they want.
Variation: The balance differs. In Europe the camp’s center of gravity is externalization — deals with Tunisia, Egypt and Mauritania, the Italy–Albania protocol, the new return hubs — combined with labor quotas; in the United States it is the long-stalled bargain of “comprehensive immigration reform,” pairing border security with more visas and legalization for long-settled residents, as in the 2024 bipartisan Senate border bill and Representative María Elvira Salazar’s Dignity Act. Some in the camp would add development aid to reduce the reasons people leave (“root causes”), though research suggests emigration from very poor countries tends to rise, not fall, as they begin to grow richer.

Camp: Protect the right to seek asylum
Adherents: Amnesty International, which says “return hubs” carry “a risk of grave human rights violations” wherever they are located and should be rejected, and urges European leaders to reaffirm the right to asylum and invest in asylum and reception systems (https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/09/migrant-return-hubs-risk-becoming-human-rights-black-hole/). Michael O’Flaherty, the Council of Europe Commissioner for Human Rights, who told the European Parliament in March 2026 that externalized asylum and return procedures risk access to judicial remedies and safeguards against refoulement (https://euperspectives.eu/2026/03/externalisation-asylum-migration-human-rights/). The US Conference of Catholic Bishops, whose November 2025 special message, approved 216 to 5, declared “we oppose the indiscriminate mass deportation of people” while affirming that nations may regulate their borders (https://www.usccb.org/news/2025/us-bishops-issue-special-message-immigration-plenary-assembly-baltimore). IOM’s director general Amy Pope: “When safe pathways are out of reach, people are forced into dangerous journeys” (https://www.iom.int/news/21-migrants-died-every-day-2025-new-iom-data-reveals).
Case: The right to seek asylum and the rule against returning anyone to persecution were written after the Second World War because countries had turned away Jewish refugees who were then murdered. They are not a policy preference to be traded against migration targets; they are among the few commitments that bind states whatever the political mood. A person who reaches a border and asks for protection must get a fair hearing there, with a lawyer and an appeal, and must not be pushed back, locked up indefinitely or shipped to a country with a record of abuse. People who fail that hearing may be returned, humanely. Rich countries receive a small fraction of the world’s refugees; most are hosted by poor neighbors of the countries they fled. Enforcement that treats every migrant as a threat — mass detention of people with no criminal record, deportations to third countries, families separated — damages the host society’s own commitment to due process and human dignity.
Best evidence: About two-thirds of the world’s refugees live in countries neighboring their own, and low- and middle-income countries host most of them, per UNHCR. Offshore schemes have repeatedly failed on their own terms: Britain’s Rwanda plan cost hundreds of millions of pounds and removed almost no one before it was scrapped in 2024, and an August 2025 ruling of the EU Court of Justice on “safe countries of origin” left Italy’s centers in Albania unable to serve their original purpose of fast-track asylum processing, so they were repurposed for detention before deportation. [Correction 2026-10-05: the order is reversed; Italy repurposed the Albania centers for detention before deportation by decree in March 2025 (decree-law 37/2025), before the CJEU judgment of 1 August 2025, after court challenges had stalled them; the page now says the centers, “stalled by court challenges, were repurposed in 2025 for detention before deportation” and gives no source for this.] IOM recorded nearly 8,000 deaths on migration routes in 2025 and more than 82,000 since 2014. In the United States, 70.6 percent of those in ICE detention in July 2026 had no criminal conviction, according to TRAC’s analysis of ICE data.
Answer to objections: To the charge that asylum has become a back door for economic migrants that no country can control, the camp answers that the remedy is fast, well-resourced and fair decisions, with real returns of those found to have no claim and legal work routes for those who come for jobs — not dismantling the right for everyone. Most applicants from countries at war are recognized, and the backlogs that make the system look like a loophole are the product of underfunding. To the charge that the conventions are outdated, it answers that the reasons people flee — war, persecution, torture — have not changed, and that weakening protection in Europe or America invites every other country to do the same.
Variation: Some in the camp concentrate on asylum law and oppose externalization (Amnesty, the Council of Europe Commissioner, the European Council on Refugees and Exiles); some focus on humane interior enforcement and opposition to mass deportation and family detention (the US Catholic bishops, the American Civil Liberties Union); some, including UNHCR itself, take a more pragmatic line, engaging with governments on return hubs and safeguards rather than opposing them outright. Many in the camp also favor wider legal routes, which makes them allies of the legal-expansion camp.

Camp: Make free movement the norm
Adherents: The Libertarian Party of the United States, whose platform states that “political freedom and escape from tyranny demand that individuals not be unreasonably constrained by government in the crossing of political boundaries” and that economic freedom demands “the unrestricted movement of human as well as financial capital across national borders” (plank 3.4, https://lp.org/platform/). The economist Bryan Caplan of George Mason University, author of “Open Borders: The Science and Ethics of Immigration” (2019), who argues that open borders would roughly double world output (https://econfaculty.gmu.edu/bcaplan/caplannaik.pdf). The political philosopher Joseph Carens, whose 1987 essay “Aliens and Citizens: The Case for Open Borders” compared citizenship in rich democracies to “feudal privilege — an inherited status that greatly enhances one’s life chances” (https://people.brandeis.edu/~teuber/Carens,%20Aliens%20and%20Citizens.pdf). On the left, the No Borders networks and campaigns to abolish immigration enforcement agencies.
Case: The question “how many should a state admit” assumes the state has a right to decide, and that is the assumption this camp rejects. Peaceful people who want to work, study, marry or live somewhere should be free to do so unless there is a compelling reason to stop them, exactly as we accept for movement between states or provinces within a country. Where someone is born is an accident, and immigration restrictions are the largest remaining legal barrier to the world’s poor improving their lives: a worker who moves from Haiti or Nigeria to the United States can multiply their income several times over without becoming any more skilled. Freedom of movement already works inside the European Union and between Australia and New Zealand, and in the United States it was the rule for most of the country’s first century.
Best evidence: Economic models surveyed by Michael Clemens estimate that removing all migration barriers would raise world output by 67 to 147 percent, dwarfing the gains from free trade. Free movement inside the EU, which let hundreds of millions of people move across countries with very different wage levels, did not produce the collapse its opponents predicted, and the EU’s own citizens feel far more positively about it than about immigration from outside. The US absorbed enormous flows before federal restrictions began in 1875 and 1882, and again during the great wave of 1880 to 1920 with minimal screening, and emerged the world’s largest economy.
Answer to objections: To the objection that open borders would overwhelm welfare states and change political culture, the camp answers with “keyhole solutions” rather than exclusion: charge entry fees or special taxes, restrict welfare eligibility for newcomers, delay voting rights, screen for security and contagious disease — all far less harmful than keeping people out entirely. To the objection that hundreds of millions would come at once, it answers that migration responds to opportunity and networks, that most people prefer to stay home, as the majority of citizens in the EU’s poorer member states have done despite their right to move, and that a gradual opening would allow institutions to adapt.
Variation: Libertarians ground the case in individual liberty and markets; left-wing “no borders” activists in anti-colonialism and global equality; utilitarian economists in the size of the gains; and moderate members of the camp favor much more open borders with fees, quotas or guest-worker schemes rather than full freedom. Carens himself presents open borders as a long-run ideal while defending more modest reforms for the present.

## Crux

The disagreement under the disagreements is this: when a country decides whom to admit, how much should the interests of the people who want to come count against the interests of the people already there — and what, in fact, do the people already there gain or lose? Almost everyone agrees that migrants themselves gain enormously from moving, and almost everyone agrees that some residents bear real costs in some circumstances. The camps divide over how large and how widespread those costs are, and over how much weight the migrants’ gains deserve when set against them. Settle both, and most of the policy fights over numbers, enforcement and asylum would largely follow.

What evidence could settle: the effects of admission on current residents, measured by type of migrant and over time. That means the size of wage effects on the least-skilled workers and on earlier immigrants; the lifetime fiscal balance of different kinds of migrants and their children at every level of government, not only the national budget; how quickly housing, schools and health care expand when the population grows, and whether immigrant workers speed or slow that expansion; crime rates by legal status, measured on comparable terms across countries; whether diversity or rapid change measurably lowers social trust, and for how long; and whether enforcement and offshore processing reduce irregular migration durably, or only displace it and at what human cost. On several of these, notably wages, fiscal effects and US crime rates, a large body of research already exists and narrows the range; on others, such as long-run effects on trust and the durability of deterrence, the evidence is thin or still coming in, and the 2025 reversal in the United States and Europe is a natural experiment whose results are not yet known.

What remains a value choice no data can close: whether a political community has a right to decide its own membership and the pace at which it changes, or whether peaceful people have a right to move that states must justify overriding; how much a citizen’s modest loss should weigh against a foreigner’s large gain; whether the right to seek asylum on a country’s territory is an absolute obligation or one that democratic majorities may limit, reroute or cap; and whether preserving a shared culture is a legitimate reason for limiting numbers or a disguise for discrimination by origin. A person who gives citizens’ interests decisive priority can accept every pro-immigration finding and still favor restriction; a person who gives outsiders equal weight can accept every restrictionist finding and still favor openness. That is why the same studies are cited by every camp, and why better evidence alone, though it would move people in the middle, will not end the argument.
