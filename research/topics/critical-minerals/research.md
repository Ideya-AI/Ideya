# Critical Minerals — research

## Overview

Critical minerals are the raw materials that clean-energy technology cannot do without and whose supply is concentrated or at risk: lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese and graphite for batteries; the rare earth elements neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium and terbium for the permanent magnets in electric motors and wind turbines; and copper for every wire and grid. This topic covers how the world should source them: where they are mined and refined, who controls each stage, what governments should do about concentrated supply (subsidies, price floors, government equity stakes, stockpiles, tariffs, trade alliances, export bans), how much new mining is needed versus recycling and demand reduction, and on what terms mining should happen on Indigenous and community lands and on the deep seabed. It deliberately excludes the broader question of how fast to decarbonize (covered on the net-zero pace page), the choice between firm power and renewables (its own page), and general trade policy (free trade versus economic nationalism), except where each bears directly on mineral sourcing. Minerals used mainly for semiconductors and weapons, such as gallium, germanium and tungsten, appear only as part of the same supply-security story.

Why now: at a summit in Washington on 23 to 25 September 2026, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping extended the truce under which China suspended its October 2025 expansion of rare earth export controls, but only by two months, to 10 January 2027, and the White House readout reported no movement on China’s mineral restrictions (Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, reported by Rinnovabili, 25 September 2026, https://www.rinnovabili.net/policy-and-affairs/environmental-policies/chinese-rare-earth-exports-trump-xi-truce/; Brookings analysis, 29 September 2026, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-did-the-trump-xi-summit-actually-accomplish/). The original suspension, agreed at Busan in October 2025, was due to expire on 10 November 2026. China’s April 2025 controls on seven medium and heavy rare earths remain in force, and in June 2026 Beijing added two US rare earth companies, MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, to its export control list (Al Jazeera, 22 June 2026, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/6/22/china-adds-10-us-firms-including-rare-earth-miner-to-export-control-list). The extension lands weeks before a 1 January 2027 deadline under US defense procurement rules to stop buying rare earth magnets and several other materials from China, which Reuters reported in July 2026 that US industry was struggling to meet (https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/international/2026/07/27/trump-may-need-to-allow-chinese-minerals-as-us-industry-struggles-to-meet-2027-deadline/). In parallel, the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration is taking public comment until 19 October 2026 on the first-ever application for a commercial permit to mine the deep seabed, filed by The Metals Company (NOAA, 19 August 2026, https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/noaa-advances-review-of-first-ever-deep-seabed-mining-commercial-recovery-application).

The debate is live because 2025 turned a long-discussed vulnerability into an actual disruption. In its July 2026 Global Critical Minerals Outlook, the International Energy Agency wrote that 2025 “marked the year when the economic risks of highly concentrated supply chains materialised at scale,” after China’s rare earth controls forced some automakers to cut output, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo replaced a cobalt export ban with quotas (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary). Governments have responded with a scale of intervention unseen in decades: the United States has taken equity stakes in mining and magnet companies, guaranteed a rare earth price floor, launched a $12 billion minerals stockpile and is courting allies for a tariff-backed “preferential trading zone” (US State Department, 4 February 2026, https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesperson/2026/02/2026-critical-minerals-ministerial; Bipartisan Policy Center, https://bipartisanpolicy.org/article/project-vault-and-forge-the-administrations-latest-moves-to-secure-critical-minerals/), while the European Union is mobilizing up to €3 billion over 12 months under its RESourceEU plan (European Commission, 3 December 2025, https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_25_2891/IP_25_2891_EN.pdf). The participants are consuming-country governments and their security establishments, producer-country governments such as Indonesia, the DRC, Chile, Zimbabwe and the African Union that want processing and jobs at home, mining companies and automakers, environmental and human rights organizations and Indigenous nations that want consent and less mining, free-market economists who see the new interventions as costly state capitalism, and critics of the energy transition who argue its mineral demands are a reason to slow it.

Note: In 2025 one country refined most of the world’s supply of almost every battery and magnet mineral: China for lithium, cobalt, graphite, manganese and rare earths, Indonesia for nickel.
Context: Mining is spread across many countries, but refining, the chemical step that turns ore into battery-grade or magnet-grade material, is far more concentrated, and that is where the leverage lies. The International Energy Agency reports that the average share of the top refining country reached 70 percent in 2025, up from 68 percent in 2020, and that Indonesia (for nickel) and China (for the others) supplied over three-quarters of all growth in refined output between 2023 and 2025; for manganese, nickel and graphite almost all new supply came from the leading supplier. Rare earths were the one exception: new US projects and higher output in Malaysia cut China’s share from 91 percent in 2023 to 85 percent in 2025 on the IEA chart, which the agency credits to targeted policy and investment support. The same report states elsewhere that China supplies over 90 percent of refined gallium, graphite, manganese and rare earths, so its rare earth figures depend on which elements are counted. Indonesia’s nickel lead was built largely by Chinese-owned smelters. The IEA executive summary uses a slightly different basis, excluding rare earths, and puts the average top-country share at 72 percent in 2025, up from 70 percent in 2023 (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary). Export controls have opened price gaps that show what the concentration is worth: the IEA reports that gallium and the heavy rare earths dysprosium and terbium cost around five times more in Europe than on China’s domestic market.

Context: The Stated Policies Scenario reflects policies governments have actually adopted, not climate targets, so faster decarbonization would raise these figures. Energy technologies, chiefly electric vehicles, battery storage, renewables and grids, drive most of the growth; the IEA reports that the energy sector accounted for about 75 percent of demand growth across key energy minerals in 2025, that global battery demand grew by over 35 percent to more than 1.5 terawatt-hours, and that lithium demand has risen about 25 percent a year over the past two years (Market overview, https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/market-overview). Copper adds the most tonnage, about 7 million tonnes by 2040. Cobalt growth has slowed because cheaper lithium iron phosphate batteries, which use no cobalt or nickel, have taken a large share of the market. Projections are highly sensitive to battery chemistry and policy: the IEA’s 2021 report found that cobalt demand in 2040 could range from 6 to 30 times its 2020 level depending on those assumptions (https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/executive-summary).

The IEA describes the gaps in the same terms: the projected 2035 copper deficit narrowed from about 30 percent in its 2025 report to 25 percent, while a cobalt gap widened from just over 15 percent to over 25 percent because the DRC’s export quotas are expected to cut supply. “Primary requirements” means total demand minus recycled supply, after refining losses. A gap on paper is not a forecast of shortage: higher prices call forth projects not yet announced, and the IEA’s “high production” case, which adds early-stage projects, closes the nickel and graphite gaps. Those who expect shortfalls point out that lithium investment fell about 40 percent in 2025 and that mines take well over a decade to develop; those who expect supply to adapt point to the lithium price collapse of 2023 and 2024, when supply overshot demand.

Note: The DRC, which mines nearly three-quarters of the world’s cobalt, now allows exports of up to 96,600 tonnes a year, well under half of its 2025 mine output of about 230,000 tonnes.
Context: In February 2025 the DRC temporarily banned cobalt exports, saying it wanted to address oversupply and low prices; in October it replaced the ban with quotas of 18,125 tonnes for the rest of 2025 and up to 96,600 tonnes a year in 2026 and 2027, including 9,600 tonnes for national strategic reserves. Mine output and exports are not the same thing, since ore can be stockpiled, so the comparison shows the scale of the restriction rather than an exact cut. The effect on prices was large: the Cobalt Institute’s Cobalt Market Report 2025 records cobalt hydroxide prices rising by over 300 percent during 2025 and cobalt metal by 130 percent (https://www.cobaltinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Cobalt-Market-Report-2025.pdf), and the IEA projects a new cobalt supply gap by 2035. The measure is the clearest recent case of a producer country using its market share as leverage, and it sits alongside Indonesia’s ban on raw nickel ore exports, Zimbabwe’s restrictions on lithium ore and Mozambique’s on graphite. Artisanal and small-scale mining, long associated with child labor in the DRC’s copper-cobalt belt, fell to less than 2 percent of DRC cobalt output in 2024 according to the industry-funded Cobalt Institute (14 May 2025, https://www.cobaltinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Cobalt-Market-Report-2024_Press-Release-1.pdf); its share rises when prices rise, and under the quota system legal artisanal output is capped through a state buyer allotted 470 tonnes a month (Cobalt Market Report 2025).

Note: The average price of battery-grade lithium carbonate rose more than fivefold from 2021 to 2022 and then fell about 86 percent by 2025.
Context: Lithium is the textbook case of the boom-and-bust cycle that makes mineral investment hard. Prices spiked in 2022 as electric vehicle sales outran supply, drew a wave of new mines in Australia, China, Africa and South America, and then collapsed as that supply arrived; the USGS reports world output up 31 percent in 2025 alone. The bust closed or delayed many projects outside China and cut investment: the IEA reports that lithium companies reduced spending by around 40 percent in 2025. Prices then turned again. The IEA reports that lithium prices more than doubled between January 2025 and April 2026, driven by demand for grid batteries and supply constraints (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary). Those who favor government price floors and stockpiles cite this volatility as the reason markets alone underbuild supply outside China; those who oppose them cite it as proof that high prices quickly bring forth new supply.

Note: The IEA estimates that full implementation of China’s expanded rare earth export controls could put about $6.5 trillion a year of production outside China at risk.
Context: The figure is the value of downstream output in the automotive, high-tech, defense and energy sectors that depends on rare earth inputs, not an estimate of what would actually be lost. It illustrates how small volumes of minerals underpin very large industries. China imposed licensing on seven medium and heavy rare earths in April 2025, forcing some automakers to cut production, and in October 2025 extended its proposed controls to foreign-made products containing Chinese-sourced rare earths or made with Chinese technology; the October measures are suspended until 10 January 2027 under the US-China truce, while the April controls remain. The IEA puts the comparable figure for a full disruption of battery-grade graphite at over $300 billion a year. China has also announced controls on battery cathode and anode materials and battery-making equipment, and the number of mineral tariff codes subject to Chinese export controls has tripled since 2023. A rare earth cutoff is not hypothetical: China cut rare earth exports in 2010 after a collision between a Chinese trawler and Japanese coast guard ships, a precedent recalled by the Cato Institute’s Steve Hanke in 2021 (https://www.cato.org/commentary/china-rattles-its-rare-earth-minerals-saber-again); China dropped those restrictions in 2015 after losing a WTO case, as Scott Lincicome of Cato notes (https://www.cato.org/blog/some-perspective-china-rare-earth-minerals).

Note: Critical minerals make up about a quarter of what a battery cell costs but only about 3 percent of the price of an average electric vehicle.
Context: This is the basis of the IEA’s argument for a “mineral security premium”: because minerals are a small share of final prices, consumers could absorb the higher cost of buying from diversified suppliers with little effect. On the IEA’s estimates a tripling of rare earth prices would raise the cost of a car by about 0.1 percent, and a tripling of battery material prices would raise the price of electric vehicles and storage systems by about 5 percent. Intermediate producers are more exposed: battery cell makers and magnet makers outside China compete on thin margins, and the IEA reports that refining projects outside the dominant supplier cost 20 to over 150 percent more to build and about 50 percent more to operate. Those who oppose subsidies and price floors read the same numbers differently: if users can absorb higher prices, they argue, users rather than taxpayers should pay them.

Source label: IEA, The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions (2021), Executive summary
Source URL: https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/executive-summary
Note: A typical electric car requires six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car, and an onshore wind plant nine times the mineral resources of a gas-fired plant.
Context: This is the core reason clean energy changes mineral markets: the new energy system front-loads its material needs into equipment, whereas the old one burns fuel continuously. The same IEA report notes the difference cuts both ways for security: a mineral shortage affects only new electric vehicles or solar plants, not those already running, while an oil shortage hits every gasoline car on the road; and minerals stay in the equipment where they can later be recovered and recycled, while burned fuel cannot. The report also found that over 50 percent of lithium and copper production was in areas of high water stress, and that average copper ore grades in Chile fell 30 percent over 15 years, meaning more rock, energy and water per tonne of metal. The 2021 comparison predates the rapid spread of lithium iron phosphate batteries, which contain no nickel or cobalt.

## Questions

Question: Should governments pay to build mineral supply outside China, or leave sourcing to markets and trade?
Tag: value
Poles: Pay for security / Let markets supply
Why contested: Everyone agrees supply is concentrated; the disagreement is whether that concentration is a market failure only the state can fix or a risk that prices, private inventories and trade will manage. Price floors, equity stakes, stockpiles, sourcing rules and tariffs cost taxpayers or consumers real money, and whether that cost is insurance or waste depends on how likely and how damaging a cutoff is, which is partly a judgment about China’s intentions and partly a judgment about the proper role of the state.
Case for pay for security: China built its position over decades with state finance, and it can flood a market to bankrupt rivals and then restrict exports when it wants leverage, as it did with rare earths in 2010 and again in 2025. A private investor cannot rationally build a refinery that China can undercut at will, which is why, as the IEA reports, refining projects outside China cost 20 to over 150 percent more to build and investment fell 9 percent in 2025 even as demand rose. Price floors, offtake contracts and stockpiles correct that failure, and the cost is small relative to what is at stake: minerals are about 3 percent of an electric vehicle’s price, while full rare earth controls could put $6.5 trillion a year of downstream production at risk. Insurance looks expensive until the house burns.
Case for let markets supply: Every past scare, including China’s 2010 rare earth embargo, ended with markets finding new sources, new chemistries and lower prices within a few years, and China lost its leverage when buyers adapted. Government equity stakes and guaranteed prices pick winners in technical industries officials do not understand, invite cronyism and leave taxpayers holding losses when prices fall, as lithium’s 86 percent price collapse from 2022 to 2025 shows prices do. The better policy is to remove the obstacles governments themselves created, above all permitting delays of decades and rules that make rare earth feedstocks hard to process, and to trade freely with allies; if manufacturers want security they can hold inventory and pay for diversified supply themselves.

Question: Will mine supply keep pace with clean-technology demand through the 2030s?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Shortfalls coming / Supply will adapt
Why contested: Projections show gaps, but projections of mineral markets have repeatedly been wrong in both directions. The IEA expects existing and announced projects to meet about two-thirds of lithium needs and three-quarters of copper and cobalt needs in 2035, yet lithium and nickel spent 2023 to 2025 in glut. The answer depends on how fast prices call forth new mines, how long those mines take, and how fast battery chemistry and recycling change demand.
Case for shortfalls coming: Mines take 16 years or more from discovery to production, so supply for the mid-2030s must be found and financed now, and it is not: investment fell in 2025, lithium spending by about 40 percent and exploration for lithium and nickel by about 40 percent. Ore grades are falling, copper deposits are deeper and poorer, and many new deposits lie in water-stressed places or on Indigenous lands where projects stall. Export quotas in the DRC and controls in China can remove supply overnight. A shortage would raise the cost of the energy transition and slow it when speed matters most.
Case for supply will adapt: The lithium boom of 2021 and 2022 brought a 31 percent jump in world output in 2025 alone and a price collapse, which shows how quickly supply responds when prices rise. Geological reserves are ample; the USGS puts identified lithium resources at about 150 million tonnes against 2025 output of about 290,000. Gaps in projections reflect only projects already announced, and the IEA’s own high-production case closes the nickel and graphite gaps. Chemistry shifts, such as the move to lithium iron phosphate batteries that use no cobalt or nickel, and sodium-ion batteries that use no lithium, cut demand for whichever mineral becomes scarce.

Question: Do bans on raw mineral exports help producer countries develop?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Bans build industry / Bans backfire
Why contested: Indonesia’s nickel ore ban is the leading test case and is read both ways. Processed nickel exports rose from about $1 billion a year before the ban to about $15 billion in 2023, but almost all the smelters are Chinese-owned, run on coal and ship to China, and the EU won a WTO case against the ban. The DRC, Zimbabwe and others are now trying versions of the same policy, so the evidence matters to a large share of the world’s supply.
Case for bans build industry: Exporting raw ore keeps poor countries at the bottom of the value chain, where they earn the least and absorb the most damage, while refining, manufacturing and the jobs that go with them happen elsewhere. Every rich country that industrialized did so behind policies to capture more value at home. Indonesia’s ban turned it into the world’s dominant nickel processor within a few years and brought tens of billions of dollars of investment; without the ban, those smelters would have been built in China. Producer countries that hold scarce resources should use that leverage, as the DRC did when its quota lifted cobalt prices.
Case for bans backfire: Export bans work only for countries with a dominant share of a mineral and enough capital to attract processors, and even then the gains go mostly to the foreign firms that build the plants. Indonesia’s smelters are largely Chinese-owned, powered by new coal plants and linked to deforestation, and most of their output feeds stainless steel rather than batteries. Bans invite retaliation and legal challenge, deter mining investment and push buyers toward substitutes; high nickel prices helped make cobalt- and nickel-free batteries attractive. Better royalties, transparent contracts and investment in skills deliver more value with less risk.

Question: Should affected communities be able to block a mine?
Tag: value
Poles: Consent as veto / Consultation suffices
Why contested: More than half of energy transition mineral projects sit on or near Indigenous peoples’ lands, according to a 2023 study in Nature Sustainability. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples sets a standard of free, prior and informed consent, but governments, including the United States, generally treat it as a duty to consult rather than a right to refuse. How much weight local consent should carry against national security, climate goals and the interests of people elsewhere is a question of values.
Case for consent as veto: Mining reshapes land, water and livelihoods for generations, and the communities who bear those costs have historically been overridden, often without benefit. A right to consent that cannot include saying no is not a right. Projects pushed through without consent, such as the Nevada lithium mines Amnesty International reported on in May 2026, end in conflict, litigation and delay anyway; projects that win genuine consent through shared ownership and benefits are more durable. A clean energy transition built on dispossession repeats the injustice of the fossil fuel era and undermines public support for the transition itself.
Case for consultation suffices: The energy transition and national security serve millions of people, including future generations threatened by climate change, and no single community should be able to block resources that a whole country, or the world, needs. Elected governments weigh these interests through permitting and environmental review, with consultation, mitigation and benefit-sharing for those affected. A universal veto would make many deposits unminable, push production to countries with weaker environmental and labor protections, and leave consumers dependent on supply chains where no one is consulted at all.

Question: Should commercial mining of the deep seabed begin?
Tag: value
Poles: Start seabed mining / Pause it first
Why contested: Nodules on the Pacific seabed hold more nickel and cobalt than land reserves and could be collected without clearing forests or displacing communities, but the deep-sea ecosystems they lie in are among the least studied on Earth. Forty-six countries back a pause, the International Seabed Authority has not adopted rules for commercial mining, and the United States is moving to license it under its own law. Scientists can narrow the uncertainty about ecological damage, but how much risk to accept before the uncertainty is resolved is a value choice.
Case for start seabed mining: Land mining for nickel and cobalt clears rainforest in Indonesia, relies on coal power, and in the DRC has involved child labor; nodules sit loose on the seafloor with no overburden to remove and no people to displace. The nodules hold billions of tonnes of metal, enough to break the concentration of supply in a few countries. Decades of exploration and test collection have produced environmental data, and regulation can require monitoring and set aside protected zones. Waiting for perfect knowledge means continuing the known harms of land mining indefinitely.
Case for pause it first: Deep-sea ecosystems recover over centuries if at all, most species in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone have not yet been described, and the sediment plumes from collection could spread far beyond mined areas. Once damage is done it cannot be reversed. The minerals are not urgently needed: battery chemistry is moving away from cobalt and nickel, recycling is growing and land supply is in surplus for nickel. A unilateral US permit would undermine the international system that treats the seabed as the common heritage of humankind.

Question: How much can recycling, new battery chemistries and lower demand reduce the need for new mines?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Cut mining deeply / Mining still dominant
Why contested: Recycling and substitution are central to the case that the transition can proceed with far less new mining, but the timing is the problem: batteries last a decade or more, so scrap arrives after demand has already grown. Estimates differ with assumptions about collection rates, car ownership and chemistry.
Case for cut mining deeply: Unlike burned fuel, metals in a battery or magnet can be recovered, and recycling rates for nickel and cobalt already exceed 40 percent of available feedstock, according to the IEA. The IEA projects recycling cutting new mine needs by 25 to 40 percent by 2050, and a study for Earthworks found 25 to 55 percent cuts by 2040; adding smaller vehicles, more public transit and longer product lives could cut further. Chemistry already moves fast: lithium iron phosphate batteries went from a niche to around 40 percent of the electric vehicle market by 2023 and removed cobalt and nickel entirely from those cars. Planning for maximum mining locks in harms that smarter demand policy would avoid.
Case for mining still dominant: Demand is growing far faster than scrap can accumulate, so recycling will cover only a minority of supply for decades; the IEA projects recycled battery metals at 20 to 30 percent of demand by 2050 and says new mines remain essential. Lower car ownership is a social choice most countries have not made. Substitution moves dependence rather than ending it: lithium iron phosphate cathodes and battery recycling capacity are both concentrated in China. Supply security in the 2030s will be decided by mines and refineries built now.

Question: Will China keep using its mineral dominance as leverage, or does doing so erode its own position?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Lasting leverage / Self-defeating weapon
Why contested: China has imposed export controls on gallium, germanium, graphite, antimony and rare earths since 2023 and suspended some of them in a trade truce, but kept others and added US companies to its control list in June 2026. Whether that leverage endures depends on how quickly other countries can build processing, which the IEA says lags far behind mining, and on whether Beijing judges restriction worth the cost to its own exporters.
Case for lasting leverage: The leverage is not in ore but in processing know-how, equipment and scale that took China decades to build and that it now controls through export bans on the technology itself. Diversified refining and magnet capacity outside China is a fraction of what is needed: the IEA reports planned magnet production outside the dominant supplier is only about a third of expected mined rare earth supply by 2035. China can calibrate controls, licensing some buyers and not others, which keeps the threat alive even when restrictions are suspended. The September 2026 summit extended the truce by only two months, which shows Beijing values keeping the lever in hand.
Case for self-defeating weapon: Each use of the weapon accelerates the alternatives that erode it. China’s 2010 embargo spurred new mines in Australia and the United States and ended with China losing a WTO case; the 2025 controls triggered tens of billions of dollars of Western public finance, and the IEA reports China’s share of rare earth refining falling from 91 to 85 percent between 2023 and 2025. Chinese producers lose export revenue and customers when controls bite, and buyers design out the restricted materials. Over a decade the threat shrinks the market share that makes it credible.

Question: Should the mineral demands of clean technology slow its deployment?
Tag: value
Poles: Slow the build-out / Build fast anyway
Why contested: Clean technologies need far more minerals per unit than the fossil systems they replace, and much of the supply runs through China, the DRC and Indonesia. Some argue this makes rapid mandates for electric vehicles and renewables a strategic and economic mistake; others argue the mining is small next to fossil fuel extraction and that delay costs more in climate damage. The facts about mining volumes are measurable, but how to weigh dependence and mining harms against climate risk is a value choice.
Case for slow the build-out: An electric car needs six times the minerals of a conventional car, and mandating that switch on a fixed timetable hands demand to supply chains China dominates, raising costs for consumers and creating strategic vulnerability. The mining required is enormous, mines take decades to open, and forcing the pace means accepting environmental and human costs abroad that wealthy countries would not accept at home. Letting consumers and utilities choose technologies, using domestic oil, gas and nuclear power meanwhile, and adopting more moderate targets would cut dependence on China and let mineral supply grow at a sustainable pace.
Case for build fast anyway: Per unit of electricity, solar and wind need dozens of times less mining than coal once the fuel itself is counted, and minerals stay in equipment to be recycled, while fossil fuels must be dug up and burned continuously. Climate damage compounds with every year of delay, and the countries that build clean-technology industries now will lead the industries of the century. Dependence on China is a reason to build supply chains faster, not to stay dependent on oil and gas markets that have their own chokepoints, as the 2026 closure of the Strait of Hormuz showed.

## Positions

Order note: the first camp is the governing policy of the United States, the European Union, Japan and most G7 governments and is the most widely held. The second is the position of most producer-country governments and the African Union. The third is the main position of environmental, human rights and Indigenous organizations. The fourth rejects the question as posed: it denies that clean-technology deployment should set the pace of mineral sourcing at all, and it is the most prominent hybrid in practice, since some who hold it, including parts of the current US administration, also support camp 1’s tools for defense and industry. The fifth, a free-market camp, is the principal critic of camp 1’s methods but shares its goal of diversified supply. Deep-sea mining splits the camps rather than forming its own: camp 1’s US wing backs it, camp 3 opposes it, and it is noted inside each. Mining companies and automakers mostly sit in camp 1 while lobbying for faster permits.

Camp: Build supply chains outside China with state backing
Adherents: US Department of State, Critical Minerals Ministerial statement, 4 February 2026, https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesperson/2026/02/2026-critical-minerals-ministerial (calling the concentrated market “a tool of political coercion and supply chain disruption” and pledging to “build new sources of supply” with 54 countries, US financing and the new Forum on Resource Geostrategic Engagement); European Commission, RESourceEU Action Plan, 3 December 2025, https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_25_2891/IP_25_2891_EN.pdf (financing, joint purchasing, stockpiling, fast-track permitting and partnerships with “like-minded countries to diversify supply chains,” building on the Critical Raw Materials Act). Also: the governments of Japan, Australia, Canada and South Korea; the G7 Critical Minerals Production Alliance; MP Materials and other Western miners and magnet makers; analysts such as Gracelin Baskaran of the Center for Strategic and International Studies.
Case: Critical minerals are now what oil was in the 1970s: the input everything else depends on, controlled by a supplier willing to cut it off for political ends. China did not win this market on cost alone; it spent decades building refining and magnet capacity with state finance, priced rivals out, and has now shown, with its 2025 rare earth controls, that it will use the result as leverage. Markets will not fix this by themselves, because no private investor will build a refinery China can bankrupt by flooding the market, and the IEA reports that capital costs outside China are 20 to over 150 percent higher. So democracies must do what China did, quickly and together: guarantee prices and offtake for new projects, take equity where needed, hold strategic stockpiles, speed permits, and pool demand among allies so that diversified supply has buyers. The cost is a modest security premium on products where minerals are a small share of the price, and it buys insurance against disruptions that could idle whole industries.
Best evidence: China refines about 74 percent of the world’s lithium, 76 percent of its cobalt, 94 percent of its graphite and 85 percent of its rare earths, and in April 2025 its rare earth controls forced some automakers outside China to cut or halt production (IEA, Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026). Targeted support works: new US projects and Malaysian output cut China’s share of rare earth refining from 91 to 85 percent between 2023 and 2025, which the IEA credits to policy and investment support. Minerals are about 3 percent of an electric vehicle’s price, so a tripling of rare earth prices would raise a car’s cost by about 0.1 percent (IEA), while full rare earth controls could put $6.5 trillion a year of production at risk. Private investment fell 9 percent in 2025 despite rising demand, evidence that markets alone are not funding diversification.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from free-market critics, is that state stakes and price floors breed cronyism, waste taxpayer money and copy the very Chinese model they oppose. The camp’s answer is that the alternative has been tried: decades of leaving minerals to the market produced today’s dependence, because the market China operates in is not free. Price floors and offtake contracts are standard tools in markets distorted by a state actor, the sums involved are small next to defense and industrial budgets, and transparency and competitive tenders, which the IEA recommends, can address favoritism without abandoning the goal. To producer-country critics who see a new scramble for resources, the camp points to its partnership agreements, which promise investment in local processing and infrastructure.
Variation inside the camp: The US wing frames the issue as national and economic security, extends it to minerals for AI, semiconductors and weapons, uses equity stakes, a $12 billion stockpile (Project Vault) and tariffs, has proposed a tariff-backed “preferential trading zone” with reference prices among allies, and supports deep-sea mining under US law. It is less concerned with clean energy as such. The European wing frames diversification as necessary for the green and digital transitions, sets 2030 benchmarks under the Critical Raw Materials Act (at least 10 percent of EU consumption mined, 40 percent processed and 25 percent recycled in the EU, and no more than 65 percent from any single non-EU country, https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/raw-materials/areas-specific-interest/critical-raw-materials/critical-raw-materials-act_en), and places more weight on environmental standards and recycling; several EU states back a deep-sea mining pause. A domestic-mining strand, led by mining companies and many US Republicans, puts permitting reform ahead of subsidies.

Camp: Process minerals in the countries that mine them
Adherents: African Union, Africa’s Green Minerals Strategy, adopted February 2025, https://au.int/en/documents/20250318/africas-green-minerals-strategy-agms (a continental framework to move Africa from raw mineral exports to local beneficiation, industrialization and value chains); Government of Indonesia, Deputy Minister of Investment and Downstreaming Todotua Pasaribu, 26 February 2026, https://en.antaranews.com/news/406194/indonesia-stands-firm-on-downstreaming-in-us-mineral-deal (“those eyeing our mineral sector must invest in processing, as our law forbids exports of raw products”). Also: the government of the DRC, whose cobalt export quotas took effect in October 2025; Zimbabwe, which restricts lithium ore exports; Chile, whose national lithium strategy gives the state a controlling role in new lithium projects; the UN Secretary-General’s Panel on Critical Energy Transition Minerals, whose 2024 principles call for value addition and benefit sharing in producing countries.
Case: For a century, resource-rich countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America exported ore and imported the finished goods made from it, keeping the low-value, high-damage end of the chain while refining, manufacturing and jobs went elsewhere. The energy transition is a chance to break that pattern, and the leverage to do it is in the ground. Countries that hold the minerals should require that processing happen at home, through export bans, quotas, local-content rules or state partnerships, and should negotiate with every buyer, Chinese, American or European, on that basis. The new Western enthusiasm for “secure supply chains” is welcome only if it means investment in producer countries’ industry, not a new scramble for raw materials on old terms. Producer countries also have the right to manage supply and prices of resources they own, as oil producers have long done.
Best evidence: After Indonesia fully banned raw nickel ore exports in 2020, its processed nickel exports rose from about $1 billion a year at their pre-ban peak to about $15 billion in 2023, and its share of world nickel output passed 50 percent (CETEx, London School of Economics, March 2026, from Statistics Indonesia data). The DRC’s 2025 export ban and quotas lifted cobalt hydroxide prices by over 300 percent during the year (Cobalt Institute), showing that producer countries can move world prices. Indonesia now supplies 45 percent of the world’s refined nickel, ahead of China at 31 percent (IEA, 2025).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that export bans mainly enrich foreign smelter owners, damage the environment and deter investment, as Indonesia’s coal-powered, Chinese-owned nickel industry illustrates, and that they break trade rules, as the WTO panel found in 2022. The camp’s answer is that every industrial power, including those now objecting, protected its infant industries, that WTO rules written by importing countries are not a neutral standard, and that the remedy for foreign-dominated, polluting smelters is better domestic regulation, partnerships and ownership requirements, not a return to exporting ore. Indonesia’s policy has drawn Western as well as Chinese companies into its nickel industry, and the next stage, battery and cathode plants, builds on the processing base the ban created.
Variation inside the camp: Instruments vary. Indonesia and Zimbabwe use outright raw-export bans; the DRC uses export quotas and is pursuing a regional battery-precursor industry with Zambia; Chile and Bolivia rely on state control or partnership in lithium; the African Union and the UN panel emphasize regional value chains, fair contracts, revenue transparency and benefit sharing rather than bans. Some in the camp also share camp 3’s emphasis on community consent, while others prioritize state-led development and have clashed with local and Indigenous opposition.

Camp: Cut mineral demand and mine only with consent
Adherents: Earthworks, Declaration on Mining and the Energy Transition, https://earthworks.org/declaration-on-mining-and-the-energy-transition/ (signed by roughly 300 organizations; calls for “prioritizing the right to Free, Prior and Informed Consent, including the right to withhold consent” and “low-impact circular economy solutions that reduce the overall demand for primary metals”); Amnesty International, 12 May 2026, https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/05/usa-boom-in-lithium-mining-across-nevada-is-violating-the-rights-of-indigenous-peoples/ (calling on governments to “require free, prior and informed consent before approving any project affecting ancestral Indigenous lands” and on companies to ensure “no project proceeds without FPIC”). Also: the Deep Sea Conservation Coalition, Greenpeace and WWF on the seabed; the 46 countries that back a deep-sea mining pause; Indigenous-led groups such as the SIRGE Coalition; the Business & Human Rights Resource Centre, which tracks allegations against transition mineral companies.
Case: The clean energy transition is necessary, but a transition that repeats the extractive injustices of the fossil fuel era is neither just nor durable. More than half of transition mineral projects sit on or near Indigenous peoples’ lands, many in places with scarce water, conflict or weak governance, and the rush for supply is already overriding communities, from Nevada to the DRC to Indonesia’s forests. The answer is to need less new mining and to mine better: design for reuse and recycling, invest in public transit and smaller vehicles rather than replacing every gasoline car with a heavy electric one, and require binding human rights and environmental due diligence. Where mining happens, affected communities, above all Indigenous peoples, must be able to give or withhold consent, and some places, including the deep seabed and sacred and ecologically critical lands, should be off limits.
Best evidence: Owen and colleagues found that 54 percent of 5,097 energy transition mineral projects lie on or near Indigenous peoples’ lands, rising to 85 percent of lithium reserves and resources in their sample (Nature Sustainability, 2023). The IEA projects that recycling could cut new mine requirements by 40 percent for copper and cobalt and 25 percent for lithium and nickel by 2050, and that recycled battery metals produce about 80 percent less greenhouse gas than mined ones; a 2021 study for Earthworks found 25 to 55 percent cuts possible by 2040. Lithium iron phosphate batteries removed cobalt and nickel from around 40 percent of electric vehicle batteries by 2023 (IEA), showing how fast demand for a problem mineral can fall. Nickel companies in Indonesia cleared more than 75,000 hectares of forest between 2007 and 2022 (CETEx, citing 2024 research).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that demand cannot fall fast enough to meet climate targets and that a community veto would push mining to places with no protections at all, slowing the transition and worsening harms. The camp’s answer is that consent-based projects are faster in the end, because projects imposed over local opposition are delayed for years by litigation and protest, and that binding due diligence laws in consuming countries, such as the European Union’s, reach supply chains wherever mining takes place. Demand reduction is not an alternative to clean energy but a way to deliver it with less damage: every battery recycled and every trip shifted to transit is a mine that does not need to be dug. The camp does not oppose all new mining; it opposes mining that proceeds without consent or that destroys irreplaceable places.
Variation inside the camp: Human rights organizations such as Amnesty focus on consent, labor rights and due diligence and accept that new mining is needed; environmental and degrowth-oriented groups put more weight on cutting consumption, especially private car ownership; marine conservation groups focus on a deep-sea mining moratorium. Some producer-country civil society groups combine this camp’s demands with camp 2’s call for local value addition.

Camp: Scale back the clean-energy mandates that drive mineral demand
Adherents: Heritage Foundation, Diana Furchtgott-Roth and Miles Pollard, “How the Forced Energy Transition and Reliance on China Will Harm America,” 22 August 2024, https://www.heritage.org/china/report/how-the-forced-energy-transition-and-reliance-china-will-harm-america (arguing that climate policies “will handcuff America to China’s critical minerals” and recommending that the US “remove requirements and subsidies” for renewables and electric vehicles while expediting domestic mining and using domestic oil, gas and nuclear power); Mark P. Mills, now executive director of the National Center for Energy Analytics, “The Hard Math of Minerals for Clean Energy,” Issues in Science and Technology, 27 January 2022, https://issues.org/environmental-economic-costs-minerals-solar-wind-batteries-mills/ (arguing that the scale of materials needed “will almost certainly place severe limits” on wind, solar and battery expansion and proposing “more moderate and longer-term deployment targets”). Also: the National Center for Energy Analytics, which argues the world is in an “age of energy expansion, not transition” facing a “minerals famine”; the Institute for Energy Research.
Case: The mineral problem is self-inflicted. Governments have mandated a rapid switch to electric vehicles, wind and solar, each of which needs several times more minerals than what it replaces, and then discovered that China controls the supply chains those mandates depend on. Forcing the pace means handing demand, jobs and leverage to Beijing, paying more for energy and vehicles, and accepting mining damage abroad that rich countries would not tolerate at home. The sensible course is to stop mandating and subsidizing particular technologies, let consumers and utilities choose, keep using abundant domestic oil, gas and nuclear power, and let mineral-intensive technologies grow at the pace that markets and secure supply can sustain. Domestic mining should be allowed and permitted quickly, but not to feed a timetable set by climate targets.
Best evidence: A typical electric car requires six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car and an onshore wind plant nine times the mineral resources of a gas-fired plant (IEA, 2021). China refines 74 to 96 percent of the world’s lithium, cobalt, graphite, manganese and rare earths (IEA, 2026), and new mines in the United States take almost 29 years on average from discovery to production (S&P Global, 2024). The IEA projects 2035 supply from existing and announced projects covering only about two-thirds of lithium needs and three-quarters of copper needs, even under current policies rather than climate targets.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that delay costs more in climate damage than it saves, and that fossil fuels need far more mining per unit of energy than renewables once the fuel is counted. The camp’s answer is that tonnage comparisons miss the point: coal and gas are produced at scale by diverse, often domestic and allied suppliers, while battery and magnet minerals run through a single strategic rival, so the security costs are of a different kind. Emissions from the United States and Europe are a shrinking share of the world total, so slowing their mandates changes the climate little while making them markedly more dependent on China. Nuclear power, which needs the least mining of any source, offers a cleaner path than a forced build-out of wind, solar and batteries.
Variation inside the camp: Heritage and allied groups want mandates and subsidies for electric vehicles and renewables repealed outright and favor continued fossil fuel use; Mills accepts that wind, solar and batteries will grow but argues for slower targets, efficiency and nuclear power. Many in the camp support camp 1’s tools, such as tariffs and domestic mining, for defense and industrial minerals while rejecting them as a means to accelerate the clean-energy transition.

Camp: Keep mineral trade open and let markets supply
Adherents: Cato Institute, Tad DeHaven, “Rare Earths, Vulcan, and Government Equity Stakes,” 24 March 2026, https://www.cato.org/blog/rare-earths-vulcan-government-equity-stakes (“partial federal ownership of private companies is both unnecessary and undesirable,” arguing that Washington should instead “expedite mine and processing permits” and “work with allies rather than make supply chains more expensive through tariffs”); Veronique de Rugy, Mercatus Center at George Mason University, “Red Flags with Project Vault,” 26 February 2026, https://www.theunseenandtheunsaid.com/p/red-flags-with-project-vault (comparing the stockpile to agricultural price supports in which “the taxpayer absorbs the losses when markets move the wrong way” and urging policymakers to “fix the permitting system” instead). Also: Scott Lincicome of the Cato Institute; the Wall Street Journal editorial board, which has criticized the administration’s equity stakes as state capitalism.
Case: Supply concentration is real, but the remedy now in fashion, governments taking equity stakes, guaranteeing prices and running stockpiles, repeats the mistakes of every past industrial policy and imitates the Chinese state capitalism it claims to resist. Officials do not know which rare earth startup or lithium project will succeed, and when the White House picks winners, capital flows to the politically connected rather than the most capable, as the deals involving firms tied to the president’s family illustrate. Price floors set above the market create surpluses and taxpayer losses. Markets have repeatedly responded to supply scares: China’s 2010 rare earth embargo produced new supply and lower prices within a few years, and lithium’s 2022 price spike produced a glut. What actually keeps Western supply small is government itself: decades-long permitting, litigation, and rules that make processing uneconomic. Fix those, trade freely with allies, and let companies that need security pay for inventory and diversified contracts.
Best evidence: After China restricted rare earth exports in 2010, buyers found other sources and China dropped the restrictions in 2015 after losing a WTO case (Scott Lincicome, Cato, 2020). The lithium carbonate price rose more than fivefold from 2021 to 2022 and then fell about 86 percent by 2025 as new supply arrived (USGS), and global lithium output rose 31 percent in 2025 alone. The US takes almost 29 years on average to bring a mine from discovery to production, against 20 in Australia (S&P Global, 2024), pointing to permitting rather than a lack of subsidy as the binding constraint. The Pentagon’s guaranteed price of $110 per kilogram for MP Materials’ rare earth oxide is well above the 2025 average market price of $69 (MP Materials; USGS).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that China is not a market actor, so leaving supply to the market means leaving it to Beijing. The camp’s answer is that this justifies targeted, transparent tools, such as buying magnets for defense needs through procurement contracts, modest stockpiles for genuine military requirements and trade agreements with allies, not government ownership of private companies or open-ended price guarantees. China’s leverage also erodes when used, as each episode of controls has pushed buyers to new suppliers. And the costs of industrial policy are not only fiscal: they include corruption, misallocated capital and higher prices for every downstream manufacturer, which weaken the economy that security depends on.
Variation inside the camp: Some, like DeHaven, accept defense procurement contracts and stockpiles as legitimate tools while rejecting equity stakes; others, like de Rugy, oppose Project Vault as well. All favor permitting reform and freer trade with allies, and most oppose tariffs on mineral imports.

## Crux

The question under the others: is the concentration of mineral supply in a few countries a temporary market condition that prices, trade and new technology will correct on their own, or a durable strategic and human vulnerability that only deliberate public action can fix, and if it is the second, who should bear the cost of fixing it: taxpayers and consumers in importing countries, the communities and ecosystems where new mines would go, or the climate, through a slower transition?

What evidence could settle: How quickly supply outside China and Indonesia responds to prices without subsidy, and whether projects backed by price floors, equity and stockpiles actually reach production at competitive cost; the next few years of US and European rare earth and lithium projects are a live test. How durable China’s leverage is: whether its share of refining keeps falling as it did for rare earths between 2023 and 2025, and whether repeated export controls accelerate alternatives faster than they cause damage. How much recycling, lithium iron phosphate and sodium-ion batteries and lower vehicle ownership can cut the need for new mines in the 2030s, as opposed to the 2050s. Whether raw-export bans like Indonesia’s produce lasting domestic industry and broad income gains or mainly foreign-owned, high-emission enclaves. How much damage seabed collection does and how fast deep-sea ecosystems recover. Each of these is measurable, and findings would move people between the camps: evidence that supply responds quickly and China’s leverage erodes strengthens the case for open markets; evidence that subsidized diversification works at modest cost strengthens the case for state backing; evidence that recycling and chemistry change can cut mining deeply strengthens the case for demand reduction.

What no data can close: How to weigh things that do not share a unit. Speed of decarbonization against the consent of the people who live on top of the deposits; national security against the efficiency and openness of trade; the development claims of producer countries against the lower prices consuming countries want; the known harms of land mining against the unknown harms of mining the seabed. Whether a community should be able to refuse a mine that millions of people elsewhere would benefit from, whether it is fair for rich countries to secure their supply chains by paying premiums that poorer producers cannot, and how much risk of ecological damage is acceptable before it is understood are questions about what matters, not about what is true. Evidence can show what each policy costs and whom it affects; it cannot say whose costs should count most.
