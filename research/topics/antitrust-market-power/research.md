# Antitrust & Market Power — research

## Overview

This topic is about how much of the economy a few large companies should be allowed to control, and what antitrust law, the body of law against monopolies, cartels and anticompetitive mergers, is for. For roughly four decades, US courts and enforcers have read the antitrust statutes (the Sherman Act of 1890, the Clayton Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914) through the “consumer welfare standard”: conduct or a merger is illegal when it is likely to harm the buyers in a market, usually through higher prices, lower output, worse quality or less innovation. A revived movement, often called neo-Brandeisian after Justice Louis Brandeis, argues that this reading lets companies grow large enough to dominate markets, squeeze workers and suppliers, and wield political power, so long as prices stay low, and that the law was written to check concentrated private power as such. The page covers that dispute and the facts underneath it: whether US markets have become more concentrated and less competitive, how the big technology platforms fit, how hard enforcers should police mergers, whether antitrust should protect workers as well as consumers, whether up-front rules for digital “gatekeepers” on the European model should supplement lawsuits, and how far enforcement should answer to the president. It is centered on the United States, with the European Union and the United Kingdom as the main comparisons. It deliberately leaves to their own pages the collection and sale of personal data (surveillance capitalism), tariffs and trade (free trade versus economic nationalism), the pricing of medicines (drug pricing versus innovation), whom a corporation should serve (shareholders versus stakeholders), and unions and collective bargaining (labor power), which appear here only where antitrust reaches them.

Why now: on 6 October 2026, Paramount Skydance completed its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, uniting the Paramount and Warner Bros. film studios, CBS, CNN, HBO and their streaming services in a deal valued at roughly $110 billion including debt (reported figures range from about $81 billion for the equity to $111 billion with debt; Philadelphia Inquirer, 6 October 2026, https://www.inquirer.com/entertainment/paramount-warner-brothers-deal-closes-20261006.html; The Next Web, https://thenextweb.com/news/paramount-warner-bros-merger-doj-cleared). The Justice Department’s Antitrust Division had cleared the merger on 12 June 2026 without conditions after an eight-month investigation, concluding that it was unlikely to harm competition in streaming, television or theatrical film, and that labor concerns raised by writers and other workers did not present an actionable antitrust issue because the evidence did not show a likely reduction in output (DOJ statement, https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/statement-department-justice-antitrust-division-closing-its-investigation-merger-paramount). Twelve state attorneys general, led by California’s Rob Bonta, sued to block it and settled on 21 September 2026 for court-enforceable commitments, including minimum film releases for five years, $1.5 billion in added US production spending, a $47.5 million fund for displaced workers, separate cable-carriage negotiations and a News Editorial Independence Board for CNN and CBS; Bonta said, “Let me be clear: This settlement is not a vote of support for this merger” (California Attorney General, https://www.oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-settlement-warner-brosparamount-litigation). The deal capped a year in which the fight over antitrust’s purpose played out in public: the head of the Antitrust Division, Gail Slater, an advocate of what she called “America First Antitrust,” left on 12 February 2026, with Bloomberg reporting that the White House had sought her ouster (NBC News, https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/justice-antitrust-chief-slater-leaving-rcna258759; Bloomberg Law, https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/justice-department-antitrust-chief-slater-resigns); the Justice Department settled its case against Live Nation and Ticketmaster mid-trial, while a coalition of states pressed on and won a jury verdict on 15 April 2026 that the companies had illegally maintained monopolies (New York Attorney General, https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-and-coalition-states-win-trial-against-live-nation-and); the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 on 29 June 2026 in Trump v. Slaughter that the president may fire Federal Trade Commission commissioners at will, overruling Humphrey’s Executor (SCOTUSblog, https://scotusblog.com/2026/06/court-allows-trump-to-fire-ftc-commissioner-and-overturns-major-restraint-on-presidential-power); and in September 2026 Judge Leonie Brinkema declined to order Google to sell its ad exchange, ordering conduct remedies instead, a year after Judge Amit Mehta declined to order the sale of Chrome in the government’s search case (National Law Review, https://natlawreview.com/article/court-declines-order-adx-divestiture-google-ad-tech-case; NPR, https://www.npr.org/2025/09/02/nx-s1-5478625/google-chrome-doj-antitrust-ruling).

The debate is live because both sides of the old consensus have had their claims tested in court and in office. The first Trump administration sued Google over search and Meta over Instagram and WhatsApp in 2020; the Biden administration’s enforcers, led by Lina Khan at the Federal Trade Commission and Jonathan Kanter at the Justice Department, added cases against Google’s ad technology, Amazon and Apple, rewrote the merger guidelines in 2023 to lower the bar for challenging deals, and blocked or deterred mergers across the economy, winning some challenges in court (Kroger and Albertsons, JetBlue and Spirit) and losing others (Microsoft and Activision). Courts have since found Google liable twice and ruled for Meta in November 2025. The Trump administration kept the 2023 guidelines and the Big Tech cases but has settled more mergers with conditions, and its own ranks have split between populists who want to break concentrated power and those who see aggressive antitrust as a drag on business and on the race with China in artificial intelligence. Europe, meanwhile, has moved from case-by-case enforcement to up-front rules for the largest platforms under its Digital Markets Act. The participants include the antitrust agencies and state attorneys general; the courts; members of Congress from Elizabeth Warren and Amy Klobuchar to Josh Hawley and Mike Lee; the largest technology companies and business groups such as the US Chamber of Commerce; think tanks from the Open Markets Institute and the American Economic Liberties Project to the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation and the International Center for Law & Economics; the European Commission and the UK Competition and Markets Authority; and economists and legal scholars such as Carl Shapiro, Herbert Hovenkamp, Fiona Scott Morton, Tim Wu, Thomas Philippon and Jan De Loecker.

## Basics

Label: Average markup of US public companies over marginal cost
Value: 61
Unit: percent above marginal cost, sales-weighted average across US publicly listed firms
Viz: ranked
Series: 1980 21; 2016 61 (highlight)
Data year: 2016 (series runs 1955 to 2016)
Source label: Jan De Loecker, Jan Eeckhout and Gabriel Unger, “The Rise of Market Power and the Macroeconomic Implications,” Quarterly Journal of Economics (2020)
Source URL: https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/135/2/561/5714769
Note: By this study’s estimate, the average US public company charged prices 21 percent above its marginal cost in 1980 and 61 percent above it in 2016.
Context: A markup is the gap between what a firm charges and what one more unit costs it to make, so a rising average markup is one sign of growing market power. De Loecker, Eeckhout and Unger built the estimate from the financial statements of US publicly traded firms from 1955 onward, and found that aggregate markups began to rise in 1980 and that the average profit rate rose from 1 percent to 8 percent over the same period. The increase was concentrated among a minority of high-markup firms; the median firm’s markup barely changed. The paper is among the most cited pieces of evidence for the view that US markets have become less competitive, and also among the most disputed. Critics such as James Traina (2018) argue that the method treats marketing, research and other overhead costs as profit, and that counting those costs shrinks the rise considerably; others note that rising markups can reflect heavy fixed investments in software and brands rather than weaker competition. The authors reply that the rise survives several alternative cost measures. The figure covers public companies only, not the whole economy.

Label: Share of US industries where concentration rose since the late 1990s
Value: 75
Unit: percent of US industries, more than (Herfindahl-Hirschman index of product-market concentration)
Viz: radial
Data year: late 1990s to 2014
Source label: Gustavo Grullon, Yelena Larkin and Roni Michaely, “Are US Industries Becoming More Concentrated?,” Review of Finance (2019)
Source URL: https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/revfin/v23y2019i4p697-743..html
Note: More than three-quarters of US industries became more concentrated from the late 1990s to the mid-2010s, by this study’s measure.
Context: Grullon, Larkin and Michaely found that the Herfindahl-Hirschman index, the standard measure of how concentrated a market is, rose in over 75 percent of US industries, and that firms in industries where concentration rose most earned higher profit margins and made more profitable acquisitions, gains reflected in higher returns to shareholders. They report that the results hold when private firms and foreign competition are counted. The finding is about national industry categories, which are not the same as the markets antitrust law defines. Esteban Rossi-Hansberg, Pierre-Daniel Sarte and Nicholas Trachter (NBER, 2018, https://www.nber.org/papers/w25066) found that while national concentration rose from 1990 to 2014, local concentration fell, partly because large chains opened outlets in towns where they competed with local businesses; the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation argues that counting imports also lowers measured concentration in manufacturing. Whether rising national concentration means weaker competition, or more efficient firms winning, is one of the central disputes on this page.

Label: Concentration level at which a market is presumed “highly concentrated”
Value: 1,800
Unit: Herfindahl-Hirschman index points (scale 0 to 10,000), 2023 Merger Guidelines
Viz: range
Range: 0 (many small firms) to 10,000 (a single firm with the whole market); 2010 guidelines threshold 2,500
Data year: 2023 (guidelines issued 18 December 2023; kept in effect by the Trump administration on 18 February 2025)
Source label: US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission, 2023 Merger Guidelines, Guideline 1
Source URL: https://www.justice.gov/atr/merger-guidelines/applying-merger-guidelines/guideline-1
Note: Under the 2023 Merger Guidelines, a market with an index above 1,800 is highly concentrated, and a merger that raises the index there by more than 100 points is presumed illegal.
Context: The Herfindahl-Hirschman index adds up the squares of each firm’s market share: four equal firms give 2,500, a monopoly 10,000. The 2023 guidelines, written under Lina Khan and Jonathan Kanter, lowered the “highly concentrated” threshold from the 2,500 used in the 2010 guidelines back to the 1,800 used from 1982 to 2010, and added a second presumption against any merger that gives the combined firm more than 30 percent of a market. Federal Trade Commission Chairman Andrew Ferguson kept the guidelines in effect in February 2025, saying the agencies should prize stability and that the guidelines were largely a restatement of earlier versions (FTC, https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-chairman-andrew-n-ferguson-announces-ftc-dojs-joint-2023-merger-guidelines-are-effect). Guidelines describe how the agencies analyze mergers; they do not bind courts, which decide each case under the Clayton Act.

Label: Share of US local labor markets that are highly concentrated
Value: 60
Unit: percent of commuting-zone and occupation labor markets with a hiring Herfindahl-Hirschman index above 2,500
Viz: radial
Series: Share of labor markets 60 (highlight); Share of employment in those markets 20
Data year: 2016
Source label: José Azar, Ioana Marinescu, Marshall Steinbaum and Bledi Taska, “Concentration in US Labor Markets: Evidence from Online Vacancy Data,” NBER Working Paper 24395 (2018)
Source URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w24395
Note: In 2016, 60 percent of US local labor markets were highly concentrated among the employers posting job openings, but those markets held 20 percent of employment.
Context: Using nearly every US online job posting in 2016, the authors measured how concentrated hiring was in each occupation in each commuting zone. The average market had an index of 4,378, the equivalent of 2.3 employers with equal shares of postings. Concentration is highest in rural areas and small cities; big metropolitan labor markets, where most people work, are far less concentrated, which is why 60 percent of markets hold only 20 percent of jobs. When a few employers dominate hiring, economists call it monopsony power, and studies by these and other authors link higher concentration to lower posted wages. The finding underpins the argument that antitrust should protect workers as well as consumers, written into the 2023 Merger Guidelines and into Justice Department and FTC labor-market cases. Critics argue that job postings overstate concentration because workers can switch occupations or commute.

Label: US workers bound by noncompete agreements
Value: 30
Unit: million workers (nearly one in five), FTC estimate
Viz: bar
Data year: 2024 (estimate published with the FTC rule of April 2024)
Source label: Federal Trade Commission, “FTC Announces Rule Banning Noncompetes” (23 April 2024)
Source URL: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-announces-rule-banning-noncompetes
Note: The FTC estimated in 2024 that about 30 million US workers, nearly one in five, were bound by a noncompete agreement.
Context: A noncompete bars a worker from joining or starting a rival business for a period after leaving a job. The FTC under Lina Khan voted in April 2024 to ban most of them, estimating that the rule would raise the average worker’s earnings by $524 a year and lead to more than 8,500 additional new businesses a year. A federal court in Texas set the rule aside in August 2024, holding that the FTC lacked authority to issue it, and in September 2025 the FTC under Andrew Ferguson moved to dismiss its appeal, while saying it would pursue harmful noncompetes case by case. The episode is a marker in the larger debate: supporters saw the rule as antitrust protecting workers’ freedom to move; opponents, including the US Chamber of Commerce, saw an agency exceeding the powers Congress gave it. Several states, including California, Minnesota and Oklahoma, ban most noncompetes by statute.

## Questions

Question: Should antitrust protect only consumers from harm, or also check corporate size and power for their own sake?
Tag: value
Poles: Consumer harm only / Check power itself
Why contested: This is the founding dispute of the debate. Since the late 1970s, US courts have read the antitrust laws as protecting the competitive process for the benefit of buyers, measured mainly through prices, output, quality and innovation. A revived anti-monopoly movement argues that the laws were written to prevent private concentrations of economic and political power, and that a narrow test has let dominant firms grow as long as prices looked low. Both sides claim the statutes’ history, and both say the other’s approach would make the law arbitrary.
Best case, consumer harm only: Antitrust works because it asks a question courts can answer: is this conduct or this merger likely to make buyers worse off by weakening competition? That test is not only about prices; it covers quality, choice and innovation, and it has supported cases against Google, Microsoft and many mergers. Adding goals such as protecting small businesses, dispersing political power or raising wages gives enforcers no way to weigh one aim against another, so outcomes would turn on who holds office. Bigness achieved by making better products at lower cost is the competitive process working, and punishing it would hurt the very consumers and workers the critics care about. Political power and inequality are real problems, but tax, labor, campaign-finance and sector laws are better tools for them, as the Berkeley economist Carl Shapiro argued in 2018.
Best case, check power itself: The Sherman Act was passed in 1890 against the railroad and oil trusts, whose danger lay in their power over farmers, workers, rivals and legislatures, not just their prices. The consumer welfare test, popularized by Robert Bork in the 1970s, narrowed that purpose and was adopted by courts without Congress changing a word. A firm that controls a marketplace its rivals depend on, such as an app store, a search engine or a dominant meatpacker, can extract tolls, copy rivals and dictate terms while charging consumers nothing or little, and a price test misses that. Concentrated economic power also becomes political power, through lobbying, through control over what news and speech reach people, and through the threat of moving jobs. A law that cannot see those harms cannot protect a competitive economy or a democracy.

Question: Has competition in the US economy weakened enough to raise prices and hold down wages?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Competition has weakened / Markets remain competitive
Why contested: Several widely cited studies find rising concentration, markups and profits since the 1980s, and falling business entry. Other economists find that the measures are flawed, that national concentration rose while local concentration fell, and that the most concentrated industries often had the fastest productivity growth. The answer shapes whether there is a problem for antitrust to solve at all.
Best case, competition has weakened: The evidence points one way from several directions. Concentration rose in more than three-quarters of US industries from the late 1990s to the 2010s, average markups of public firms rose from about 21 percent above cost in 1980 to 61 percent in 2016, and corporate profits rose as a share of the economy while business investment and the startup rate lagged. Thomas Philippon’s comparison of the United States and Europe found that US prices for broadband and mobile service rose relative to Europe’s as American markets consolidated. Merger retrospectives, such as the reviews by John Kwoka, find that many consummated mergers in concentrated markets raised prices. In labor markets, most local markets have few employers hiring for a given occupation, and studies link that to lower wages. Taken together, this is a pattern of weakened competition that four decades of permissive enforcement allowed.
Best case, markets remain competitive: The headline studies measure the wrong things. Concentration in broad national industry codes says little about competition in real markets, and local concentration, where people shop and work, has fallen as national chains entered small towns. Markup estimates are sensitive to how overhead such as research, software and marketing is counted, and rise much less when it is. Much of the rise in concentration reflects superstar firms that won customers with better technology and lower prices: Amazon, Walmart and the big software firms grew because people preferred them. Productivity grew fastest in industries where concentration rose, and entry rates fell for demographic reasons that have nothing to do with antitrust. Prices for many consumer goods and digital services have fallen in real terms. A diagnosis this uncertain does not justify remaking the law.

Question: Are the biggest technology platforms illegal monopolies, or companies that won by being better?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Entrenched monopolies / Earned dominance
Why contested: US courts have now ruled both ways. Google was found to have illegally maintained monopolies in search (2024) and in parts of advertising technology (2025); Meta won its case in November 2025 when the court found that TikTok and YouTube compete with Instagram and Facebook; the FTC’s case against Amazon is set for trial in 2027 and the Justice Department’s against Apple is in discovery. The disagreement is over whether network effects and defaults lock in dominance, or whether competition from new technology, now generative AI, keeps even the largest firms in check.
Best case, entrenched monopolies: Digital markets tip: the more users a platform has, the more valuable it becomes, so the leader’s advantage feeds on itself. The largest firms have used that position to block the paths rivals could take. Google paid $26.3 billion in 2021 alone to be the default search engine on phones and browsers, a court found, because defaults are rarely changed. Facebook bought Instagram for $1 billion in 2012 and WhatsApp for $19 billion in 2014, when each was a fast-growing potential rival, and internal emails showed its leaders saw buying as preferable to competing. Apple controls the only way to install apps on iPhones in the United States and was found in contempt of court in 2025 for defying an order to let developers steer customers to cheaper payment options. Each firm can tax the businesses that depend on it, and the rivals that might discipline them never get the scale to try.
Best case, earned dominance: These companies became large by offering products people chose, often for free, and they keep spending enormous sums to stay ahead. Search defaults matter, but Google won the default deals because partners judged it the best engine, and users switch easily when they want to. The Meta trial showed how fast markets move: TikTok, a newcomer, took a large share of attention within a few years, and the court found that it and YouTube compete directly with Meta. Generative AI is reshaping search, advertising and software, and dominant positions of a decade ago look less secure every year. Acquisitions such as Instagram succeeded because the buyer invested in them; most acquired startups fail. Courts that hear the full evidence, as in Meta’s case, have found the competition real.

Question: Should regulators block more mergers, including small purchases of young rivals?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Block more deals / Let most deals through
Why contested: Only about 2 percent of reported mergers receive an in-depth review, and the agencies challenge a few dozen at most each year. The 2023 Merger Guidelines lowered the concentration thresholds for presuming harm and treated a series of small acquisitions as one, which the Trump administration has kept on paper while settling more deals with conditions. The dispute is over how often mergers harm competition, how well enforcers can predict which ones will, and whether the cost of blocking a good deal is worse than the cost of allowing a bad one.
Best case, block more deals: Mergers are the one form of market power the law can stop before it exists, and once two rivals combine, unwinding them is rarely possible. Retrospective studies of mergers in concentrated markets find that many raised prices, and courts have been slow to credit claims that efficiencies will be passed on to buyers. Large platforms have bought hundreds of young firms below the reporting thresholds, and acquisitions of potential rivals, the “killer acquisitions” documented in pharmaceuticals and suspected in technology, remove competition that would have emerged later. Hospital, grocery, meatpacking and airline mergers left many communities with one or two choices. The cost of erring toward caution is modest, since a good business can usually grow on its own; the cost of erring toward permissiveness can last for generations.
Best case, let most deals through: The great majority of mergers combine complementary assets, rescue failing firms, or let founders and investors cash out, which is what makes venture funding of startups possible. Most raise no competitive concern, and agencies already examine the few that do. Enforcers who try to block deals on speculative theories lose in court, as the FTC did against Microsoft’s purchase of Activision and Meta’s purchase of Within, and the attempts themselves deter beneficial deals and chill startup investment. The threat of a long review makes the largest firms more likely to build rival products themselves, which can be worse for small firms than being bought. Where a merger does raise a real problem, a targeted divestiture or condition usually solves it without blocking the whole transaction.

Question: Should the largest digital platforms face up-front rules rather than case-by-case lawsuits?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Write rules in advance / Litigate case by case
Why contested: The European Union’s Digital Markets Act and the United Kingdom’s 2024 digital markets law set obligations for designated gatekeepers in advance, while the United States relies on lawsuits under century-old statutes. The US monopoly cases against Google took four to five years to reach liability rulings and have yet to produce remedies that have run their course. Whether rules work better depends on evidence still coming in from Europe, and on whether regulators can write rules that keep up with technology.
Best case, write rules in advance: By the time a monopoly case is won, the market it was about has often moved on and the harm has been locked in. Google’s search case was filed in 2020, decided in 2024, remedied in 2025 and is still on appeal. Judges cannot supervise the evolving technical architecture of a platform for a decade. Clear rules, such as no self-preferencing, the right to uninstall and choose defaults, the right to steer customers outside an app store and interoperability, tell every gatekeeper in advance what it may not do and let rivals plan. Fiona Scott Morton, a former chief economist at the Justice Department’s Antitrust Division, argues that antitrust enforcement alone is no longer enough to restore competition in these markets, and that light-touch, specialist regulation is needed.
Best case, litigate case by case: Up-front rules apply the same prohibitions to every designated firm whether or not the conduct harms anyone in that setting, and they freeze today’s view of the technology into law. Europe’s experience shows the costs: Apple and Google have delayed or withheld features, including AI tools, from European users, citing the act, and compliance disputes have turned into years of their own litigation. Case-by-case enforcement asks whether specific conduct harms competition in a specific market, and US courts have shown with the Google rulings that the existing laws can reach dominant platforms. A regulator that sets the rules for a handful of firms is also exposed to capture and to political pressure, including from foreign governments. The Trump administration and US technology companies further argue that rules aimed almost entirely at American firms work as a trade barrier.

Question: Should antitrust protect workers’ pay and bargaining power, not just consumers?
Tag: value
Poles: Protect workers too / Leave it to labor law
Why contested: The antitrust laws have long covered buyers’ cartels, including employers agreeing not to poach each other’s workers, but enforcers rarely treated labor-market harm as a reason to block a merger until recently. The 2023 Merger Guidelines and the 2022 decision blocking Penguin Random House’s purchase of Simon & Schuster, which rested on harm to authors’ advances, made labor a central theory; the FTC’s 2024 attempt to ban noncompete agreements was struck down. In the Paramount–Warner Bros. review, the Justice Department concluded that workers’ concerns did not raise an actionable antitrust issue without evidence of reduced output.
Best case, protect workers too: The Sherman Act condemns restraints of trade in every market, and a labor market is a market in which employers are the buyers. When a few employers dominate hiring for nurses, truck drivers or meat-plant workers in a region, they can hold wages below what competition would pay, and a merger that removes one of them harms workers as surely as one that raises prices harms consumers. Noncompete and no-poach agreements, which bind tens of millions of workers, stop people from taking better jobs or starting rival businesses. Most Americans earn their living as workers, and an antitrust law that ignores the labor market protects their role as shoppers while ignoring their paychecks.
Best case, leave it to labor law: Antitrust can and should stop wage-fixing cartels and no-poach pacts, which are plain conspiracies. Going further, by blocking mergers on predicted wage effects, asks enforcers to forecast labor markets they measure poorly, and job-posting data overstate concentration because workers can change occupations, commute or move. Mergers that reduce costs often expand output and hiring. When enforcement turns toward protecting particular workers, it risks becoming industrial policy that preserves inefficient firms. Wages, bargaining and job mobility are better addressed by labor law, unions, state limits on noncompetes and a strong job market, tools designed for the purpose and accountable to legislatures.

Question: Do large, dominant American firms help the United States compete with China in AI and technology?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Scale wins the race / Competition wins the race
Why contested: Generative AI requires vast spending on chips, data centers and research, most of it by a handful of US companies, and the rivalry with China has become a frame for economic policy. One side argues that breaking up or hobbling the leaders would hand ground to Chinese firms; the other that dominant incumbents, protected from challenge, innovate less and that open competition is the American advantage. Evidence from past technology races exists on both sides.
Best case, scale wins the race: The frontier of AI is set by firms that can spend tens of billions of dollars a year on computing and research, and in the United States those are the large technology companies and the labs they fund. China’s state-backed champions operate at that scale. Robert Atkinson of the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation argues that America’s large, innovative technology firms are a key component of its arsenal in this rivalry, that they fund research no government budget could match, and that a “big is bad” antitrust policy would leave Beijing simply waiting for the United States to weaken itself. Breaking up Google or restricting its AI investments would scatter the resources needed to stay ahead, and Europe, which regulates its digital markets heavily, has produced few world-leading technology firms.
Best case, competition wins the race: The United States leads in technology because its markets let newcomers displace incumbents. Antitrust pressure that led IBM to sell software separately from its computers in 1969, and the AT&T breakup, helped clear the way for the software and telecommunications industries, and the Microsoft case of the late 1990s kept the path open for Google and others. Dominant firms protected from challenge tend to defend old businesses rather than invent new ones, and the most important AI advances of recent years came from smaller labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic, and from China’s DeepSeek, operating outside or alongside the giants. Allowing a few firms to control chips, cloud computing, data and distribution would make American AI less inventive and more fragile, and “national champion” arguments are what incumbent industries have always used to ask for protection.

Question: Should antitrust enforcement answer to the president, or be insulated from politics?
Tag: value
Poles: Accountable to the president / Insulated from politics
Why contested: The Federal Trade Commission was created in 1914 as a bipartisan commission whose members could be removed only for cause. In Trump v. Slaughter, decided on 29 June 2026, the Supreme Court held that the president may remove FTC commissioners at will, overruling Humphrey’s Executor (1935). The Justice Department’s Antitrust Division has always reported to the president, but merger reviews of politically connected deals in 2025 and 2026, and the departures of senior enforcers, have raised the question of whether enforcement decisions are being made on the merits.
Best case, accountable to the president: Antitrust enforcement is executive power: deciding whom to sue, which mergers to challenge and what settlements to accept. In a democracy that power should be exercised by officials accountable to voters through an elected president, not by commissioners who cannot be removed. Independent agencies were never really insulated from politics; they reflected the views of whoever appointed them and were shaped by the industries they oversaw. Presidential control lets voters choose an antitrust policy, as they did in electing administrations that took opposite views of mergers, and the courts, which decide every contested case, remain an independent check. The Supreme Court’s ruling restores the constitutional design.
Best case, insulated from politics: The value of antitrust depends on businesses and the public believing that cases turn on evidence, not on whether a company’s owners are friends or enemies of the president. When a merger can be cleared or challenged depending on its parties’ relationship with the White House, the law becomes a tool for rewarding allies and punishing critics, especially in media. Roger Alford, a senior Antitrust Division official removed in July 2025, publicly accused Justice Department leaders of letting politically connected lobbyists override staff in settling the Hewlett Packard Enterprise–Juniper merger (Bloomberg Law, https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/doj-perverted-justice-in-hpe-deal-dismissed-official-says), and a dozen states sued over the Paramount–Warner Bros. deal the department had cleared. Bipartisan commissions with staggered terms gave enforcement continuity across administrations; without them, Congress and the courts must find other safeguards.

Question: When a company is found to hold an illegal monopoly, should courts break it up?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Break it up / Fix the conduct
Why contested: Structural remedies have a long history, from Standard Oil in 1911 to AT&T in 1984, but modern courts rarely order them. In 2025 and 2026, judges found Google liable twice and declined both times to order a divestiture, choosing data-sharing, interoperability and contract rules instead, while Europe has said only a structural remedy may resolve Google’s ad-technology conflict of interest. States that won the Live Nation trial are now seeking a sale of Ticketmaster. Whether conduct remedies restore competition is a question with some historical evidence, much of it disputed.
Best case, break it up: Conduct remedies ask the monopolist to stop behaving like a monopolist while keeping every incentive to do so, and they require years of monitoring by courts and technical committees that the firm can outlast. The Microsoft settlement of 2001 imposed conduct rules that critics say did little; the AT&T breakup, by contrast, opened long-distance service and telephone equipment to competition and helped launch the modern telecommunications industry. When a firm owns both a marketplace and products that compete on it, as Google does in advertising technology or Live Nation in venues and ticketing, the conflict of interest is structural, and only separating the businesses removes it. The point of a remedy is to restore the competition the violation destroyed, not to regulate the monopoly forever.
Best case, fix the conduct: A breakup is irreversible and can destroy the efficiencies that made the integrated product valuable, harming customers who were not part of the case. Modern technology businesses are deeply integrated in code, data and staff, and Judge Brinkema found in 2026 that divesting Google’s ad exchange would be unprecedented and complex, while behavioral remedies could achieve the same aims. Courts should impose the least drastic remedy that restores competition, and targeted rules on exclusivity, defaults, interoperability and data can be tailored to the specific violation. The AT&T breakup took a decade and was followed by reconsolidation of most of the old Bell companies, and remedies aimed at 2020 markets may be obsolete before a divestiture could be completed, as generative AI changes search and advertising.

## Positions

Camp: Restore antitrust as a check on corporate power
Adherents: The Open Markets Institute, a Washington think tank that helped revive the anti-monopoly tradition: “The Open Markets Institute works to help people relearn how to use competition policy to build stronger democracies, more just and equitable societies, more innovative and sustainable economies, and a more secure and peaceful world” (https://www.openmarketsinstitute.org/our-mission). The American Economic Liberties Project, which says it works “to dismantle concentrated economic power and advance economic liberty for all” and that “monopolistic corporations govern much of our economic lives and exert extraordinary influence over our democracy” (https://www.economicliberties.us/). Lina Khan, FTC chair from 2021 to 2025, whose 2017 Yale Law Journal note argued that pegging competition to “consumer welfare,” defined as short-term price effects, “is unequipped to capture the architecture of market power in the modern economy” (https://www.yalelawjournal.org/note/amazons-antitrust-paradox). On the right, Senator Josh Hawley, whose 2021 Trust-Busting for the Twenty-First Century Act would ban acquisitions by companies worth more than $100 billion and replace the current test: “A small group of woke mega-corporations control the products Americans can buy, the information Americans can receive, and the speech Americans can engage in” (https://www.hawley.senate.gov/senator-hawley-introduces-trust-busting-twenty-first-century-act-plan-bust-anti-competitive-big/); and Gail Slater, head of the Antitrust Division from 2025 to February 2026: “The alliance of Big Business and Big Government must be broken” (https://www.justice.gov/opa/speech/assistant-attorney-general-gail-slater-delivers-first-antitrust-address-university-notre). Also associated: Senator Elizabeth Warren, Tim Wu of Columbia Law School (The Curse of Bigness), Jonathan Kanter, Matt Stoller, the Institute for Local Self-Reliance, and on the right Steve Bannon and American Compass.
Case: Antitrust was born as a democratic movement against private power, and it should be one again. The Sherman Act passed in 1890 because railroads, oil refiners and trusts could dictate terms to farmers, small merchants and workers and could buy legislatures; senators who voted for it spoke of liberty and self-government as much as of prices. Beginning in the 1970s, judges influenced by the Chicago School reduced that purpose to one test, the effect on consumer prices, and enforcement shrank to match. The result is an economy in which four firms pack most of the beef, four airlines fly most passengers, two companies control the smartphone operating systems and one handles about nine in ten searches. Dominant firms set the terms for the businesses that depend on them, copy and bury rivals, push down wages where they are the main employer, and turn economic size into political influence and control over news and speech. Low prices do not cure any of this, and many of the harms, such as a startup that never got funded or a farmer with one buyer, never show up in a price. The law should return to protecting open markets as a structure: presumptions against mergers that concentrate markets, bright-line limits on dominant platforms competing against the businesses that rely on them, attention to workers and suppliers, and breakups where a firm’s power cannot otherwise be checked. The camp spans the left and the populist right. Its progressive wing, around Khan, Wu and Warren, emphasizes workers, small business and inequality; its conservative wing, around Hawley, Slater and Bannon, emphasizes Big Tech’s power over speech and its alliance with government, and the two wings have split over whether the Trump administration’s merger settlements and the Paramount–Warner Bros. clearance betray the cause.
Evidence: More than three-quarters of US industries became more concentrated from the late 1990s to the 2010s, and average markups of public firms rose from about 21 percent above cost in 1980 to 61 percent in 2016. Courts found that Google illegally maintained monopolies in search and in parts of advertising technology, and a jury found in April 2026 that Live Nation and Ticketmaster did the same in concert ticketing, overcharging New York fans $1.72 a ticket. Sixty percent of local labor markets were highly concentrated among employers in 2016, and about 30 million workers were bound by noncompete agreements. The five largest technology firms made 616 acquisitions between 2010 and 2019 that were never reported to the agencies. Public opinion is with the camp across party lines: 78 percent of Americans told Pew in 2024 that social media companies have too much power in politics.
Answer to objections: To the charge that a broader standard is vague and would let enforcers pursue whatever they dislike, the camp answers that the consumer welfare test is itself malleable, since it lets courts credit speculative efficiencies, and that the older approach relied on clearer rules, such as structural presumptions and limits on dominant firms competing with their own customers, that are more predictable than an economist’s forecast of prices. To the charge that it would hurt consumers, it answers that competition is what keeps prices low over time, and that monopolies raise prices once rivals are gone, as the Ticketmaster verdict found. To the charge that big American firms are needed to beat China, it answers that national champions grow complacent and that the US technology industry was built by antitrust cases that opened markets to newcomers. And to the observation that courts have rejected several of its cases, it answers that this shows the need for Congress to restore the law’s original purpose.

Camp: Enforce the consumer welfare standard more vigorously
Adherents: The Washington Center for Equitable Growth, which published “Restoring competition in the United States” (November 2020) by Bill Baer, Jonathan Baker, Michael Kades, Fiona Scott Morton, Nancy Rose, Carl Shapiro and Tim Wu: “The U.S. economy is plagued by a problem of excessive market power,” and “Antitrust enforcement has failed to prevent this problem”; it urged $600 million in added annual funding for the agencies and legislation to ease plaintiffs’ burdens (https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/shapiro/restoringcompetition.pdf). Its authors included Tim Wu, a leading voice of the anti-monopoly camp, which shows how much the two camps share on enforcement even as they divide on its purpose. Carl Shapiro of the University of California, Berkeley, in “Antitrust in a time of populism” (2018): “a body of economic evidence supports more vigorous merger enforcement in the United States,” but “antitrust law and antitrust institutions are ill suited to directly address concerns associated with the political power of large corporations or other public policy goals such as income inequality or job creation” (https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/shapiro/antitrustpopulism.pdf). Also associated: Herbert Hovenkamp of the University of Pennsylvania, Jonathan Baker, Steven Salop, John Kwoka, Nancy Rose, the American Antitrust Institute, and in practice much of the career staff of the agencies and the judges who decided the Google cases on consumer-harm grounds.
Case: The goal of antitrust is right; the problem is that it has been under-enforced. Protecting the competitive process for the benefit of trading partners, consumers and, properly read, workers and suppliers too, gives the law a clear and testable aim, one that economics can inform and courts can apply. Over four decades, however, courts loaded plaintiffs with heavy burdens, credited speculative efficiencies, and grew skeptical of cases against dominant firms, while agency budgets stagnated as the economy grew. The evidence of rising market power, from markups to merger retrospectives showing price increases after deals in concentrated markets, calls for tightening enforcement within the existing framework: stronger presumptions against mergers that raise concentration, more cases against exclusionary conduct by dominant firms, attention to potential competition and labor markets, and more money for enforcers. That framework has delivered: the Google rulings were won on the consumer welfare standard, as were challenges to the Kroger–Albertsons and JetBlue–Spirit mergers. Abandoning it for goals such as dispersing political power or protecting small business would give enforcers no principled way to decide cases, invite courts to reject them, and make antitrust a political weapon for whoever holds office, a risk the past year has made concrete. Political power and inequality are real problems that need their own remedies: campaign-finance, tax and labor law.
Evidence: The De Loecker, Eeckhout and Unger markup series and the Grullon, Larkin and Michaely concentration findings; merger retrospectives, including Kwoka’s reviews and studies of hospital and airline mergers, finding post-merger price increases in concentrated markets; the Google search and ad tech liability rulings and the Live Nation verdict, all reached under existing doctrine; the record in which the FTC and Justice Department review only about 2 percent of reported mergers in depth; and Shapiro’s analysis that the rise in concentration is real but that the link to the political power of corporations is a separate problem for other tools.
Answer to objections: To the anti-monopoly camp’s charge that the consumer welfare standard caused four decades of weak enforcement, it answers that the failure lay in how courts applied the standard and in the agencies’ timidity and budgets, not in the goal, and that recent wins prove the standard can reach the largest firms. To the restraint camp’s charge that rising concentration reflects efficient superstar firms, it answers that efficiency does not explain the price increases found after many mergers, or the exclusionary conduct courts found at Google and Live Nation, and that the costs of under-enforcement fall on consumers who cannot see the competition they lost. To the charge that more enforcement means more mistakes, it answers that the error costs run both ways and that the current presumption against intervention has been calibrated to avoid only one kind.

Camp: Keep antitrust narrow to protect scale and innovation
Adherents: The US Chamber of Commerce, whose senior vice president Sean Heather wrote in March 2025 that “the Neo-Brandeisian effort to drive transformational change to antitrust has failed” and that “The consumer welfare standard survives, and the rule of reason was not replaced by bright-line rules” (https://www.uschamber.com/antitrust/assessing-the-impact-of-the-neo-brandeisian-movement); the Chamber filed in support of Meta in the FTC’s appeal in 2026 (https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/U.S.-Chamber-Amicus-Brief-FTC-v.-Meta-D.C.-Circuit.pdf). The Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, whose president Robert Atkinson said in 2021 that “large firms aren’t innately bad” and “market power can boost economic welfare,” and that the neo-Brandeisian project aims “to limit the size and economic influence of large corporations” (https://itif.org/publications/2021/03/10/antitrust-hearings-give-momentum-progressive-crusade-based-dubious-theories); and in 2024 that “a key component of America’s arsenal of democracy is its large, highly innovative technology firms” (https://itif.org/publications/2024/05/10/america-needs-big-tech-to-beat-big-china). Also associated: the International Center for Law & Economics, the Global Antitrust Institute at George Mason University’s Antonin Scalia Law School, former FTC commissioners Joshua Wright and Christine Wilson, NetChoice and the Computer & Communications Industry Association, and at the far end libertarians such as Dominick Armentano who argue the antitrust laws should be repealed.
Case: Antitrust should stop cartels and the rare mergers or exclusionary tactics that demonstrably harm consumers, and otherwise stay out of the way. Large firms are, on average, more productive, pay higher wages, invest more in research and export more than small ones, and much of the rise in concentration reflects the most efficient firms winning customers in markets transformed by software, logistics and scale. Firms that grow because people prefer their products are what competition is supposed to produce, and punishing success discourages the investment that creates it. The consumer welfare standard, built over decades of case law and economics, disciplines enforcers by making them prove harm, and it has survived the neo-Brandeisian challenge: Congress declined to rewrite the antitrust laws, and courts rejected many of the Biden-era agencies’ novel merger theories. Turning antitrust against size itself, or against Big Tech in particular, would weaken the American companies that lead the world in technology at the moment they are competing with Chinese firms backed by their state, and Europe’s heavy regulation of digital markets shows the cost in lost innovation. The camp’s members differ in degree: the Chamber and ICLE defend existing doctrine and its case-by-case enforcement, ITIF adds a national-competitiveness argument for tolerating scale, and libertarian critics would scrap the laws altogether.
Evidence: Studies showing that local concentration fell as national concentration rose (Rossi-Hansberg, Sarte and Trachter) and that markup estimates fall when overhead costs are counted; the courts’ rejection of the FTC’s challenges to Microsoft–Activision and Meta–Within and its monopoly case against Meta, and the Google judges’ refusal to order breakups; the low rate at which reported mergers raise concern; the decline in many consumer prices and the free provision of search, maps, email and social media; the concentration of the world’s largest technology firms in the United States rather than in Europe; and the delays to new products in Europe that firms attribute to the Digital Markets Act.
Answer to objections: To the anti-monopoly camp’s charge that the consumer welfare standard ignores power, it answers that the standard already covers quality, innovation and harm to trading partners, and that “power” without a measure of harm is an invitation to arbitrary enforcement and political favoritism, which the controversies of 2025 and 2026 illustrate. To the more-vigorous-enforcement camp’s evidence of rising markups and post-merger price increases, it answers that the markup evidence is contested and that the retrospectives cover a small, non-random sample of deals. To the charge that large firms entrench themselves, it points to the speed with which TikTok challenged Meta and generative AI challenged Google, and argues that dynamic competition from new technology disciplines incumbents faster than any lawsuit.

Camp: Regulate digital gatekeepers with up-front rules
Adherents: The European Commission, which enforces the Digital Markets Act: “The Digital Markets Act is the EU’s law to make the markets in the digital sector fairer and more contestable” (https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/about-dma_en); it has designated seven gatekeepers and fined Apple €500 million and Meta €200 million under the act in April 2025. Fiona Scott Morton of the Yale School of Management, a former chief economist of the Justice Department’s Antitrust Division, in June 2025: “antitrust enforcement alone is no longer sufficient to restore competition in these markets” and “we need a framework of light-touch, pro-competitive regulation” (https://www.promarket.org/2025/06/26/a-pro-competitive-handbook-for-policymakers-to-unlock-digital-competition/). Also associated: the UK Competition and Markets Authority and its Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024; Jason Furman, who chaired the UK’s 2019 Digital Competition Expert Panel; Japan’s smartphone software competition law; the Stigler Center’s 2019 committee report on digital platforms; and in the United States Senators Amy Klobuchar and Chuck Grassley, sponsors of the American Innovation and Choice Online Act, which would have barred dominant platforms from favoring their own products.
Case: Digital platforms with network effects are a special case, and they need a special tool. A search engine, app store or operating system that billions of people use becomes a gateway every business must pass through, and its owner is tempted to favor its own services, copy its customers and lock users in. General antitrust law reaches this conduct only after years of litigation, a market definition fight and a remedy a single judge must design and police, by which time the market has tipped. The better approach is to identify the few gatekeepers by objective size criteria and set clear obligations in advance: no self-preferencing, freedom to uninstall apps and change defaults, the right for developers to tell customers about cheaper offers, interoperability and data portability, enforced by a specialist regulator that can adjust the rules as technology changes. The goal is the economic one the consumer welfare tradition shares, contestable markets where rivals can compete on the merits, not hostility to size as such, which separates this camp from the anti-monopoly movement; but it agrees with that movement that lawsuits alone are too slow. Europe has adopted this model, the United Kingdom and Japan have followed with their own versions, and its supporters in the United States want Congress to do the same.
Evidence: The time US monopoly cases take: the Google search case was filed in 2020, decided in 2024, remedied in 2025 and remains on appeal, and Apple’s dispute with Epic Games over app store steering, begun in 2020, reached the Supreme Court in 2026 after a contempt finding; the EU designations of seven gatekeepers and 23 core platform services; the Court of Justice’s July 2026 ruling upholding Google’s Android fine and holding that regulators need not run an as-efficient-competitor test where network effects make rival entry practically impossible; the changes gatekeepers have made in Europe under the act, including choice screens for browsers and search engines and alternative app distribution on iPhones; and the EU General Court’s 2026 rulings that gatekeepers must comply first and challenge later.
Answer to objections: To the charge that rules freeze technology and delay products, it answers that the obligations are principles with room to adapt, that the delays were the gatekeepers’ own choices, and that European users have gained options, such as alternative app stores, that US users lack. To the charge that the rules target American firms, it answers that designation follows size and user numbers, not nationality, and covers ByteDance and Booking, and that American firms dominate the list because they dominate the markets. To the anti-monopoly camp’s preference for breakups, it answers that rules can be applied now while a structural case takes a decade. To the restraint camp’s claim that existing law suffices, it points to how little the US Google remedies have changed so far and to Judge Brinkema’s finding in 2026 that a court-ordered divestiture of Google’s ad exchange would be unprecedented and complex.

## Crux

Crux: Should the law act against a large firm’s hold on a market before anyone can prove it has harmed the people who buy from it, sell to it or work for it, or only once that harm is shown?

Every camp agrees that cartels should be punished and that some monopolies are harmful. They divide over when power alone is enough to act. The anti-monopoly camp says concentrated power is itself the danger, to prices over time, to workers, suppliers and rivals, and to self-government, so the law should prevent it through structural rules and breakups without waiting for proof of injury in each case. The camp that wants vigorous enforcement of the consumer welfare standard says the law should act on evidence of harm to competition, but that presumptions against concentrating mergers and dominant-firm conduct should make that evidence easier to establish. The camp that wants antitrust kept narrow says size usually reflects efficiency, so enforcers should have to prove harm case by case, because acting on power alone would punish success and invite political favoritism. The gatekeeper-rules camp says that for a few digital platforms, whose markets tip and whose cases take a decade, rules set in advance should replace the wait for proof.

What evidence could settle: whether the rise in national concentration and markups since 1980 reflects weaker competition or more efficient firms, and how much of it shows up in local markets where people shop and work; how often mergers that passed review later raised prices, cut wages or slowed innovation, and how often blocked mergers would have helped; whether concentrated employers hold down pay; whether conduct remedies such as those imposed on Google restore competition within a few years, and whether past breakups such as AT&T’s did; whether the Digital Markets Act makes European markets measurably more contestable or mainly delays products; and whether the largest firms out-innovate the rivals they might otherwise face, including in AI.

What no data can close: whether dispersing private economic power is a goal worth some cost in efficiency, for the sake of liberty, small enterprise and democratic self-government; how to weigh the risk of blocking a beneficial deal against the risk of allowing a harmful one that cannot be undone; how much discretion to entrust to enforcers and regulators, and whether they should answer to the president or be insulated from politics; and whether national competitiveness against China should count in deciding how large American firms may grow.
