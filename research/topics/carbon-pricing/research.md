# Carbon Pricing — research

Slug: carbon-pricing
Scope: Carbon taxes, cap-and-trade, and tradable credits for avoided or removed emissions.
Researched: 2026-10-06

## Overview

Carbon pricing puts a money cost on releasing greenhouse gases so that the cost of climate damage shows up in the prices people and firms pay. It comes in three main forms. A carbon tax sets the price directly: a government charges a fixed amount per tonne of carbon dioxide (or its equivalent) emitted or contained in fuel. An emissions trading system, or cap-and-trade, sets the quantity instead: a government caps total emissions from covered sectors, issues allowances up to the cap, and lets firms buy and sell them, so the market sets the price. Carbon crediting lets a project that avoids emissions (protecting a forest, replacing a cookstove, destroying an industrial gas) or removes carbon from the air (planting trees, direct air capture) earn a tradable credit for each tonne, which a company or country can buy to count against its own emissions — voluntarily, or inside a compliance scheme such as an ETS, the airline scheme CORSIA, or the Paris Agreement’s Article 6. This topic covers all three, plus the border carbon adjustments that extend a domestic carbon price to imports. It deliberately excludes the broader question of how fast economies should decarbonize (net-zero pace), supply-side restrictions on extraction (fossil fuel phase-out), and engineered carbon removal as a technology (carbon dioxide removal), touching them only where they bear on how carbon is priced.

The why-now event: on 17 July 2026 the European Commission tabled its proposal to revise the EU Emissions Trading System, the world’s largest carbon market by revenue and the oldest major one. The proposal keeps the system and extends it toward the EU’s 2040 target, but slows the yearly tightening of the cap from the current 4.3 percent to 3.7 percent for 2031–2035 and 1.7 percent from 2036, keeps free allowances for heavy industry into the 2030s, slows their phase-out alongside the new carbon border levy to 2038, and creates a €100 billion Industrial Decarbonisation Bank (European Commission ETS review factsheet, July 2026; ICAP summary of the proposal). It came after months of pressure: on 26 February 2026 Italy’s industry minister Adolfo Urso called the ETS “only a tax, a tariff on the energy-intensive companies” and asked for it to be suspended (Euronews, 26 February 2026); Poland and nine other member states asked for slower removal of free allowances; and a March 2026 oil supply shock tied to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz — which the World Bank calls the largest on record — sent energy prices up across Europe. EU leaders declined to suspend the system, and the proposal now goes to the Council and Parliament, with a deal targeted for the first half of 2027.

The debate is live because carbon pricing is spreading and shrinking at once. The World Bank’s State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2026 counts 87 carbon taxes and trading systems covering 29 percent of global emissions, with India, Japan and Viet Nam launching national trading systems and the EU’s border carbon levy entering its definitive phase on 1 January 2026. In the same period Canada scrapped its consumer carbon price (1 April 2025) and repealed it in law (March 2026), British Columbia ended the first broad carbon tax in North America, the EU postponed its second trading system for buildings and road fuel to 2028, the United States withdrew its social cost of carbon estimates (January 2025), and a global carbon price on shipping at the International Maritime Organization was postponed in October 2025, with an adoption vote now set for December 2026. Meanwhile, the voluntary market in carbon credits has shrunk from its 2021 peak after peer-reviewed studies found most credits issued did not represent real reductions. The people in this argument are economists who see pricing as the cheapest way to cut emissions, governments and the EU institutions that run trading systems, heavy industry and the countries that host it, populist and conservative parties that campaign against carbon taxes, climate-justice groups that oppose putting a price on pollution at all, and a carbon-credit industry of developers, standard-setters, buyers and host countries in the Global South.

- Data year: prices as of 1 April 2026
- Source label: World Bank, State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2026; World Bank, State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2024 for the benchmarks
- Source URL: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099051826185087983/pdf/P502283-3ee8b1dc-6f1a-46dd-8141-e6534fd34af9.pdf (benchmarks: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099081624122529330/pdf/P50228315fd8d1050186341ea02e1c107bc.pdf)
- Note: The average direct carbon price worldwide is about US$21 a tonne, double its 2016 level and well below the benchmark ranges economists and the IPCC associate with the Paris goals.
- Context: The average rose about seven percent in the year to April 2026, driven by ETS prices. Prices vary widely: some European carbon taxes and the EU ETS sit above US$60, while many systems in Asia and Latin America price at a few dollars. The High-Level Commission on Carbon Prices, chaired by Joseph Stiglitz and Nicholas Stern in 2017, gave a price corridor for a well-below-2 °C path assuming supporting policies; the IPCC range is the modeled marginal cost of the last tonne abated on 1.5 °C pathways. Those who favor pricing read the gap as evidence prices must rise; those who favor other instruments read it as evidence prices cannot politically reach the needed level.

### 8. Emission reductions attributed to carbon pricing, meta-analysis
- Label: Emission cuts from introducing a carbon price
- Value: 5 to 21
- Unit: percent reduction in covered emissions (statistically significant effects, by scheme); 4 to 15 after correcting for publication bias
- Data year: published 2024; studies of schemes in operation up to the early 2020s
- Source label: Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al., “Systematic review and meta-analysis of ex-post evaluations on the effectiveness of carbon pricing,” Nature Communications, 2024
- Source URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48512-w
- Note: A 2024 review of 80 causal studies found that introducing a carbon price cut emissions by between 5 and 21 percent in at least 17 of the 21 schemes studied.
- Context: The study pooled 483 effect sizes from 80 ex-post evaluations of 21 carbon pricing schemes and found “immediate and substantial emission reductions for at least 17 of these policies, despite the low level of prices in most instances.” The reductions are measured against what the studies estimate would otherwise have happened, over the evaluation periods of each study, not as a yearly rate. The authors flag gaps: dozens of schemes have never been rigorously evaluated, and the evidence says little about how much more reduction a higher price buys. An earlier review reached a more skeptical reading (stat 9); the two use different methods and units, and which reading is right is one of the open questions on this page.
- Visualization: range (5 to 21 percent, with the bias-corrected 4 to 15 marked)

### 9. Annual emission reductions attributed to carbon pricing, earlier review
- Label: Yearly emission cuts from carbon pricing, 2021 review
- Value: 0 to 2
- Unit: percent per year (aggregate reductions in most studies); EU ETS 0 to 1.5 percent per year
- Data year: published 2021; 37 studies of policies since 1990
- Source label: Jessica F. Green, “Does carbon pricing reduce emissions? A review of ex-post analyses,” Environmental Research Letters, 2021
- Source URL: https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/abdae9
- Note: A 2021 review of 37 ex-post studies found that most estimated carbon pricing cut emissions by between 0 and 2 percent a year.
- Context: Green found that only 37 studies assessed the actual effect of carbon pricing on emissions, most of them about Europe, and that carbon taxes generally performed better than emissions trading systems. She compared the results with the IPCC’s finding that emissions must fall 45 percent below 2010 levels by 2030 for 1.5 °C and concluded that “the evidence indicates that carbon pricing has a limited impact on emissions.” The figure is a yearly rate and is not directly comparable with the 2024 meta-analysis’s cumulative 5–21 percent; a 1–2 percent yearly cut sustained for a decade is in the same range. The disagreement between the two papers is less about the size of the effect than about whether that effect is large enough to make pricing the main climate instrument.

## Questions

### 1. Does a carbon price cut emissions enough to be the main climate policy?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: main instrument / supporting instrument
- Why contested: Every serious review finds that carbon prices reduce covered emissions, so the dispute is about size. A 2024 meta-analysis of 80 ex-post studies found cuts of 5 to 21 percent across at least 17 of 21 schemes (Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al., Nature Communications, 2024), while a 2021 review of 37 studies found yearly cuts mostly of 0 to 2 percent and judged the impact “limited” (Green, Environmental Research Letters, 2021). Both figures are compatible; the disagreement is over whether that effect, at today’s prices, is a foundation or a supplement.
- Main instrument: Those who favor pricing argue that the measured effects came from prices that were mostly low, often under US$20 a tonne, so they understate what a price at the benchmark level would do. A price reaches every emitter and every choice at once, including ones regulators cannot foresee, and it finds the cheapest cuts first. The EU, whose system has run longest at a meaningful price, has halved covered emissions since 2005, and the UK’s carbon price floor, introduced in 2013, helped drive coal from about 40 percent of its electricity in 2012 to zero when its last coal plant closed on 30 September 2024 (Ember, 2024).
- Supporting instrument: Those who see pricing as secondary argue that the halving in Europe and the drop in California came mostly from renewable subsidies, coal phase-out laws, efficiency standards and recessions, with the price playing a smaller part. A 1 to 2 percent yearly cut is far short of what the Paris goals require, and the price needed to do the job alone is, on the IPCC’s 1.5 °C range, several times the current world average of about US$21. Standards and public investment, which built the cheap solar, wind and batteries that now drive emissions down, have the better record.

### 2. Can carbon prices rise high enough to matter and survive politically?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: politically durable / politically doomed
- Why contested: Carbon pricing now covers 29 percent of global emissions and raises over US$100 billion a year, yet Australia repealed its carbon tax in 2014, France froze its fuel-tax rise after the 2018 gilets jaunes protests, Canada scrapped its consumer price in 2025, and Italy and other EU members pressed in 2026 to suspend or soften the EU system. Washington voters, by contrast, kept their program in 2024 by about 62 percent.
- Politically durable: Those who expect pricing to last point to its spread over two decades, to the EU and California systems that have survived recessions and energy shocks, and to Washington’s 2024 vote. They argue the failures came from visible consumer taxes introduced without visible benefits, and that design fixes — returning revenue, funding local projects, phasing prices in, covering industry upstream — make pricing durable.
- Politically doomed: Those who expect pricing to stall argue that every time a carbon price becomes high enough to be felt at the pump or on a heating bill, it becomes the opposition’s best campaign issue, as it did in Canada, Australia and France. The 2026 EU fight happened in the system’s strongest jurisdiction, and the result was a slower cap and longer free allocation. A policy that works only at prices voters will not accept is, in this view, not a policy that works.

### 3. Should carbon pricing revenue go back to households or into clean investment?
- Tag: value
- Poles: return as dividends / invest in transition
- Why contested: Economists often favor equal per-person rebates because they make a carbon price progressive and keep the government from growing, and Canada ran the largest example. A 20-country survey published in 2025 found that a carbon tax with equal cash transfers drew 37 percent support in high-income countries, against around 60 percent when revenue funded green infrastructure or subsidies (Dechezleprêtre et al., American Economic Review, 2025). Washington and California now call their systems “cap-and-invest.”
- Return as dividends: Those who favor dividends argue that returning the money keeps the price signal intact while leaving most lower-income households better off, as Canada’s Parliamentary Budget Officer found for direct costs. It answers the charge that carbon pricing is a tax grab, and it lets households, not governments, decide how to adjust.
- Invest in transition: Those who favor investment argue that dividends were invisible to voters — Canadians rarely knew they received them — and so failed politically, while spending on transit, clean power and home retrofits produces benefits people can see. Investment also lowers the cost of cutting emissions, which reduces the price needed, and can be directed to the communities most exposed to pollution.

### 4. Do carbon credits from avoided deforestation, cookstoves and similar projects represent real reductions?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: mostly real / mostly not
- Why contested: A 2024 synthesis of 14 rigorous studies estimated that less than 16 percent of the credits it examined represented real reductions (Probst et al., Nature Communications, 2024). The credit industry has since adopted integrity standards, the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market labels approved methodologies, and highly rated credits trade at a premium. The open question is whether reformed credits are reliable, not whether past ones were.
- Mostly real: Those who defend credits argue that the studies examined older methodologies and project types, some of which have since been rejected by the Integrity Council or replaced with stricter baselines, satellite monitoring and buffer pools. Some project types score well even in the critical studies, such as destruction of the industrial gas HFC-23. Credits are, on this view, a fixable measurement problem attached to real finance for forests and communities.
- Mostly not: Those who doubt credits argue that the failure is structural: a credit requires a counterfactual — what would have happened without the project — that cannot be observed, and every party to the sale, from developer to verifier to buyer, gains from an inflated estimate. New rules have not yet been tested by the same kind of independent ex-post study, and until they are, a credit lets a buyer claim a reduction that may not exist.

### 5. Should buyers be allowed to count purchased credits against their own emissions?
- Tag: value
- Poles: count credits / cut at source
- Why contested: The question sits underneath corporate net-zero claims, the airline scheme CORSIA, the Paris Agreement’s Article 6 and proposals to let removal credits into compliance markets such as the EU ETS. Even if every credit were real, a tonne bought abroad lets the buyer emit a tonne at home.
- Count credits: Those who favor counting credits argue that a tonne avoided or removed anywhere has the same climate effect, so letting buyers pay for the cheapest tonnes lowers the cost of meeting targets and moves money to developing countries that need it. Kenya’s president William Ruto called Africa’s carbon sinks “an unparalleled economic goldmine” in 2023. Removals will be needed for emissions that cannot be cut, and only a market will pay for them.
- Cut at source: Those who favor cutting at source argue that offsetting delays the changes rich emitters must make, keeps pollution in the communities near their plants, and shifts the work of mitigation onto land and people in the Global South, sometimes at the cost of Indigenous land rights. If credits are used at all, on this view, they should finance additional action rather than cancel out a buyer’s own emissions.

### 6. Should heavy industry be shielded from the carbon price, and should imports be charged at the border?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: shield and adjust / price fully
- Why contested: Free allowances and border levies both answer leakage — the risk that a carbon price moves production and its emissions to places without one. The EU began charging its border levy on cement, steel, aluminum, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen in 2026, while its July 2026 proposal keeps free allowances for industry into the 2030s. How large leakage actually is, and whether the border levy is protection in disguise, are both disputed.
- Shield and adjust: Those who favor protection argue that without free allocation or a border charge, a carbon price simply exports factories, jobs and emissions to China, India or the United States, as Italy’s and Poland’s governments argued in 2026. A border levy also pressures trading partners to price carbon, and the World Bank reports that several, from Albania to Malaysia, have cited it in moving toward their own prices.
- Price fully: Those who favor a full price argue that ex-post studies have found little evidence of large-scale leakage so far, and that free allocation blunts the incentive to decarbonize exactly where emissions are hardest to cut. Developing-country governments add that the border levy charges their exporters for a cost they did not choose and that rich countries caused most of the warming; India, China and South Africa have objected to it as unilateral trade protection.

### 7. What price should a tonne of carbon carry?
- Tag: value
- Poles: high social cost / low or none
- Why contested: In theory the price should equal the social cost of carbon, the present value of damage from one more tonne. US government estimates have ranged from about US$1 to US$380 a tonne depending on discount rate and whether damage abroad counts, and the United States withdrew its estimates in January 2025.
- High social cost: Those who favor a high value argue that future generations’ welfare should count nearly as much as ours, so discount rates should be low, and that a national price should reflect global damage because emissions mix globally and every country benefits when others act. Modeling of 1.5 °C pathways puts the needed price in the hundreds of dollars.
- Low or none: Those who favor a low value argue that governments should weigh the costs to their own citizens today, that market interest rates are the honest discount rate, and that damage estimates thousands of years out are too uncertain to set prices on. A domestic price high enough to matter imposes certain costs now for uncertain benefits that depend on other countries acting too.

### 8. Should rich and poor countries face the same carbon price?
- Tag: value
- Poles: one global price / differentiated prices
- Why contested: Average prices range from about US$68 a tonne in Europe and Central Asia to a few dollars in Latin America and the Middle East (World Bank, 2026). The IMF proposed tiered floors of US$75, US$50 and US$25 by income; the shipping levy debated at the International Maritime Organization would apply one price to every ship.
- One global price: Those who favor a single price argue that a tonne does the same damage wherever it is emitted, so the cheapest cuts should be made first wherever they are, and differentiated prices invite leakage toward low-price countries. Fairness, on this view, is better handled by transferring revenue than by varying the price.
- Differentiated prices: Those who favor different prices argue that the countries that emitted least historically and have the least income should not pay the same per tonne as those that caused the problem, and that the Paris Agreement’s principle of “common but differentiated responsibilities” commits rich countries to move first.

## Positions

### 1. Make a rising carbon price the core of climate policy
- Adherents: Climate Leadership Council (institution), whose “Economists’ Statement on Carbon Dividends,” published in the Wall Street Journal on 17 January 2019, says “a carbon tax offers the most cost-effective lever to reduce carbon emissions” and was signed by former Federal Reserve chairs Ben Bernanke, Alan Greenspan and Janet Yellen and over two dozen Nobel laureates — https://clcouncil.org/economists-statement/ ; International Monetary Fund (institution), whose staff argued in a May 2022 blog by Jean Chateau, Florence Jaumotte and Gregor Schwerhoff for an international carbon price floor of US$75, US$50 and US$25 a tonne for high-, middle- and low-income countries by 2030 — https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2022/05/19/blog-why-countries-must-cooperate-on-carbon-prices ; Citizens’ Climate Lobby, a volunteer organization that campaigns for a US carbon fee returned as a dividend — https://citizensclimatelobby.org/
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Climate change is a pollution problem, and the cheapest way to cut pollution is to make polluters pay for it. A price on carbon reaches every emitter, every product and every choice, from how a steel plant runs to whether a family buys a heat pump, without a regulator having to know in advance where the cheapest cuts are. Firms and households that can cut cheaply do so first; those that cannot pay. That is why a price achieves a given cut at a lower cost than any mix of mandates, and why almost every economist, left and right, supports one. The price should start moderate, rise predictably, and cover as much of the economy as possible. Its revenue can be returned to households so that most lower-income families come out ahead, or used to cut other taxes, and a border adjustment can protect industry from countries that do not price carbon. Standards and subsidies have a place where markets fail, as in research, but they should support the price, not replace it.
- Variation inside the camp: tax versus cap-and-trade (a tax fixes the price, a cap fixes the quantity); dividends versus investment versus tax cuts for the revenue; one global price versus tiered floors such as the IMF’s.
- Best evidence: The 2024 meta-analysis of 80 ex-post studies found that carbon pricing cut emissions by 5 to 21 percent in at least 17 of 21 schemes, “despite the low level of prices in most instances” (Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al., 2024). EU ETS emissions have halved since 2005. The UK’s carbon price floor, introduced in 2013, helped take coal from about 40 percent of British electricity in 2012 to zero in 2024 (Ember). Carbon pricing raised about US$107 billion in 2025 (World Bank).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that prices never rise high enough to matter and voters reject them): Prices were low because systems were designed with loose caps and generous free allocation, not because pricing does not work, and the EU fixed its oversupply with the Market Stability Reserve after 2018. The political failures came from consumer taxes whose rebates voters could not see; Washington’s 62 percent vote shows a program can survive a direct challenge when its benefits are visible. Standards and subsidies cost more per tonne and carry their own political risk: many of the clean-energy tax credits the United States enacted in 2022 were cut back or phased out by a law signed in July 2025.

### 2. Pair a modest carbon price with standards and public investment
- Adherents: Third Way (institution), whose February 2020 memo “Carbon Pricing: One Piece of the Climate Puzzle” says carbon pricing “remains a crucial part of the climate puzzle” but that “nearly all carbon prices currently in place are too low to drive the ambitious decarbonization proponents have sought” — https://www.thirdway.org/memo/carbon-pricing-one-piece-of-the-climate-puzzle ; Danny Cullenward and David Victor, authors of “Making Climate Policy Work” (Polity, 2020), who argue that markets’ problems “are structural” and that policy must rely “more heavily on smart regulation and industrial policy” — https://www.ghgpolicy.org/book
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Carbon pricing works in theory and helps at the margin, but two decades of experience show what it can and cannot do. Prices have stayed far below the level needed because the industries that would pay most have the political power to keep them low, and voters punish prices they can feel. The cuts that have happened came mostly from policies that target technologies directly: renewable mandates and subsidies that drove the cost of solar and wind down, efficiency and vehicle standards, coal phase-out laws, and public investment in grids and research. These work where prices do not, in sectors where a few dollars a tonne changes nothing, such as steel, cement or buildings, and where the problem is that the clean technology is not yet built at scale. Keep a carbon price as a backstop and a revenue source, cover industry with it, but do not stake the climate on it.
- Variation inside the camp: how much weight the price should carry; whether to rely on public investment (the US Inflation Reduction Act model) or on regulation and standards; whether offsets should be kept out of compliance markets entirely.
- Best evidence: Studies of California’s program have found that complementary policies, not the cap, drove most of its emission cuts while allowance prices stayed near the floor. The World Bank reported in 2024 that less than 1 percent of global emissions faced a price at the lower bound of the level needed for the Paris goals. The 2021 review of 37 ex-post studies found yearly reductions mostly between 0 and 2 percent (Green, 2021). Solar module prices fell by roughly 90 percent over the 2010s, driven largely by subsidies and deployment mandates in Germany and China rather than by carbon prices [unverified].
- Answer to the strongest objection (that standards and subsidies cost more per tonne than a price): The cost advantage of pricing is real in a model and small in practice, because the price never reaches the level the model assumes. Subsidies and standards created markets for technologies that then became cheaper than fossil fuels, which no plausible carbon price would have done on its own; that learning-curve effect is the largest cost saving in climate policy. A theoretically efficient policy that cannot be passed or sustained at an effective level does not cut emissions.

### 3. Repeal or suspend carbon prices to cut energy costs
- Adherents: Canadian Taxpayers Federation (institution), which in March 2025 applauded Pierre Poilievre’s pledge to end Canada’s industrial carbon tax, arguing that “carbon taxes on refineries make gas more expensive, carbon taxes on utilities make home heating more expensive and carbon taxes on fertilizer plants increase costs for farmers and that makes groceries more expensive” — https://www.taxpayer.com/newsroom/taxpayers-federation-applauds-poilievre%E2%80%99s-commitment-to-end-industrial-carbon-tax?id=18778 ; Pierre Poilievre, leader of Canada’s Conservative Party, who pledged in March 2025 to repeal the federal carbon pricing law for industry as well as consumers — https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/poilievre-industrial-carbon-price-1.7485500 ; Adolfo Urso, Italy’s industry minister, who on 26 February 2026 called for the EU ETS to be suspended, calling it “only a tax, a tariff on the energy-intensive companies that struggle to remain competitive” — https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/02/26/italy-calls-for-suspension-of-eu-carbon-market
- Case in the camp’s own voice: A carbon price is a tax on energy, and energy is in everything people buy. It raises what families pay for fuel, heating and food, and what factories pay to make steel, fertilizer and cement, while doing little for the climate because the countries where emissions are growing fastest do not impose one. The result is higher living costs at home and production, jobs and emissions moving abroad. Voters understood this: they rejected carbon taxes in Australia and Canada and forced France to back down. Rebates do not fix it, because they are a government handing back part of what it took while the economy pays the rest. Emissions should fall through innovation, nuclear power and cleaner technology that wins on cost, not through policies designed to make energy more expensive.
- Variation inside the camp: full repeal (Canada’s Conservatives, the US Republican position against any federal carbon tax) versus suspension or slowing pending reform (Italy, and the ten EU governments led by Poland that asked in 2026 for a flatter cap and longer free allocation); some, such as Poilievre, would replace the tax with tax credits rewarding low-emission production.
- Best evidence: Canada’s Parliamentary Budget Officer estimated in 2024 that most households faced a net cost from the federal fuel charge once its effect on wages and investment income was counted, even though rebates exceeded direct fuel costs. A carbon tax drew some of the lowest support of any climate policy in a 20-country survey published in 2025 (Dechezleprêtre et al.). Europe’s energy-intensive industries have cut output and closed plants since 2022, and the EU’s own July 2026 proposal slows the cap and extends free allocation in response.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that removing the price removes the cheapest way to cut emissions and leaves climate damage unpaid): Canada’s and the UK’s emissions trends were driven mostly by power-sector changes that do not need a consumer price, and the cuts achievable from a politically tolerable price are small. The damage figures that justify high prices rest on contested discount rates and long-range models, while the cost of energy today is certain and falls hardest on rural and lower-income households. A country acting alone bears the cost while the climate benefit depends on others.

### 4. Cut emissions at the source without pricing or trading pollution
- Adherents: Indigenous Environmental Network (institution), which calls carbon pricing a “false solution” and writes that “cap and trade systems are a way for polluters to continue to pollute and make more profit” — https://www.ienearth.org/carbon-pricing/ ; Climate Justice Alliance (institution), which with the Indigenous Environmental Network released the report “Carbon Pricing: A Critical Perspective for Community Resistance” at COP23 in November 2017, describing cap-and-trade, offsets and carbon taxes as false solutions that “do not cut emissions at source” — https://climatejusticealliance.org/groundbreaking-carbon-pricing-report-released-indigenous-environmental-network-climate-justice-alliance-cop-23/ ; Tom Goldtooth, executive director of the Indigenous Environmental Network, quoted in the same release
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Putting a price on carbon turns the atmosphere into property and pollution into a right that can be bought. It lets the biggest polluters keep polluting as long as they can pay, and it concentrates the harm in the low-income, Black, Brown and Indigenous communities next to refineries, power plants and industrial zones, which breathe the other pollutants that come with the carbon. Trading and offsets are worse: they let a corporation in the North claim credit for a forest in the South, often on land taken from or controlled without the consent of the Indigenous peoples who live there, while the reductions on paper do not happen. The answer is not a better market but a managed decline of fossil fuel extraction and burning, binding limits on facilities, keeping fossil fuels in the ground, and public investment in a just transition led by the communities most affected.
- Variation inside the camp: some groups accept a carbon tax whose revenue funds frontline communities while rejecting trading and offsets; others reject any pricing as commodification.
- Best evidence: The 2024 synthesis that found less than 16 percent of studied credits represented real reductions (Probst et al.); the 2023 Science study that found most avoided-deforestation projects examined had not significantly reduced deforestation (West et al.); a 2018 study of California’s cap-and-trade program from 2011 to 2015, which found that 52 percent of regulated facilities reported higher in-state greenhouse gas emissions after trading began and that neighborhoods with rising emissions nearby had higher proportions of people of color and low-income residents (Cushing et al., PLOS Medicine, 2018; critics note the study had no control group for the post-recession rebound); and the low price and limited cuts recorded in most systems.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that direct limits without a price cost more and that the EU ETS has halved its covered emissions): The UK and others closed coal plants with bans and limits, which work on a known timetable; the price in such cases sped up what regulation was already doing. A cost estimate that leaves out the health of fence-line communities and the rights of forest peoples is not a full cost. Pricing has had thirty years as the favored policy of governments and economists, and global emissions are still higher than when it started.

### 5. Scale trade in high-integrity carbon credits
- Adherents: Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market (institution), whose chair Annette Nazareth said at the launch of its Core Carbon Principles on 29 March 2023 that a shared understanding of high integrity “is a pre-condition for the development and growth of a viable and vibrant” voluntary carbon market — https://icvcm.org/integrity-council-launches-global-benchmark-for-high-integrity-carbon-credits/ ; William Ruto, president of Kenya, who at the Africa Climate Summit in Nairobi on 4 September 2023 called Africa’s carbon sinks “an unparalleled economic goldmine” that “should translate into billions of dollars” — https://www.france24.com/en/africa/20230905-kenya-bets-on-carbon-credits-as-it-hosts-african-climate-summit
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Carbon credits move money from those who emit to those who can cut or remove emissions at lower cost, and most of those places are in the developing world, where forests, cookstoves, methane capture and clean power are short of finance. The early market had serious quality problems, and the answer has been to fix them, not abandon the tool: independent standards, the Integrity Council’s labels, ratings agencies, CORSIA eligibility rules and the UN’s new Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism. Companies that buy credits are cutting their own emissions faster, not slower: MSCI found in 2023 that firms using material quantities of credits were decarbonizing at twice the rate of those that did not, and credits paid for while they cut are better than nothing paid at all. Removal credits, from forests to direct air capture, will be the main way to pay for taking carbon out of the air.
- Variation inside the camp: credits as a supplement to a buyer’s own cuts versus as an offset counted against them; nature-based versus engineered removals; voluntary use versus inclusion in compliance markets such as the EU ETS and Article 6 trading between countries.
- Best evidence: Highly rated credits now trade at a premium, and CORSIA-eligible credits traded at US$15–22 a tonne from September 2025 against US$1–14 for most other types (World Bank, 2026); capital committed to future credit projects reached about US$12 to 16 billion in 2025 (World Bank, citing AlliedOffsets/MSCI); the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism issued its first credits; and even the critical 2024 synthesis found that some project types, such as HFC-23 destruction, delivered most of their claimed reductions (Probst et al.).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that most credits have not represented real reductions): The studies looked at a market built before integrity standards existed and at project types the new standards have tightened or rejected. Every regulated market has needed rules to establish trust, and the response to measurement error is better measurement — satellite monitoring of forests, dynamic baselines, buffer pools and ratings — not closing the main channel of private climate finance to the countries that most need it.

## Crux

Can a price on carbon, at a level governments can actually sustain, do enough of the work of cutting emissions to justify what it costs the people who pay it — or is the work better done some other way?

What evidence could settle: How much emissions fall per dollar of carbon price, and whether that effect grows as prices rise; how much of the declines in the EU, California and the UK the price caused as opposed to subsidies, standards and coal closures; how large carbon leakage really is; whether reformed carbon credits deliver the reductions they claim when tested by independent ex-post studies; and whether designs that return or visibly reinvest revenue, as in Washington, reliably survive the rise in energy prices that a stronger cap would bring. Each of these is measurable, and the record is now long enough — two decades in Europe, over a decade in California, eighty-seven instruments worldwide — that the answers are sharpening.

What no data can close: How much present costs to households and industry should weigh against future climate damage, and how far into the future that damage should count; whether a country should price the harm its emissions do to people abroad; whether rich and poor countries, and rich and poor households, should pay the same per tonne; and whether it is acceptable in principle for an emitter to pay for the right to pollute, or to pay someone else to reduce on its behalf. Those who would make pricing the core of policy, those who would pair it with standards, those who would repeal it, those who reject pricing pollution, and those who would scale credit markets answer these questions differently even when they agree on the numbers.
