# Carbon Pricing — research

Slug: carbon-pricing
Scope: Carbon taxes, cap-and-trade, and tradable credits for avoided or removed emissions.
Researched: 2026-11-23

## Overview

Carbon pricing puts a money cost on releasing greenhouse gases, so that the damage those gases do to the climate shows up in the prices that firms and households pay. It comes in three main forms. A carbon tax sets the price directly: a government charges a fixed amount per tonne of carbon dioxide, or its equivalent in other gases, emitted or contained in fuel. An emissions trading system, or cap-and-trade, sets the quantity instead: a government caps total emissions from the sectors it covers, issues allowances up to the cap, and lets firms buy and sell them, so the market sets the price. Carbon crediting lets a project that avoids emissions (protecting a forest, replacing a cookstove, destroying an industrial gas) or removes carbon from the air (planting trees, capturing carbon directly) earn a tradable credit for each tonne, which a company or a country can buy and count against its own emissions, either voluntarily or inside a compliance scheme such as an emissions trading system, the aviation scheme CORSIA or the Paris Agreement’s Article 6. This topic covers all three, along with the border carbon levies that extend a domestic carbon price to imports and the free allowances that shield heavy industry from it. It deliberately leaves aside how fast economies should reach net zero, restrictions on fossil fuel supply and extraction, and the wider argument over growth and the climate, touching them only where they bear on how carbon is priced.

The why-now event: on 12 November 2026 the European Parliament and the Council of the EU reached a provisional agreement to give energy-intensive industries about 121 million additional free emission allowances for 2026 to 2030, on top of the free allocation already planned, to protect them from the risk of production moving to countries with laxer climate rules (Eunews, 13 November 2026, https://www.eunews.it/en/2026/11/13/council-and-parliament-agree-on-free-ets-co2-allowance-increase-to-shield-industry-from-foreign-competition/). It is the latest step in a year-long fight over the EU Emissions Trading System, the world’s oldest major carbon market and the largest by revenue. On 26 February 2026 Italy’s industry minister Adolfo Urso called for the system to be suspended (Euronews). A March 2026 cut in world oil supply of about 10 million barrels a day, tied to disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, which the World Bank calls the largest oil shock on record, sent energy prices up across Europe (World Bank, State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2026). On 17 July 2026 the European Commission proposed rules for the system’s next phase, 2031 to 2040, that slow the yearly tightening of the cap from 4.3 percent now to 3.7 percent for 2031 to 2035 and 1.7 percent from 2036, keep free allowances for industry through 2040, and stretch the phase-out of free allowances in sectors covered by the EU’s carbon border levy from 2034 to 2038 (ICAP, https://icapcarbonaction.com/en/news/eu-commission-publishes-eu-ets-review-proposal). At their 15 and 16 October 2026 summit, EU leaders asked the Commission to update its assessment of the second trading system, for heating and road fuels, already postponed to 2028, after Poland, Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic pressed to delay it to 2030; no further postponement was agreed (EU Today, 17 October 2026, https://eutoday.net/eu-ets2-delay-carbon-pricing-energy-costs-summit/). Two more decisions are close at hand: COP31 in Antalya closed in November 2026 with a group of governments backing a statement to grow corporate demand for carbon credits, and the International Maritime Organization is set to resume, on 4 December 2026, its adjourned vote on a global carbon price for shipping, subject to confirmation the same week (IMO, 1 May 2026).

The debate is live because carbon pricing is spreading and retreating at the same time. The World Bank counts 87 carbon taxes and trading systems covering 29 percent of global emissions in April 2026, with national trading systems newly running in India, Japan and Viet Nam and the EU’s border carbon levy charging importers since 1 January 2026. Over the same period Canada scrapped its consumer carbon price (zeroed on 1 April 2025 and repealed in law on 12 March 2026), Pennsylvania abandoned its plan to join the northeastern US power-sector market (November 2025), the United States withdrew its official estimates of the damage done by a tonne of carbon (January 2025), and the voluntary market for carbon credits shrank to about a quarter of its 2021 value after peer-reviewed studies found that most credits examined did not represent real reductions. The argument runs between economists and international institutions who see a price as the cheapest way to cut emissions; governments that run carbon markets alongside subsidies and standards; heavy industry and the parties and governments that speak for energy costs; climate-justice and Indigenous groups that reject putting a price on pollution at all; and a carbon-credit industry of developers, standard-setters, corporate buyers and host countries, many of them in the Global South.

### 2. Average direct carbon price, worldwide
- Label: Average carbon price across carbon taxes and trading systems
- Value: nearly 21
- Unit: US dollars per tonne of CO2-equivalent (2026 dollars), across implemented instruments
- Comparison rows (different sources, noted): 2016 average — US$10 per tonne (World Bank 2026, 2026 dollars); price the High-Level Commission on Carbon Prices said in 2017 was needed by 2030 to be on track for well below 2 °C — US$50 to 100 per tonne, whose inflation-adjusted lower edge the World Bank put at US$63 in 2024 dollars; IPCC estimate of the marginal cost of abatement consistent with 1.5 °C — US$170 to 290 per tonne, or US$226 to 385 in 2024 dollars (World Bank 2024 restating the IPCC)
- Data year: prices as of 1 April 2026; benchmarks as stated by the World Bank in 2024
- Source label: World Bank, State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2026; World Bank, State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2024 for the benchmarks
- Source URL: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099051826185087983/pdf/P502283-3ee8b1dc-6f1a-46dd-8141-e6534fd34af9.pdf (benchmarks: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099081624122529330/pdf/P50228315fd8d1050186341ea02e1c107bc.pdf)
- Note: The average direct carbon price worldwide is nearly US$21 a tonne, about double its 2016 level and below every benchmark range economists and the IPCC have attached to the Paris temperature goals.
- Context: The World Bank reports that the average rose seven percent in the year to April 2026, driven by trading system prices, and has doubled since 2016. Prices vary widely around the average: Singapore raised its carbon tax 80 percent, from SGD 25 to SGD 45 a tonne, and South Africa raised its tax 31 percent for 2026, while New Zealand’s allowance price fell 26 percent over the year. The benchmarks come from different methods and are not interchangeable. The High-Level Commission on Carbon Prices, chaired by Joseph Stiglitz and Nicholas Stern, gave a price corridor for a well-below-2 °C path that assumed supporting policies alongside the price; the IPCC range is the modeled cost of cutting the last tonne on 1.5 °C pathways. Those who favor pricing read the gap as evidence that prices must rise; those who favor other instruments read it as evidence that prices cannot rise that far politically.
- Visualization: range (current average on a scale from zero to the IPCC 1.5 °C range, with the 2017 commission’s band marked)

### 5. Emission reductions attributed to carbon pricing, 2024 meta-analysis
- Label: Emission cuts from introducing a carbon price
- Value: 5 to 21
- Unit: percent reduction in emissions, statistically significant effects by scheme (4 to 15 percent after correcting for publication bias)
- Data year: published May 2024; evaluations of schemes in operation up to the early 2020s
- Source label: Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al., “Systematic review and meta-analysis of ex-post evaluations on the effectiveness of carbon pricing,” Nature Communications, 2024
- Source URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48512-w
- Note: A 2024 review of 80 causal studies found that introducing a carbon price cut emissions by 5 to 21 percent in the schemes where the effect was statistically significant, at least 17 of the 21 studied.
- Context: The study pooled 483 effect sizes from 80 ex-post evaluations of 21 carbon pricing schemes and found “immediate and substantial emission reductions for at least 17 of these policies, despite the low level of prices in most instances.” The reductions are measured against what each study estimates would otherwise have happened over its evaluation period, not as a yearly rate. The authors flag two gaps: dozens of schemes have never been rigorously evaluated, and the evidence says little about how much more a higher price would cut. An earlier review reached a more skeptical reading (stat 6); the two use different methods and units, and which reading is right is one of the open questions on this page.
- Visualization: range (5 to 21 percent, with the bias-corrected 4 to 15 marked)

### 6. Yearly emission reductions attributed to carbon pricing, 2021 review
- Label: Yearly emission cuts from carbon pricing, 2021 review
- Value: 0 to 2
- Unit: percent per year (aggregate reductions found by most studies); EU trading system 0 to 1.5 percent per year
- Data year: published March 2021; 37 studies of policies since 1990
- Source label: Jessica F. Green, “Does carbon pricing reduce emissions? A review of ex-post analyses,” Environmental Research Letters, 2021
- Source URL: https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/abdae9 (open access; the publisher’s site may block automated requests, DOI https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/abdae9)
- Note: A 2021 review of 37 ex-post studies found that most estimated carbon pricing had cut emissions by 0 to 2 percent a year.
- Context: Green found that only 37 studies assessed the actual effect of carbon pricing on emissions, most of them about Europe, and that carbon taxes generally performed better than trading systems. She set the result against the IPCC finding that emissions must fall 45 percent below 2010 levels by 2030 to hold warming to 1.5 °C and concluded that “the evidence indicates that carbon pricing has a limited impact on emissions.” The figure is a yearly rate and is not directly comparable with the 2024 meta-analysis’s cumulative 5 to 21 percent; a cut of one to two percent a year sustained for a decade lands in a similar range. The two papers differ less on the size of the effect than on whether that effect is large enough to make pricing the main climate instrument.
- Visualization: range (0 to 2 percent per year)

- Value: 140 to 380
- Unit: US dollars per tonne of CO2 emitted in 2030 (2020 dollars), 2023 EPA estimates at discount rates of 2.5 to 1.5 percent
- Comparison rows (different vintages and bases, noted): 2019 EPA estimates, domestic damages only — US$1 to 8 a tonne (2016 dollars, at 7 and 3 percent discount rates); 2021 interagency estimate — US$89 for 2030 emissions at 2.5 percent (2020 dollars); 2023 EPA estimates — US$140 to 380 for 2030 emissions (2020 dollars)

## Questions

### 1. Does a carbon price cut emissions enough to be the main climate policy?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: main instrument / supporting instrument
- Why contested: Every serious review finds that carbon prices reduce the emissions they cover, so the dispute is about size and attribution. A 2024 meta-analysis of 80 ex-post evaluations found cuts of 5 to 21 percent in the schemes with significant effects, at least 17 of 21 (Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al., Nature Communications, 2024), while a 2021 review of 37 studies found yearly cuts mostly of 0 to 2 percent and judged the impact “limited” (Green, Environmental Research Letters, 2021). The two findings can both be true; the disagreement is over whether that effect, at today’s prices, is a foundation for climate policy or a supplement to it.
- Main instrument: Those who favor pricing argue that the measured effects came from prices that were mostly low, so they understate what a price at benchmark levels would do. A price reaches every emitter and every decision at once, including ones no regulator could foresee, and it finds the cheapest cuts first. The EU system, which has run longest at a meaningful price, has seen covered emissions halve since 2005 (European Commission, 2026), and Britain’s rising minimum carbon price for power generators, introduced in 2013, made coal uneconomic from 2015 and helped take coal from 39 percent of British electricity in 2012 to none when the last plant closed on 30 September 2024 (Ember, https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/the-uks-journey-to-a-coal-power-phase-out/).
- Supporting instrument: Those who see pricing as secondary argue that Europe’s halving and California’s decline came mostly from renewable subsidies, coal phase-out laws, efficiency and vehicle standards and recessions, with the price playing a smaller part. A cut of one or two percent a year is far short of what the Paris goals require, and the price that would do the whole job, on the IPCC’s 1.5 °C range, is ten or more times the current world average of about US$21. The technologies now driving emissions down were made cheap by subsidies and deployment mandates; Our World in Data reports that solar photovoltaic costs fell 90 percent in a decade (https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/solar-panel-prices-have-fallen-by-around-20-every-time-global-capacity-doubled).

### 2. Can carbon prices rise high enough to matter and still survive politically?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: politically durable / politically doomed
- Why contested: Carbon pricing now covers 29 percent of global emissions and raises over US$100 billion a year (World Bank, 2026), yet Australia repealed its carbon tax in 2014, France dropped a planned fuel-tax rise after the 2018 gilets jaunes protests, Canada scrapped its consumer price in 2025, and in 2026 Italy called for the EU system’s suspension while EU lawmakers added free allowances for industry. Washington voters, by contrast, kept their program in 2024 by about 62 percent.
- Politically durable: Those who expect pricing to last point to two decades of spread, to the EU and California systems that have survived recessions and energy shocks, and to Washington’s vote. They argue that the failures came from visible consumer taxes introduced without visible benefits, and that design answers them: returning or visibly spending the revenue, phasing prices in, pricing industry upstream, and pairing the price with a border levy so domestic firms are not undercut. California renewed its program through 2045 in 2025, and India, Japan and Viet Nam launched national systems.
- Politically doomed: Those who expect pricing to stall argue that whenever a carbon price is high enough to be felt at the pump or on a heating bill it becomes the opposition’s best campaign issue, as it did in Australia, France and Canada. The 2026 fight happened in the system’s strongest home, and the result so far is a proposed slower cap, free allowances extended to 2040 and a second trading system for heating and road fuel delayed and under review. A policy that works only at prices voters will not accept is, in this view, not a policy that works.

### 3. Should carbon prices be eased when energy prices spike?
- Tag: value
- Poles: hold the signal / relieve the shock
- Why contested: A March 2026 cut in world oil supply that the World Bank calls the largest oil shock on record pushed up energy prices across Europe and beyond. In February 2026 Italy asked to suspend the EU trading system until it was reformed (Euronews), and in October Poland, Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic pressed to delay the second system, for heating and road fuel, from 2028 to 2030, while the Nordic member states resisted reopening the timetable and leaders agreed only to a review (EU Today, 17 October 2026).
- Hold the signal: Those who would hold the price argue that a carbon price matters most for investment decisions that last decades, and that suspending it whenever fuel is dear tells every investor the signal is negotiable. A shock caused by dependence on imported oil and gas is, on this view, the best argument for cutting that dependence faster; relief for households is better delivered through targeted transfers funded by carbon revenue than by switching the price off.
- Relieve the shock: Those who would ease the price argue that adding a carbon cost on top of a record energy shock hits households and energy-intensive firms at the moment they can least absorb it, and pushes production to competitors that pay no such price. Temporary suspension or price caps, on this view, protect public support for climate policy over the long run, because a policy that deepens a cost-of-living crisis is the one most likely to be repealed for good.

### 4. Should carbon pricing revenue go back to households or into clean investment?
- Tag: value
- Poles: return as dividends / invest in transition
- Why contested: Many economists favor equal per-person rebates because they make a carbon price progressive and keep government from growing, and Canada ran the largest example until 2025. A 20-country survey published in 2025 found that a carbon tax with equal cash transfers drew 37 percent support in high-income countries, against around 60 percent when the revenue funded green infrastructure, low-carbon subsidies or income tax cuts (Dechezleprêtre et al., American Economic Review, 2025). Washington and California now call their systems cap-and-invest, and EU law requires its trading revenue to go to climate and energy purposes.
- Return as dividends: Those who favor dividends argue that returning the money keeps the price signal intact while leaving most lower-income households better off on direct costs, as Canada’s Parliamentary Budget Officer found. It answers the charge that carbon pricing is a tax grab, and it lets households, not governments, decide how to adjust.
- Invest in transition: Those who favor investment argue that dividends proved invisible to voters and so failed politically, while spending on transit, clean power and home retrofits produces benefits people can see. Investment also lowers the cost of cutting emissions, which reduces the price needed, and can be steered to the communities most exposed to pollution; Washington directs at least 35 percent of its revenue to overburdened communities (World Bank, 2026).

### 5. Do carbon credits from avoided deforestation, cookstoves and similar projects represent real reductions?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: mostly real / mostly not
- Why contested: A 2024 synthesis of 14 rigorous studies estimated that less than 16 percent of the credits it examined represented real reductions (Probst et al., Nature Communications, 2024). The credit industry has since adopted integrity standards, the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market labels approved methodologies, and highly rated credits sell at a premium (World Bank, 2026). The open question is whether reformed credits are reliable, not whether many past ones were.
- Mostly real: Those who defend credits argue that the studies examined older methodologies and project types, some since rejected or tightened, and that new rules require stricter baselines, satellite monitoring of forests and buffer pools against reversal. Some project types score well even in the critical studies, such as destruction of the industrial gas HFC-23 at 68 percent. Credits are, on this view, a fixable measurement problem attached to real money for forests and communities.
- Mostly not: Those who doubt credits argue that the failure is structural: a credit depends on a counterfactual, what would have happened without the project, that cannot be observed, and every party to the sale, from developer to verifier to buyer, gains from an inflated estimate. The reformed rules have not yet been tested by independent ex-post studies of the kind that found the earlier failures, and until they are a credit lets a buyer claim a reduction that may not exist.

### 6. Should buyers be allowed to count purchased credits against their own emissions?
- Tag: value
- Poles: count credits / cut at source
- Why contested: The question underlies corporate net-zero claims, the aviation scheme CORSIA, trading between countries under the Paris Agreement’s Article 6, and the European Commission’s July 2026 proposal to set aside allowances to buy up to 260 million tonnes of international credits for 2036 to 2040, about 2 percent of the EU’s 1990 net emissions (ICAP). Even if every credit were real, a tonne bought elsewhere lets the buyer emit a tonne at home.
- Count credits: Those who favor counting credits argue that a tonne avoided or removed anywhere has the same effect on the climate, so letting buyers pay for the cheapest tonnes lowers the cost of meeting targets and moves money to developing countries. Kenya’s president William Ruto called Africa’s carbon sinks an “unparalleled economic goldmine” in 2023. Removals will be needed to balance emissions that cannot be cut, and only a market will pay for them at scale.
- Cut at source: Those who favor cutting at source argue that offsetting delays the changes rich emitters must make, keeps pollution in the communities next to their plants, and shifts the work of mitigation onto land and people in the Global South, sometimes at the cost of Indigenous land rights. If credits are used at all, on this view, they should fund action beyond a buyer’s own cuts rather than cancel out its emissions.

### 7. Should heavy industry be shielded from the carbon price with free allowances and border levies?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: shield and adjust / price fully
- Why contested: Free allowances and border levies both answer leakage, the risk that a carbon price moves production and its emissions to places without one. The EU began charging its border levy on cement, steel, aluminum, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen in 2026, its lawmakers agreed in November 2026 to add about 121 million free allowances for industry for 2026 to 2030, and the Commission proposes keeping free allocation through 2040. How large leakage actually is, and whether border levies are protection in disguise, are both disputed.
- Shield and adjust: Those who favor protection argue that without free allocation or a border charge a carbon price simply exports factories, jobs and emissions to China, India or the United States, as Italy’s industry minister argued in February 2026 in calling the EU system “only a tax, a tariff on the energy-intensive companies” (Euronews). A border levy also presses trading partners to price carbon, and the World Bank reports that Albania, Malaysia, Serbia and others have cited the EU’s in moving toward their own prices.
- Price fully: Those who favor a full price argue that ex-post studies have found little evidence of large-scale leakage so far, and that free allocation blunts the incentive to decarbonize exactly where emissions are hardest to cut. Developing-country governments add that a border levy charges their exporters for a cost they did not choose, set by countries that caused most past warming; India, China and South Africa have objected to the EU’s as unilateral trade protection.

### 8. What price should a tonne of carbon carry?
- Tag: value
- Poles: high social cost / low or none
- Why contested: In theory the price should equal the social cost of carbon, the present value of the damage from one more tonne. US government estimates have ranged from about US$1 to US$380 a tonne depending on the discount rate and whether damage abroad counts, and the United States withdrew its estimates in January 2025 (Congressional Research Service, 2025).
- High social cost: Those who favor a high value argue that the welfare of future generations should count nearly as much as ours, so discount rates should be low, and that a national price should reflect global damage because emissions mix globally and every country gains when others act. Modeled 1.5 °C pathways put the needed marginal cost in the hundreds of dollars a tonne.
- Low or none: Those who favor a low value argue that governments should weigh the costs to their own citizens today, that market interest rates are the honest discount rate, and that damage estimates centuries out are too uncertain to set prices by. A domestic price high enough to matter imposes certain costs now for uncertain benefits that depend on other countries acting too.

### 9. Should rich and poor countries face the same carbon price?
- Tag: value
- Poles: one global price / differentiated prices
- Why contested: Average prices run from about US$68 a tonne in Europe and Central Asia to US$4 in Latin America and about US$2 in the Middle East and North Africa (World Bank, 2026). IMF staff proposed tiered floors of US$75, US$50 and US$25 by income; the shipping levy before the International Maritime Organization would apply the same price to every large ship whatever its flag.
- One global price: Those who favor a single price argue that a tonne does the same damage wherever it is emitted, so the cheapest cuts should be made first wherever they are, and differentiated prices invite production to move toward low-price countries. Fairness, on this view, is better handled by transferring revenue than by varying the price.
- Differentiated prices: Those who favor different prices argue that countries that emitted least historically and have the least income should not pay the same per tonne as those that caused most of the problem, and that the Paris Agreement’s principle of common but differentiated responsibilities commits rich countries to move first and furthest.

## Positions

Order note: the hybrid camp is placed first because it describes what most governments that price carbon actually do (the EU, California, Washington, Canada and others run a carbon price alongside standards and subsidies) and what most large environmental groups support; the pure-pricing camp is the debate’s classic pole and sits second. The camp that rejects pricing pollution at all is fourth, ahead of the credit-market camp, because it rejects the question as posed across all three instruments, while the credit camp concerns one of them; an editor could reasonably swap the last two.

### 1. Pair a carbon price with standards and public investment
- Adherents: Third Way (institution), whose February 2020 memo “Carbon Pricing: One Piece of the Climate Puzzle” says “Carbon pricing remains a crucial part of the climate puzzle” but that “Nearly all carbon prices currently in place are too low to drive the ambitious decarbonization proponents have sought” — https://www.thirdway.org/memo/carbon-pricing-one-piece-of-the-climate-puzzle ; Danny Cullenward and David Victor, authors of “Making Climate Policy Work” (Polity, 2020), whose book page says that “markets’ problems are structural” and that facing that reality “requires relying more heavily on smart regulation and industrial policy” — https://www.ghgpolicy.org/book
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Carbon pricing works in theory and helps in practice, but two decades of experience show what it can and cannot do on its own. Prices have stayed far below the levels models call for because the industries that would pay most have the political power to keep them low and voters punish prices they can feel. Most of the cuts so far came from policies aimed at technologies directly: renewable mandates and subsidies that drove the cost of solar and wind down, efficiency and vehicle standards, coal phase-out rules, and public investment in grids and research. Those tools work where a price of a few dollars or even a few tens of dollars a tonne changes little, in steel, cement, buildings and anywhere the clean technology is not yet built at scale. Keep a carbon price as a backstop, a revenue source and a way to cover industry, return or invest its revenue where people can see it, but do not stake the climate on the price alone.
- Variation inside the camp: how much weight the price should carry, from an equal partner to a backstop; whether to lean on public investment and tax credits (the US Inflation Reduction Act model) or on regulation and standards; whether offsets should be kept out of compliance markets entirely.
- Best evidence: In 2024 only seven instruments, covering less than one percent of global emissions, were priced at or above the low end of the level the High-Level Commission on Carbon Prices said was needed (World Bank, 2024). A 2021 review of 37 ex-post studies found yearly reductions mostly of 0 to 2 percent (Green, 2021). Solar photovoltaic costs fell 90 percent in a decade (Our World in Data), a decline this camp credits mainly to subsidies and deployment rather than carbon prices. Cullenward and Victor argue that California’s cuts came mostly from complementary policies while its allowance prices stayed low.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that standards and subsidies cost more per tonne than a price): The cost advantage of pricing is large in a model and small in practice, because the price never reaches the level the model assumes. Subsidies and standards created markets for technologies that then became cheaper than fossil fuels, which no politically plausible carbon price would have done on its own, and that learning effect is the largest cost saving in climate policy. A theoretically efficient policy that cannot be passed or sustained at an effective level does not cut emissions.

### 2. Make a rising carbon price the core of climate policy
- Adherents: Climate Leadership Council (institution), whose “Economists’ Statement on Carbon Dividends,” signed by US economists including Nobel laureates and former Federal Reserve chairs, says “A carbon tax offers the most cost-effective lever to reduce carbon emissions at the scale and speed that is necessary” — https://clcouncil.org/economists-statement/ ; International Monetary Fund (institution), whose staff argued in a May 2022 blog by Jean Chateau, Florence Jaumotte and Gregor Schwerhoff for an international carbon price floor phased in by 2030, at “$75 per ton of carbon for high-income countries,” “$50 per ton of carbon for middle-income countries” and “$25 per ton of carbon for low-income countries” — https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2022/05/19/blog-why-countries-must-cooperate-on-carbon-prices ; Citizens’ Climate Lobby, a volunteer organization that campaigns for a US carbon fee returned to households as a dividend — https://citizensclimatelobby.org/
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Climate change is a pollution problem, and the cheapest way to cut pollution is to make polluters pay for it. A price on carbon reaches every emitter, every product and every choice, from how a steel plant runs to whether a family buys a heat pump, without a regulator having to know in advance where the cheapest cuts are. Those who can cut cheaply do so first; those who cannot, pay. That is why a price achieves a given cut at lower cost than any mix of mandates, and why economists across the political spectrum support one. The price should start moderate, rise predictably and cover as much of the economy as possible. Its revenue can be returned to households so most lower-income families come out ahead, or used to cut other taxes, and a border adjustment can protect industry from countries that do not price carbon. Standards and subsidies have a place where markets fail, as in early-stage research, but they should support the price, not replace it.
- Variation inside the camp: tax versus cap-and-trade (a tax fixes the price, a cap fixes the quantity); dividends versus tax cuts versus investment for the revenue; one global price versus tiered floors such as the IMF staff proposal.
- Best evidence: The 2024 meta-analysis of 80 ex-post evaluations found that introducing a carbon price cut emissions by 5 to 21 percent in at least 17 of 21 schemes, “despite the low level of prices in most instances” (Döbbeling-Hildebrandt et al., 2024). Emissions covered by the EU system have halved since 2005 (European Commission, 2026). Britain’s rising minimum carbon price for power generators, from 2013, made coal uneconomic from 2015 and preceded the end of coal power in 2024 (Ember). Carbon pricing raised over US$107 billion in 2025 (World Bank, 2026).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that prices never rise high enough to matter and voters reject them): Prices were low because systems were designed with loose caps and generous free allocation, not because pricing does not work; the EU tightened its cap and built a reserve to absorb surplus allowances, and its price rose accordingly. The political failures came from consumer taxes whose rebates voters could not see, and Washington’s 62 percent vote shows a program can survive a direct challenge when its benefits are visible. Subsidies and standards carry their own political risk and fiscal cost: many of the wind and solar tax credits the United States enacted in 2022 were cut back by a law signed on 4 July 2025 (Congressional Research Service, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12624/IN12624.2.pdf).

### 3. Repeal or suspend carbon prices to cut energy costs
- Adherents: Canadian Taxpayers Federation (institution), whose federal director Franco Terrazzano said in March 2025, applauding the Conservative pledge to end the industrial carbon price, “Carbon taxes on refineries make gas more expensive, carbon taxes on utilities make home heating more expensive and carbon taxes on fertilizer plants increase costs for farmers and that makes groceries more expensive” — https://www.taxpayer.com/newsroom/taxpayers-federation-applauds-poilievre%E2%80%99s-commitment-to-end-industrial-carbon-tax?id=18778 ; Pierre Poilievre, leader of Canada’s Conservative Party, who said in March 2025 that a government led by him would repeal both the consumer carbon tax and the federal standards for pricing emissions from large industrial emitters — https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/poilievre-industrial-carbon-price-1.7485500 ; Adolfo Urso, Italy’s minister for business, who on 26 February 2026 said “the mechanism of the ETS, as it is currently drafted, is only a tax, a tariff on the energy-intensive companies” and called for it to be suspended pending reform — https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/02/26/italy-calls-for-suspension-of-eu-carbon-market
- Case in the camp’s own voice: A carbon price is a tax on energy, and energy is in everything people buy. It raises what families pay for fuel, heating and food, and what factories pay to make steel, fertilizer and cement, while doing little for the climate because the countries where emissions are growing fastest do not impose one. The result is higher living costs at home, and production, jobs and emissions moving abroad. Voters understood this: they turned against carbon taxes in Australia and Canada and forced France to back down. Rebates do not fix it, because they hand back part of what was taken while the economy pays the rest. In an energy price shock like 2026’s, adding a carbon cost on top is the worst possible policy. Emissions should fall through innovation, nuclear power and cleaner technology that wins on cost, not through policies designed to make energy dearer.
- Variation inside the camp: full repeal (Canada’s Conservatives; the US Republican position against any federal carbon tax) versus suspension, slowing or capping pending reform (Italy; the Central European governments pressing in 2026 to delay the EU’s second trading system to 2030); some in the camp would replace a domestic carbon price with a fee on carbon-intensive imports, as in the Foreign Pollution Fee Act reintroduced by US Senators Bill Cassidy and Lindsey Graham in April 2025.
- Best evidence: Canada’s Parliamentary Budget Officer estimated in 2024 that most households would see a net loss from the federal fuel charge once its effect on wages and investment income was counted, even though rebates exceeded direct fuel costs. A carbon tax drew some of the lowest support of any climate policy in a 20-country survey published in 2025 (Dechezleprêtre et al.). The EU’s own institutions agreed in November 2026 to add about 121 million free allowances to shield industry, and the Commission’s July 2026 proposal slows the cap and extends free allocation.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that removing the price removes the cheapest way to cut emissions and leaves climate damage unpaid): The cuts achievable from a price voters will tolerate are small, and the large declines in power-sector emissions came from technology and plant closures that do not need a consumer price. The damage figures that justify high prices rest on contested discount rates and long-range models, while the cost of energy today is certain and falls hardest on rural and lower-income households. A country acting alone bears the cost while the climate benefit depends on everyone else.

### 4. Cut emissions at the source without pricing or trading pollution
- Adherents: Indigenous Environmental Network (institution), whose carbon pricing page is headed “Carbon Pricing is a False Solution to Climate Chaos” and says “Cap and trade systems are a way for polluters to continue to pollute and make more profit” — https://www.ienearth.org/carbon-pricing/ ; Climate Justice Alliance (institution), which with the Indigenous Environmental Network released the report “Carbon Pricing: A Critical Perspective for Community Resistance” at COP23 in November 2017, its release describing cap-and-trade, carbon offsets and carbon tax systems as “false solutions that do not cut emissions at source” — https://climatejusticealliance.org/groundbreaking-carbon-pricing-report-released-indigenous-environmental-network-climate-justice-alliance-cop-23/ ; Tom Goldtooth, executive director of the Indigenous Environmental Network and co-author of that report
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Putting a price on carbon turns the atmosphere into property and pollution into a right that can be bought. It lets the biggest polluters keep polluting as long as they can pay, and it concentrates harm in the low-income, Black, Brown and Indigenous communities next to refineries, power plants and industrial zones, which breathe the other pollutants that come with the carbon. Trading and offsets are worse: they let a corporation in the North claim credit for a forest in the South, often on land taken from or managed without the consent of the Indigenous peoples who live there, while the reductions on paper do not happen. The answer is not a better market but a managed decline of fossil fuel extraction and burning, binding limits on each facility, keeping fossil fuels in the ground, and public investment in a just transition led by the communities most affected.
- Variation inside the camp: some groups accept a carbon tax whose revenue funds frontline communities while rejecting trading and offsets; others reject any pricing of carbon as commodification.
- Best evidence: The 2024 synthesis that found less than 16 percent of studied credits represented real reductions (Probst et al.); the 2023 Science study that found most avoided-deforestation projects examined had not significantly reduced deforestation (West et al.); a 2018 study of California’s cap-and-trade program from 2011 to 2015, which found that 52 percent of regulated facilities reported higher in-state greenhouse gas emissions after trading began and that neighborhoods with increases nearby had higher proportions of people of color and poor residents (Cushing et al., PLOS Medicine, 2018, https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1002604; the authors describe the results as preliminary, covering the program’s first years); and the low prices and limited yearly cuts recorded in most systems (stats 2 and 6).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that direct limits without a price cost more, and that the EU system has halved its covered emissions): A cost estimate that leaves out the health of fence-line communities and the rights of forest peoples is not a full cost. Coal plants have been closed by rules and timetables, which deliver a known result on a known date, and where a price helped it sped up what regulation was already doing. Pricing has been the favored policy of governments and economists for three decades, and global emissions are still higher than when it started.

### 5. Scale trade in high-integrity carbon credits
- Adherents: Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market (institution), whose chair Annette Nazareth said at the launch of its Core Carbon Principles on 29 March 2023 that “a high-integrity voluntary carbon market is one of those tools” needed to secure a livable future — https://icvcm.org/integrity-council-launches-global-benchmark-for-high-integrity-carbon-credits/ ; the Coalition to Grow Carbon Markets (institution), a government-led group launched on 24 June 2025 by Kenya, Singapore and the United Kingdom, committed to “credible business use of high-integrity carbon credits alongside deep corporate decarbonisation,” which issued its Antalya Statement at COP31 in November 2026 — https://www.nccs.gov.sg/the-coalition-to-grow-carbon-markets/ ; William Ruto, president of Kenya, who at the Africa Climate Summit in September 2023 called Africa’s carbon sinks an “unparalleled economic goldmine” that “should translate into billions of dollars” — https://www.france24.com/en/africa/20230905-kenya-bets-on-carbon-credits-as-it-hosts-african-climate-summit
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Carbon credits move money from those who emit to those who can cut or remove emissions at lower cost, and most of those places are in the developing world, where forests, clean cookstoves, methane capture and clean power are short of finance. The early market had serious quality problems, and the answer has been to fix them, not abandon the tool: independent standards, the Integrity Council’s labels, ratings agencies, CORSIA eligibility rules and the UN’s new Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism. Companies that buy credits are cutting their own emissions faster, not slower, and credits paid for while they cut are better than nothing paid at all. Removal credits, from forests to direct air capture, will be the main way to pay for taking carbon out of the air.
- Variation inside the camp: credits as a contribution beyond a buyer’s own cuts versus as an offset counted against them; nature-based versus engineered removals; voluntary use versus inclusion in compliance markets such as Article 6 trading between countries, CORSIA, or the international credits the European Commission proposes to buy for 2036 to 2040.
- Best evidence: MSCI found in June 2023 that companies using material quantities of carbon credits were “decarbonizing at twice the rate of companies that do not use carbon credits” (https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/paper/corporate-emission-performance-and-the-use-of-carbon-credits). Highly rated credits sell at a premium, and CORSIA-eligible credits have traded at US$15 to 22 a tonne since September 2025 against US$1 to 14 for most other types (World Bank, 2026). Capital committed to future credit projects reached an estimated US$12 to 16 billion in 2025 (World Bank, 2026). Even the critical 2024 synthesis found that HFC-23 destruction projects delivered 68 percent of their claimed reductions (Probst et al.).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that most credits have not represented real reductions): The studies looked at a market built before integrity standards existed and at project types the new standards have tightened or rejected. Every regulated market has needed rules to establish trust, and the answer to measurement error is better measurement, from satellite monitoring of forests to dynamic baselines, buffer pools and independent ratings, not closing the main channel of private climate finance to the countries that most need it.

## Crux

Can a price on carbon, at a level governments can actually sustain, do enough of the work of cutting emissions to justify what it costs the people who pay it, or is that work better done some other way?

What evidence could settle: How much emissions fall per dollar of carbon price, and whether the effect grows as prices rise; how much of the declines in the EU, California and Britain the price caused, as opposed to subsidies, standards, coal closures and recessions; how large carbon leakage really is, and whether border levies stop it without becoming trade barriers; whether reformed carbon credits deliver the reductions they claim when tested by independent ex-post studies; and whether designs that return or visibly reinvest revenue, as Washington’s does, reliably survive the rise in energy prices a tighter cap brings, including in a shock like 2026’s. Each of these is measurable, and the record is now long enough, two decades in Europe, over a decade in California and 87 instruments worldwide, that the answers are sharpening.

What no data can close: How much today’s costs to households and industry should weigh against future climate damage, and how far into the future that damage should count; whether a country should price the harm its emissions do to people abroad; whether rich and poor countries, and rich and poor households, should pay the same per tonne; whether climate policy should hold steady through an energy price shock or bend to it; and whether it is acceptable in principle for an emitter to pay for the right to pollute, or to pay someone else to reduce on its behalf. Those who would pair a price with standards, those who would make the price the core, those who would repeal it, those who reject pricing pollution and those who would scale credit markets answer these questions differently even when they agree on the numbers.
