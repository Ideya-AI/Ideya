# Universal Basic Income — research

## Overview

Universal basic income is a recurring cash payment made to every member of a political community, individually, without a means test and without a work requirement. The topic here is whether that design is better than the two families of policy it competes with: targeted transfers, which send money or in-kind benefits only to people who qualify by income, family status, disability or job-search behavior, and a job guarantee, which offers publicly funded employment at a set wage to anyone who wants it. The scope covers the design question, the evidence from experiments and from real programs that resemble a basic income, the arithmetic of paying for one, and the arguments about what unconditional cash does to work, dignity and the shape of the welfare state. It deliberately excludes the broader question of how large a welfare state should be, the minimum wage, in-kind benefits such as health insurance or housing vouchers considered on their own merits, and cash transfers as an instrument of foreign aid, which appear here only as evidence about what unconditional money does to people who receive it.

Why now: in November 2025 President Donald Trump promised a “tariff dividend” of at least $2,000 a person to everyone except high earners, funded by import duties, which would have been the largest near-universal cash payment in US history and came from the political side that has generally opposed unconditional cash. On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court held 6 to 3 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs, voiding the duties that were to pay for it (Supreme Court of the United States, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1287_4gcj.pdf). No bill authorizing the checks has been introduced and the funding source no longer exists, but the episode moved the argument: a universal payment had become a mainstream proposition on both left and right within a single year.

The same year pushed local policy in opposite directions. On 20 November 2025 the Cook County Board of Commissioners in Illinois voted unanimously to put $7.5 million of recurring money into its fiscal 2026 budget, making Cook County the first government body in the United States to fund guaranteed income as a permanent line item rather than a time-limited pilot (Economic Security Project, https://economicsecurityproject.org/news/cook-county-becomes-first-government-body-in-nation-to-make-guaranteed-income-permanent/). Six weeks later Texas Senate Bill 2010 forced every local guaranteed income program in that state to stop by 1 January 2026, ending a fight that had already reached the Texas Supreme Court over Harris County’s $20.5 million Uplift Harris program.

The debate is live for three further reasons. The pilot wave that began with Stockton in 2019 has produced results rather than promises, including the largest randomized trial ever run on cash in the United States, whose revised findings in August 2026 showed measurable reductions in work, and a twelve-year trial in Kenya whose recipients did not work less at all. Voters have started deciding it directly, and mostly saying no: Hamburg rejected a publicly funded basic income pilot 62.7 percent to 37.3 percent on 12 October 2025, and six US states have banned their own local governments from trying. And artificial intelligence has put the old automation argument back on the table, with the heads of major AI labs among the people arguing that some form of universal payment will eventually be needed. The participants are city and county governments, state legislatures, development economists, labor economists, welfare-policy think tanks on both left and right, anti-poverty organizations, unions who generally prefer jobs to cash, and technology executives who fund the experiments.

Source label: Vivalt, Rhodes, Bartik, Broockman, Krause & Miller, NBER Working Paper 32719
Source URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719
Note: In a three-year randomized trial, 1,000 low-income people who received $1,000 a month earned about $1,900 a year less than the control group and were 4.2 percentage points less likely to be in the labor market.
Context: This is the OpenResearch Unconditional Cash Study, funded largely by Sam Altman and run in Texas and Illinois with a control group of 2,000 people receiving $50 a month. It is the largest randomized trial of unconditional cash ever conducted in the United States and it cuts both ways. Recipients did reduce work: hours fell by one to two a week, their partners cut hours by a comparable amount, and the biggest increase in time use was leisure. They did not move into better jobs; the authors say their confidence intervals rule out even small improvements in job quality. Recipients also spent more, moved house more often, reported better financial resilience, and a companion paper published 20 July 2025 found clearer improvements in parenting. The numbers above come from the August 2026 revision; the original July 2024 working paper reported a smaller effect, about $1,500 a year and a 2.0 percentage point participation drop, so anyone citing the study should say which version. The published version appears in the Quarterly Journal of Economics.

Label: Effect of a twelve-year basic income in Kenya
Value: 50
Unit: percent increase in household income for the lump-sum group, relative to control
Viz: bar
Data year: 2023
Source label: GiveDirectly, with Banerjee, Faye, Krueger, Niehaus & Suri
Source URL: https://www.givedirectly.org/2023-ubi-results
Note: After two years, households given a single $500 lump sum had incomes roughly 50 percent above the control group, and no group in the study reduced its total labor supply.
Context: This is the world’s largest and longest basic income experiment: about 23,000 people in 195 Kenyan villages, with 100 more villages as controls, split into a twelve-year monthly UBI of $22.50, a two-year monthly UBI of the same size, and a one-time $500 lump sum, against a local extreme-poverty line of about $33 a month. The comparison is informative because at the two-year mark all three treatment arms had received roughly the same total money. The lump sum and the long-term guarantee produced large gains in enterprise formation and savings; the short-term monthly payment did much less, which the authors read as evidence that people need either enough money at once or a long enough horizon to invest rather than consume. Recipients did not work less and did not drink more. The result is in tension with the US trial above, and the most common explanation is that in a setting of extreme poverty and thin credit markets, cash relieves a capital constraint rather than buying leisure.

Context: Between 2017 and 2025 at least 122 US pilots across 33 states and the District of Columbia distributed $481 million to more than 40,000 recipients. The gap between the two averages is a sample-size story: most pilots enrolled a few hundred people at most, are underpowered to detect an employment effect of a few percentage points, and suffered heavy attrition, so their individual estimates scatter around zero. The four largest pilots, which hold 55 percent of all treatment participants, imply an income elasticity of labor supply of about -0.18, close to the academic literature. The authors also caution that every high-quality pilot ran during or just after the pandemic, and that time-limited payments to a selected group may not predict what a permanent, universal payment would do.

Note: Finnish basic income recipients worked 78 days over the study year against 72 for the control group, a small difference; the clearer effects were on wellbeing.
Context: Finland paid €560 a month, tax-free and with no reporting requirements, to 2,000 randomly selected unemployed people aged 25 to 58 through 2017 and 2018. Employment effects were small and hard to isolate in the second year because Finland introduced an unrelated activation model for jobseekers at the same time. The survey findings were more consistent: recipients reported greater life satisfaction, less depression and loneliness, better perceived cognitive functioning, more trust in institutions, and markedly less bureaucratic hassle. The survey response rate was low, 31 percent among recipients and 20 percent among controls, which limits how firmly those findings can be held.

Context: SEED gave $500 a month for 24 months to 125 randomly selected residents of neighborhoods at or below Stockton’s median income, starting February 2019, and it is the pilot that launched the American wave. Note: Every eligible Alaskan receives $1,200 in 2026, a $1,000 dividend plus a one-time $200 energy relief payment, with distribution starting 1 October 2026.
Context: Alaska has paid an unconditional annual dividend to nearly every resident since 1982, funded by investment returns on oil royalties, and it is the closest thing in the world to a long-running universal basic income in a rich country. Note: In the OECD’s simulation, a budget-neutral basic income moves more people into poverty than out of it in Finland, France and the United Kingdom, because the money is spread away from the people existing benefits target.
Context: This is the strongest technical objection to a basic income in countries that already have dense welfare states, and the OECD states it plainly: a basic income is not an efficient tool for reducing poverty where existing benefits are well targeted. People who currently receive unemployment insurance, early retirement pensions or several stacked means-tested benefits lose more than the flat payment gives back. The countervailing finding in the same simulation is that people entitled to benefits who do not claim them gain, and that the UK’s aggregate poverty gap narrows even as the headcount rises, because a basic income reaches the very poorest, who are often the people missing from the claiming system. The simulation assumes disability and housing support is retained and that pensioners are unaffected, which makes it a relatively generous test of the idea.

They remain the largest body of evidence on guaranteed income in a rich country, and the modern trials broadly reproduce them. The reductions were real but smaller than opponents predicted, and much of the reduction among wives and youth was time spent on childcare and schooling. The experiments also produced a widely reported finding of increased marital dissolution in Seattle and Denver, which helped kill the policy politically in the late 1970s and which later reanalysis, including by the original investigators, substantially walked back.

## Questions

Question: Does unconditional cash reduce how much people work, and by enough to matter?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Small and acceptable / Large and self-defeating
Why contested: The trials disagree, and the disagreement tracks the setting rather than the design. In extreme poverty, cash relieves a constraint and people work at least as much; in a rich country with an existing safety net, cash buys some leisure. Nobody has run a permanent, universal payment anywhere, so every estimate extrapolates from a temporary payment to a selected group.
Case for small and acceptable: The historical negative income tax experiments found married men cutting work by about two weeks a year, and much of the larger reduction among wives and young people went into childcare and schooling rather than idleness. Iran’s universal transfer, the largest real-world case at roughly 28 percent of median per capita expenditure, showed no aggregate reduction in hours or participation and positive effects for women and the self-employed. Alaska’s forty-year dividend has not cut aggregate employment. The Kenyan twelve-year trial found no fall in labor supply and a rise in enterprise. Even the US trial that found the clearest reduction found it at one to two hours a week, roughly a tenth of a work week, which is what economists would expect from an income effect of that size and not a collapse in work effort. Some of the reduction is desirable: a parent cutting hours to care for a child or a worker leaving a job that was making them ill are outcomes a policy could reasonably want.
Case for large and self-defeating: The revised results of the largest US randomized trial show a 4.2 percentage point fall in labor market participation and about $1,900 a year less earned income per recipient, with partners cutting hours too and the freed time going mostly to leisure rather than education or job search. Job quality did not improve, and the authors say they can rule out even small improvements, which undercuts the hope that cash lets people hold out for better work. Averaged across only the four largest US pilots, employment fell 3.2 percentage points, implying an income elasticity of labor supply near -0.18 that matches the wider literature. A permanent, universal payment would be much larger than any trial payment and financed by taxes that add their own work disincentive on top, so the labor-supply effect of a real program should be expected to exceed anything measured so far, and that reduction both shrinks the tax base paying for the program and raises its cost.

Question: Per dollar spent, does a universal payment reduce poverty more than a targeted one?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Universal reaches further / Targeting goes further
Why contested: The answer depends almost entirely on how good the existing targeting is, and that varies enormously between a Scandinavian welfare state and a low-income country with no benefit registry. Both sides are describing different countries and often do not notice.
Case for universal reaches further: Means tests leak in both directions. Twelve percent of SNAP-eligible individuals and 22 percent of EITC-eligible taxpayers receive nothing, and the people who miss out are disproportionately those least able to navigate a claim. Verification is expensive: Supplemental Security Income spends about 7 to 8 percent of its budget on administration against 0.5 percent for Social Security. Means tests also impose effective marginal tax rates that can exceed 80 percent as benefits taper, punishing exactly the earnings they claim to encourage. In low-income countries, where only 9.7 percent of the population has any social protection at all, there is no targeting infrastructure to be more efficient than; the practical alternative to universal cash is nothing. The OECD’s own simulation found that a basic income narrows the UK poverty gap even where it raises the headcount, because it reaches the very poorest who are missing from the claiming system.
Case for targeting goes further: The arithmetic is unforgiving. The OECD modeled a budget-neutral basic income in four European countries and found it would pay about €527 a month in Finland, €456 in France, €158 in Italy and £230 in the UK, none of them reaching the national poverty line, and would push more working-age people into poverty than out of it in three of the four. The money that goes to the comfortable has to come from somewhere, and in a fixed budget it comes from the people who currently receive several stacked benefits: the disabled, the long-term unemployed, lone parents. The IMF found a basic income at 25 percent of median income would cost 6.5 percent of GDP in advanced economies while reducing poverty by 7.5 percentage points, a ratio existing targeted programs beat in most rich countries. Take-up problems are real but are cheaper to fix by simplifying applications, which is how SNAP reached its highest participation rate in half a century, than by paying everyone.

Question: Is receiving public support rightly conditional on working or looking for work?
Tag: value
Poles: Reciprocity required / Income unconditional
Why contested: This is the oldest fault line in welfare policy and it is not an empirical question. Two people can agree on every number about labor supply and still disagree, because they disagree about what citizens owe each other.
Case for reciprocity required: A society is a scheme of cooperation, and the claim to a share of its product is grounded in contributing to it. Conditionality is not cruelty but the expression of a norm most people hold: that able adults should contribute what they can, and that support is a bridge back to contribution rather than a permanent alternative to it. Work is also the main route by which adults acquire skills, structure, social contact and standing, so a policy that makes withdrawal from work easier does people harm the cash does not repair. Public support for redistribution in practice rests on this norm; surveys consistently show that support collapses when a benefit is described as going to people who could work and choose not to, which is why every direct popular vote on unconditional income has lost heavily. A program that violates a norm this widely held will not survive the first recession that makes it expensive.
Case for income unconditional: Conditionality assumes the state can tell who deserves support, and it cannot. Someone caring for a disabled parent, raising a child, studying, recovering from illness or between jobs in a town with no jobs is doing something valuable that no work test recognizes; the test catches them and pushes them into whatever work is nearest, which is often worse than what they would find with a little security. Unconditional income also changes the balance of power inside the labor market: a worker who can afford to say no is negotiating, and a worker who cannot is not. Conditionality carries its own moral cost in surveillance, stigma and the humiliation of proving need to a caseworker, and Finland’s participants reported less bureaucratic strain and more trust as a direct result of its removal. The deepest version of the claim is that in an economy built on inherited capital, common resources and accumulated public knowledge, a basic share of the product is owed to everyone as a matter of right, not earned by each generation from scratch.

Question: Should a basic income replace existing benefits or sit on top of them?
Tag: value
Poles: Replace the system / Add a floor
Why contested: The same words describe two policies with opposite distributional effects, and adherents on both left and right call their version a basic income. A libertarian basic income that folds in the whole welfare state and a social-democratic one that adds to it share a name and almost nothing else.
Case for replace the system: The case for replacement is coherence and honesty. The current US system is dozens of programs with different eligibility rules, different agencies, different phase-out schedules that interact to produce marginal tax rates nobody designed, and an administrative apparatus whose cost and intrusiveness falls on the poorest. Collapsing it into one payment removes the caseworker’s discretion, the stigma, the paperwork and the cliff effects in a single step, and lets recipients decide what they need rather than receiving housing aid they cannot convert to childcare. It is also the only version that is affordable without a large new tax, which is why versions of it have been proposed by Milton Friedman, Charles Murray and Andrew Yang. Yang’s design made the trade explicit: recipients could take the $1,000 or keep their existing benefits, not both.
Case for add a floor: Replacement is where the poorest lose. A flat payment cannot do the work of programs that vary with need, because needs vary: a wheelchair user, a dialysis patient and a family in an expensive city all cost more to keep out of poverty than the average person. The OECD simulation shows what replacement does in practice, with more working-age people pushed into poverty than lifted out of it in Finland, France and the UK, precisely because unemployment insurance and disability payments are larger than any affordable flat rate. A basic income built as a floor under the existing system keeps categorical support for categorical needs and adds unconditional security underneath, which is what almost every left proposal, including the Cook County and Stockton programs, actually does. On this view the replacement version is not a basic income at all but a welfare cut wearing its name.

Question: Would a basic income be captured by landlords and sellers rather than kept by recipients?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Recipients keep it / Rents absorb it
Why contested: Nobody has observed a permanent universal payment in a tight housing market, so the question is answered by analogy: to housing vouchers, to student loans, to local cash transfer experiments, and the analogies point different ways.
Case for recipients keep it: The best general equilibrium evidence comes from a study that saturated 653 rural Kenyan villages with cash equal to more than 15 percent of local GDP, and found a local income multiplier of about 2.5 with minimal price inflation, including large gains for people who received nothing. Cash spent locally raises demand, and where supply can respond, it responds. A universal payment is also spread evenly across the whole country rather than concentrated in one housing submarket, unlike a housing voucher, so it does not bid up a fixed local stock the way a place-based subsidy can. Alaska has paid a dividend to everyone for forty years without observable capitalization into statewide rents. And to the extent price effects do occur, they fall on goods in inelastic supply, which is an argument for building more housing rather than for withholding income.
Case for rents absorb it: Where supply is fixed, extra purchasing power becomes higher prices, and housing in growing cities is close to fixed in the short run. Research on US housing vouchers and on federal student aid finds substantial pass-through to landlords and to tuition, and the mechanism is identical: a subsidy for an inelastically supplied good is partly captured by whoever owns it. A universal payment is the most predictable income stream a landlord could price against, since it never stops and never varies. The Kenyan multiplier evidence comes from a rural economy with a lot of slack, unimproved land and spare labor, which is close to the opposite of a supply-constrained metropolitan housing market. If a significant share of the payment ends up as rent, the policy transfers money from taxpayers to property owners with the recipient as a conduit, and its distributional effect is not the one intended.

Question: Will artificial intelligence displace enough work to make a basic income necessary?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Displacement is arriving / Labor markets adapt
Why contested: Automation has repeatedly destroyed occupations without producing lasting unemployment, and the people who say this time is different have said it before. But the early labor data from the generative AI period is the first in decades that shows a clear cohort-specific effect.
Case for displacement is arriving: Stanford’s Digital Economy Lab found that employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations fell about 13 percent between late 2022 and late 2025 relative to less-exposed peers, with no comparable gap for experienced workers, and the mechanism was reduced hiring rather than layoffs. Roughly eight in ten Americans now expect AI to reduce the number of US jobs over the next decade. The people building the systems say so themselves: Sam Altman argued in 2016 that a basic income would become necessary and then funded the largest US trial of it. If the entry rungs of the career ladder are removed, the problem is not a temporary mismatch that retraining fixes but a structural change in who the economy has use for, and cash is the only instrument that works regardless of which jobs go.
Case for labor markets adapt: There is still no evidence of economy-wide job displacement; aggregate employment has not fallen, and AI-attributed layoff announcements are a small fraction of all announced cuts. The historical record is one-sided: mechanized agriculture, containerized shipping, spreadsheets and industrial robots each eliminated large categories of work and each was followed by higher total employment, because falling costs raise demand and create tasks nobody forecast. Entry-level weakness in AI-exposed occupations is real but confounded with a post-pandemic hiring correction, high interest rates and a white-collar labor market that was overbuilt in 2021 and 2022. Building a permanent entitlement on a forecast that has been wrong for two centuries is an expensive bet, and if displacement does arrive, wage insurance, training and adjustment assistance are better matched to a transition than an unconditional payment to everyone including people whose jobs are untouched.

Question: Do results from time-limited pilots tell us anything about a permanent national program?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Pilots generalize / Pilots cannot
Why contested: The entire modern evidence base is pilots, and the features that make a pilot feasible, small, temporary and targeted, are exactly the features a basic income does not have.
Case for pilots generalize: The pilots now number more than 120 in the United States alone, with $481 million paid to over 40,000 people, plus national-scale evidence from Iran, four decades of Alaskan dividends, five income-maintenance experiments in the 1970s and the largest and longest trial in Kenya. The findings are broadly consistent across very different designs and decades: people spend the money on necessities, mental health improves, labor supply moves a little and not much. That convergence across settings is what external validity looks like in social science. Refusing to generalize is not caution but a demand for the one experiment nobody will ever be allowed to run, and it has the convenient property of never being satisfied.
Case for pilots cannot: A pilot payment that ends in two years is not the same good as a payment that never ends; people treat it as a windfall to save or invest rather than as a permanent change in what they can afford, which is precisely what the Kenyan study found when the two-year and twelve-year arms diverged despite identical money. Pilots pay a few hundred people out of philanthropy, so there is no tax to finance them and therefore none of the economy-wide effects that dominate the cost estimates. They do not produce general equilibrium effects on wages, prices or social norms, because everyone around the recipient is unaffected. Most had small samples and heavy attrition, and nearly every high-quality US pilot ran during or just after the pandemic, when transfers, labor markets and eviction rules were all abnormal. The two models that tried to project a national program from this evidence produced answers 16 percentage points of GDP apart.

Question: Would a universal payment prove politically durable, or erode into something smaller?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Universality protects it / Universality erodes
Why contested: The standard political science claim is that universal programs are the hardest to cut, and Social Security is the exhibit. But the few real universal cash programs have been cut, repeatedly, and the exhibits for that are Alaska and Iran.
Case for universality protects it: Programs for the poor are poor programs. Means-tested benefits have narrow constituencies, attract fraud narratives and get cut in every fiscal squeeze; universal programs create a majority of beneficiaries who will defend them. Social Security and Medicare are the least-cut parts of the US budget for exactly this reason. A basic income would have the broadest constituency any program has ever had, which is why some conservatives oppose it as a ratchet and some progressives support it as one. The 2021 Child Tax Credit expansion, near-universal but time-limited by statute, lapsed because it had a sunset date and no organized constituency yet, not because voters rejected it.
Case for universality erodes: Alaska’s dividend is universal, popular, forty years old, and the legislature has ignored its statutory formula every year since 2016, paying $1,000 in 2025 where the formula implied $3,650. Iran’s universal transfer has been eroded by inflation and partially means-tested. In both cases universality survived while the amount did not, which is the failure mode that matters, because a basic income too small to live on is not a basic income. Fiscal pressure does not have to repeal a program to defeat it; it only has to stop indexing it. And the direct votes suggest the political constituency is weaker than the theory predicts: Hamburg rejected a three-year pilot by nearly two to one, Switzerland rejected a permanent scheme by more than three to one, and six US states have banned their own cities from trying.

Question: Is cash or a guaranteed job the better answer to economic insecurity?
Tag: value
Poles: Cash and freedom / Jobs and purpose
Why contested: The two proposals are usually costed against each other as if they solved the same problem, but they rest on different views of what work is for. One treats work as a means to income; the other treats it as a source of meaning, structure and social standing that money cannot buy.
Case for cash and freedom: Cash is the only transfer that respects the recipient’s judgment about their own life, and the evidence is that people spend it on food, rent, transport and their children, not on the vices opponents predict. A job guarantee cannot reach the people most in need of support, because the reason many of them are poor is that they cannot work: carers, the chronically ill, the disabled, people in areas with no transport. It also requires the state to manufacture millions of genuinely useful jobs and to supervise the people doing them, which is a vast administrative undertaking with its own coercion built in, since the offer is only as voluntary as the alternative to refusing it. And a job guarantee does nothing about the underlying problem if AI displacement is real, since the state would be creating jobs to replace jobs the market found unnecessary.
Case for jobs and purpose: Unemployment damages people in ways that income replacement does not repair; the evidence on wellbeing is that losing a job hurts far more than losing the equivalent income, and that the harm is about identity, routine and social connection. A job guarantee delivers those alongside the money, sets an effective wage floor across the whole labor market by giving every worker an outside option, and produces public goods, care work, maintenance and environmental restoration that markets under-supply. It is also far cheaper: the Levy Institute costs a $15-an-hour program covering 11 to 16 million people at 0.8 to 2 percent of GDP net, against 6.5 percent for a basic income at a quarter of median income, because a job guarantee only pays people who take it up. And it keeps faith with the reciprocity norm that public support depends on, which is why unions and much of the labor movement prefer it.

## Positions

Camp: Expand and improve targeted transfers instead of paying everyone
Adherents: Robert Greenstein, founder of the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, “Universal Basic Income May Sound Attractive But, If It Occurred, Would Likelier Increase Poverty Than Reduce It” (https://www.cbpp.org/research/poverty-and-opportunity/commentary-universal-basic-income-may-sound-attractive-but-if-it); the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, Basic Income as a Policy Option (https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/topics/policy-sub-issues/income-support-redistribution-and-work-incentives/basic-income-policy-option-2017-brackground-technical-note.pdf)
Case in its own voice: We are not against cash and we are not against generosity. We are against arithmetic that does not work. A country has a finite amount it will spend on transfers, and the question is where that money lands. Spread it evenly and you take it from the bottom fifth and hand a large share of it to people who are comfortable. Greenstein put it plainly: redirecting dollars currently targeted at the poorest to universal payments across the whole income scale is redistribution upward, and it would increase poverty rather than reduce it. The existing system is not elegant, but it was built program by program because needs differ. A disabled adult, a lone parent in an expensive city and a healthy single person with a job do not need the same amount of money, and a flat payment that ignores the difference is not fairer, only simpler. Every real problem the basic income movement names is a problem we agree exists and that we know how to fix without dismantling anything: raise take-up by simplifying applications, smooth benefit cliffs, pay the Child Tax Credit monthly and make it fully refundable, and extend coverage to the people currently missing. Those are unglamorous reforms with large and measurable returns, and they cost a fraction of a universal payment.
Best evidence: The OECD simulated a budget-neutral basic income in Finland, France, Italy and the United Kingdom and found it would fund only €527, €456, €158 and £230 a month respectively, none reaching the national poverty line, and would push more working-age people into poverty than out of it in three of the four countries. The IMF found a basic income at 25 percent of median income would cost 6.5 percent of GDP in advanced economies for a 7.5 percentage point poverty reduction, a return existing targeted programs beat in most rich countries. The 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit, a targeted improvement rather than a universal payment, cut US child poverty from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent in a single year, the largest fall on record, and its lapse doubled it again.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that means tests miss people and humiliate those they reach. We take it seriously and it is why we have spent decades on take-up. But the numbers do not support abandonment: SNAP now reaches 88 percent of eligible individuals, its highest rate in nearly 50 years, and it got there by simplifying applications, not by paying everyone. The remaining 12 percent is a solvable outreach problem costing a few billion dollars, not a reason to spend two trillion. On stigma, we note that the basic income answer is to hand the poorest less money and call it dignity. A version of this camp goes further and argues the answer to insecurity is not cash at all but universal basic services, free or near-free childcare, transport, housing and health provision, on the grounds that a pound of public service buys more security than a pound in hand; that argument shares our diagnosis and differs on the instrument.

Camp: Require work or job-seeking as a condition of public support
Adherents: The Heritage Foundation, “Universal Basic Income Harms Recipients and Increases Dependence on Government” (https://www.heritage.org/welfare/report/universal-basic-income-harms-recipients-and-increases-dependence-government); the Foundation for Government Accountability, “Why States Should Ban Universal Basic Income Schemes” (https://thefga.org/research/why-states-should-ban-universal-basic-income-schemes/)
Case in its own voice: Work is not a cost that welfare policy should minimize. It is the thing that lifts people out of poverty and keeps them out, and it is also how adults acquire skills, structure, self-respect and a place in a community. The 1996 welfare reform proved the point: when benefits came with a work expectation, caseloads fell, employment among single mothers rose and child poverty fell. Unconditional cash reverses that lesson at exactly the wrong moment, with millions of open jobs going unfilled and a labor force that has still not recovered its pre-pandemic participation. Under a basic income, for the first time, an adult who refuses to work is entitled to the same public support as one who works two jobs, and the message that sends is not a technicality; it is the whole meaning of the policy. We also object to the way this is being done. City councils and county boards are spending taxpayer money on redistribution schemes the legislature never authorized, and states are right to stop them, which is why Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, South Dakota and Texas have.
Best evidence: The largest randomized trial of unconditional cash in the United States found recipients cut labor market participation by 4.2 percentage points and earned about $1,900 a year less, with the freed time going mainly to leisure and no improvement in job quality that the authors could detect. Across the four largest US guaranteed income pilots, holding 55 percent of all treatment participants, employment fell 3.2 percentage points. The 1970s negative income tax experiments found the same direction: reductions of two to four weeks of work a year, which is why Congress abandoned the idea and built a work-based system instead. Gross cost figures confirm the scale of the commitment: $2.8 trillion a year for $1,000 a month to every US adult, against $7.0 trillion in total federal spending.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that many poor adults cannot work, and that a work requirement punishes carers, the sick and the disabled. That is a fair description of a badly designed requirement and not of ours. Every serious work-conditional program exempts the disabled, the elderly and the primary carers of young children; the requirement applies to able-bodied adults without dependents, who are a minority of recipients and the group where the behavioral response is largest. We would rather argue about where to draw those exemptions than accept a design that draws none. On the claim that cash pilots show employment rising, we point out that the pilots showing gains are the small ones, too small to measure a three-point effect, and that the large ones show losses.

Camp: Pay a universal basic income on top of the existing safety net
Adherents: The Economic Security Project (https://economicsecurityproject.org/guaranteedincome/); Michael Tubbs, founder of Mayors for a Guaranteed Income and former mayor of Stockton (https://www.tides.org/blog/guaranteed-income-stockton-mayor-michael-tubbs/); the Basic Income Earth Network (https://basicincome.org/about-basic-income/)
Case in its own voice: Give people cash, no strings attached, and put it underneath everything else rather than in place of it. The point is a floor: an amount below which nobody in a rich country falls, paid automatically, so that a car repair or a late paycheck or a sick week does not turn into an eviction. We are not proposing to take away anyone’s disability payment or housing assistance, and any proposal that does is a welfare cut using our language. What we are proposing is that the floor exist at all, and that it exist without a caseworker deciding who deserves it. The evidence from our own pilots is not that people stop working; it is that people who could not previously take an unpaid training course, turn down a shift that conflicted with childcare, or leave a job that was making them ill can now do those things. That is the freedom the money buys. It is also faster and cheaper to deliver than any program we could design, because there is nothing to verify.
Best evidence: In Stockton, full-time employment among recipients rose from 28 to 40 percent in a year against a 5-point rise in the control group, and recipients reported large falls in anxiety and depression, with under one percent of tracked spending going to alcohol or tobacco. The twelve-year Kenyan trial, the longest and largest ever run, found no reduction in labor supply in any arm and incomes 50 percent above control in the lump-sum group. Iran’s universal transfer reached more than 70 million people at 28 percent of median per capita expenditure with no fall in aggregate hours worked and gains for women. Alaska has paid an unconditional dividend to every resident since 1982 without reducing aggregate employment. Across 122 US pilots, $481 million has gone to more than 40,000 people and the money went to groceries, rent, utilities and car repairs.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is the cost, and it is the only objection that lands. Our answer has three parts. First, the $2.8 trillion figure is gross: a basic income recovered through the tax system from people who do not need it costs a fraction of that net, which is why serious designs pair the payment with a tax schedule rather than quoting the payment alone. Second, the comparison should be against what poverty already costs in emergency rooms, child protection, policing and lost human capital, not against zero. Third, we are not asking for the full program tomorrow. The route runs through what Cook County just did, making a county-scale program permanent, and through a federal pilot like the one introduced in Congress in October 2025, so that the design questions are answered before the money is committed.

Camp: Pay unconditional allowances to broad categories rather than to everyone
Adherents: The Niskanen Center, “The Conservative Case for a Child Allowance” by Samuel Hammond and Robert Orr (https://www.niskanencenter.org/report-the-conservative-case-for-a-child-allowance/); Senator Mitt Romney’s Family Security Act, drafted with Hammond’s input (https://spotlightonpoverty.org/spotlight-exclusives/niskanen-centers-sam-hammond-on-mitt-romneys-plan-for-social-security-for-kids/)
Case in its own voice: The interesting part of the basic income idea is unconditionality, not universality, and you can have the first without paying for the second. Send a flat monthly payment to every family with a child, or to every adult over 65, or to every disabled adult, with no work test, no means test and no caseworker. You get almost all of the simplicity, all of the automatic take-up and all of the dignity, at a fifth of the cost, because you are not paying working-age adults without dependents who are the group most likely to reduce their work in response. Category is doing the targeting that a means test used to do, and it is doing it with information the government already has. This is also the only version of the idea with a real political coalition: the Family Security Act came from a Republican senator, the expanded Child Tax Credit came from a Democratic Congress, and both did the same thing. The United States spends 0.1 percent of GDP on cash benefits to families, the lowest in the OECD, and has the highest child poverty rate in the developed world. That is the gap to close first.
Best evidence: The 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit is the proof of concept: near-universal, monthly, unconditional on earnings, and it cut child poverty from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent in one year, then let it more than double to 12.4 percent when it lapsed. The policy reached families with no earnings, which the old credit did not, and the research found no large employment response over the period it operated. Every other rich country already does some version of this; the US is the outlier, and the countries with child allowances have child poverty rates a fraction of the American one.
Answer to the strongest objection: Basic income advocates say this is half a loaf that entrenches the idea that only some people deserve unconditional money. We would say it is the loaf that can be bought. The objection from the other direction, that a child allowance pays people to have children they cannot support, is answered by the data: allowances of this size have small effects on fertility and large effects on the material conditions of children who already exist. On the charge that our conservative version quietly consolidates the Earned Income Tax Credit and head-of-household filing into the allowance and therefore cuts some working families, that is a real trade in Romney’s design and it is negotiable; the allowance does not require it.

Camp: Guarantee a job rather than an income
Adherents: Pavlina Tcherneva and the Levy Economics Institute, Public Service Employment: A Path to Full Employment (https://www.levyinstitute.org/pubs/rpr_4_18.pdf); Mark Paul, William Darity Jr. and Darrick Hamilton, “A Path to Ending Poverty by Way of Ending Unemployment: A Federal Job Guarantee” (https://www.rsfjournal.org/content/4/3/44)
Case in its own voice: The question as posed is wrong. It assumes that the problem is how to distribute income and that the only variable is who gets a check. The problem is that the economy does not produce enough decent work, and no amount of cash fixes that. The research on unemployment is unambiguous: the harm is overwhelmingly non-financial, somewhere between 85 and 93 percent of the total cost, and it comes from the loss of structure, purpose, standing and social contact. Hand an unemployed person money and you have addressed the small part. Offer them a job at a living wage doing work their community needs, care, maintenance, environmental restoration, and you address all of it, and get the work done. A job guarantee also sets a wage floor across the entire labor market, because no private employer can hire below the public option, which does more for low-wage workers than a payment they would receive whether or not they were exploited. And it is affordable in a way a basic income is not, because only the people who want it take it up.
Best evidence: The Levy Institute costs a $15-an-hour program with benefits, employing 11 to 16 million people at peak, at 0.8 to 2 percent of GDP net, against the IMF’s 6.5 percent of GDP for a basic income at a quarter of median income. Paul, Darity and Hamilton note that full-time earnings under a job guarantee, a median around $32,500 a year, exceed what any politically realistic basic income could pay. The historical precedent is the Works Progress Administration and, more recently, India’s rural employment guarantee, which has provided work to tens of millions of households.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that we would exclude the people least able to work, and require the state to invent millions of jobs it cannot supervise. On exclusion, we are not proposing to abolish anything: disability payments, pensions, child benefits and income support stay, and the job guarantee sits alongside them for people who want work and cannot find it. On the jobs themselves, the claim that there is nothing useful to do is refuted by any walk through an American city: unmet care needs, deferred maintenance, degraded land. The design question is real and we answer it with locally proposed, federally funded projects rather than a central jobs bureau. And on coercion, an offer is not a requirement; the guarantee obliges the government, not the worker.

Camp: Replace the existing welfare system with a single unconditional payment
Adherents: Charles Murray, American Enterprise Institute, In Our Hands: A Plan to Replace the Welfare State (https://www.aei.org/research-products/book/in-our-hands-a-plan-to-replace-the-welfare-state/); Andrew Yang, whose 2020 presidential campaign made the Freedom Dividend its central proposal (https://2020.yang2020.com/policies/the-freedom-dividend/)
Case in its own voice: The case is not generosity, it is liberty and honesty about what the current system costs. Government transfers in the United States run to trillions of dollars administered through dozens of programs, each with its own eligibility rules, its own agency, its own phase-out schedule, and the combined effect is a maze that punishes work through effective marginal tax rates nobody intended and treats poor adults as wards rather than citizens. Murray’s proposal is to scrap the lot, Social Security, Medicare and every income transfer, and send every American over 21 a fixed sum for life, with a surtax clawing back half of it from higher earners. Yang’s was gentler, $1,000 a month with recipients choosing between the dividend and their existing benefits, funded by a value-added tax on the companies capturing the gains from automation. Both share the core claim: a citizen with cash in hand makes better decisions about their own life than an agency makes on their behalf, and the money currently spent deciding for them is the waste. It also restores something the welfare state destroyed, which is the network of family, neighbors and voluntary association that used to deal with need, because those institutions can only function when people have resources to bring to them.
Best evidence: The administrative comparison is stark and uncontested: Social Security spends about 0.5 percent of benefits on administration while means-tested Supplemental Security Income spends about 7 to 8 percent, and the burden borne by claimants in time and denied claims appears in neither figure. Twelve percent of SNAP-eligible individuals and 22 percent of EITC-eligible taxpayers receive nothing at all because the system is too complicated to navigate. Murray’s arithmetic is that current transfer spending already exceeds what his grant would cost, which is the point: the money exists, it is simply being spent on a system rather than on people.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that the poorest lose, because a flat payment cannot replace benefits that vary with need, and the OECD simulation showing more people pushed into poverty than lifted out is the evidence for it. Two answers. First, that simulation holds the budget fixed at current European levels; our proposal sets the grant at a level we specify and funds it, rather than dividing an existing pot. Second, the objection assumes the current system delivers what it promises to the people it targets, and the take-up numbers say it does not. We would accept a transitional design of the kind Yang proposed, where nobody is forced to give up an existing benefit, precisely because we would rather win the argument on results than on compulsion. We also accept the fair charge that our camp and the floor-under-the-safety-net camp share a name and almost nothing else, and that readers should always ask which one a given proposal is.

## Crux

Under every argument in this topic sits one question: what does a guaranteed floor do to the person standing on it? Everyone in the debate agrees that nobody in a rich country should be destitute, and almost everyone agrees that the money has to come from taxes on the same society that receives it. What they do not agree on is whether security is the precondition for effort or a substitute for it. One side believes that a person who knows the rent is covered takes the training course, starts the business, leaves the abusive employer, finishes the degree, and that the resulting society is more productive than one held together by the fear of falling. The other believes that a person who knows the rent is covered does somewhat less of everything, that the effect compounds across a whole population and across generations, and that a society which detaches income from contribution loses something it cannot get back by spending more. Every other disagreement in this topic — about cost, about targeting, about pilots, about jobs versus cash — is downstream of that one, because the answer determines whether the money spent comes back.

What evidence could settle: whether a permanent, universal, livable payment financed by real taxes changes total work, earnings, business formation, education, health and the outcomes of recipients’ children, and by how much. This is a measurable quantity. The obstacle is that no such program has existed. Every estimate available today comes from a payment that was temporary, or small, or given to a selected group, or financed by philanthropy rather than taxation, and the differences between those designs turn out to matter enormously: the Kenyan trial found that a two-year payment and a twelve-year payment of identical total value produced very different behavior. A national program run long enough to observe, with a real tax base behind it and a pre-registered design, would settle most of the empirical dispute within a decade. Short of that, the question can be narrowed by better evidence on the income elasticity of labor supply at the bottom of the distribution, on whether reduced work hours go to care, study and job search or to leisure, and on the long-run outcomes of children in households that received unconditional money.

What evidence cannot settle: how to value a reduction in paid work. Suppose the empirical question were answered tomorrow and the number were exactly the one the largest US trial found, four percentage points of labor force participation and an hour or two a week. That result does not tell anyone whether the policy succeeded. A parent who cuts hours to be home when a child gets off the bus, a worker who leaves a job that was damaging their health, a person who stops driving for a rideshare app to finish a qualification — these are reductions in measured labor supply, and whether they are losses or gains depends on what a society thinks work is for and on what it believes people owe each other in return for support. Behind that sits an older question no dataset reaches: whether a share of a society’s product is something each generation earns from scratch, or something owed to every member by virtue of membership, given that most of what makes a modern economy productive was inherited rather than made by anyone now alive. A person who answers that second question one way will find a small work reduction acceptable; a person who answers it the other way will find it disqualifying, and they will both be reasoning correctly from their premises.
