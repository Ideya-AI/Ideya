# Assisted Dying — research

## Overview

Assisted dying here means a death brought about, at the person’s own settled request, with help from the state-regulated medical system: a doctor writes a prescription the person swallows, or a doctor or nurse administers a lethal drug. The topic covers whether a competent adult has a right to that help, and the design choices that follow if the answer is yes — who qualifies, how capacity and voluntariness are tested, how long the waiting periods run, who may refuse to take part, and what oversight looks like afterward. It takes in the two main legal models: assisted suicide, where the person performs the final act, and voluntary euthanasia, where a clinician does. It excludes suicide and its prevention generally, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment and do-not-resuscitate decisions, which are legal nearly everywhere and rest on a different principle, and non-voluntary or involuntary killing of patients who have not asked. It also excludes the question of whether a longer life is worth having, which belongs to a different topic.

Why now: on 11 September 2026 the House of Commons rejected the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill at second reading by 286 votes to 270 (Pharmaceutical Journal, https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/assisted-dying-bill-defeated-in-house-of-commons). The bill, introduced by Lauren Edwards MP on 17 June 2026, was substantially the same text that an earlier Commons had approved at third reading on 20 June 2025 by 314 to 291, before it ran out of time in the House of Lords when the session ended in April 2026. Britain therefore spent two years moving toward legalization and then reversed: the Scottish Parliament had already rejected the Assisted Dying for Terminally Ill Adults (Scotland) Bill at stage 3 on 17 March 2026 by 69 to 57.

Elsewhere the movement ran the other way in the same months. France enacted a right to assistance in dying in LOI n° 2026-794 of 18 August 2026, after the National Assembly’s final vote of 291 to 241 on 15 July 2026 and approval by the Constitutional Council on 14 August (Légifrance, https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/id/JORFTEXT000054706877/). Jersey adopted its own law on 26 February 2026 and received Royal Assent in July 2026, with a service planned for 2027. In the United States, Illinois signed its End-of-Life Options for Terminally Ill Patients Act on 12 December 2025, in force from 12 September 2026, and New York’s Medical Aid in Dying Act took effect on 5 August 2026.

The debate is live because the practice has stopped being marginal where it exists. Assisted deaths are now about 6 percent of all deaths in the Netherlands, 5.1 percent in Canada and 7.9 percent in Quebec, which raises questions about drift and about the health and social systems people are choosing against that nobody had to answer when the numbers were in the hundreds. The participants are legislatures and courts, national medical associations that have moved from opposition toward neutrality in some countries and not others, palliative medicine specialists divided among themselves, disability rights organizations that are among the most organized opponents, religious institutions, and campaigning charities on both sides with large memberships.

Context: This single row of numbers does most of the work in the argument, and it is also the row most easily misread. The jurisdictions differ in what they permit: the Netherlands, Belgium, Canada, Switzerland and Spain allow assistance for non-terminal conditions, while Australia, New Zealand, Oregon and the other US states require a terminal prognosis, usually six months. They differ in how long the law has been running — 1997 in Oregon, 2002 in the Netherlands and Belgium, 2016 in Canada, 2021 in Spain and New Zealand — and uptake rises for many years after a law starts. They differ in how the deaths are counted: Switzerland’s figure is derived by dividing 1,774 assisted suicides among residents in 2024 by an estimated 71,100 total deaths, and Health Canada explicitly warns that its 5.1 percent should not be treated as a cause-of-death share, because the death certificate records the underlying disease. The order does not track how permissive a law is on paper: Spain’s law allows assistance for non-terminal suffering and produces one of the lowest rates anywhere. Underlying counts, where they help: Netherlands 10,341 notifications; Canada 16,499; Belgium 4,486 registrations; Switzerland 1,774; New Zealand 486 in the year to 31 March 2026; Oregon 400; Spain 565.

Context: Quebec legislated before the rest of Canada, in 2014, and its rate has risen every year: 7.3 percent in 2023-24, 7.9 percent in 2024-25, with growth slowing from 10 percent to 9 percent. The commission judged 99.7 percent of administrations compliant with the law. The average interval from signed request to administration was 44 days. For comparison, continuous palliative sedation — sedating a dying patient to unconsciousness until death — accounted for 2.7 percent of Quebec deaths in the same year.

Context: Patients may cite more than one concern, so the figures do not sum to 100. This table is the empirical heart of several arguments at once. Canada’s reporting shows the same pattern in a different form: Health Canada found that people citing self-perceived burden reported more sources of suffering on average than those who did not, and that burden was the sole source of suffering in fewer than five cases nationally in 2024.

Note: 732 of Canada’s 16,499 assisted deaths in 2024 — 4.4 percent — were under Track 2, for people whose natural death was not reasonably foreseeable.
Context: Track 2 was created by Bill C-7 in 2021 after a Quebec court held that restricting MAID to the dying was unconstitutional, and it is the provision that separates Canada from Australia, New Zealand and the US states. Track 2 growth is slowing, from 33.3 percent between 2022 and 2023 to 17.1 percent between 2023 and 2024. A separate Canadian provision, for people whose sole underlying condition is a mental illness, has been postponed twice (Bill C-39, March 2023; Bill C-62, February 2024) and is currently due to take effect on 17 March 2027.

Context: Compliance findings run at this level year after year: 7 in 2021, 13 in 2022, 5 in 2023, 6 in 2024, 7 in 2025. Supporters cite this as proof that a mature system is being followed. Quebec’s commission reports a comparable 99.7 percent compliance rate, and Oregon made no referrals to its medical board for reporting failures in 2025.

Note: Between 1 April 2009 and 31 March 2026 police referred 209 assisted suicide cases to the CPS; 131 were not proceeded with, 42 were withdrawn, 6 were prosecuted successfully and 2 ended in acquittal.
Context: Section 2 of the Suicide Act 1961 makes encouraging or assisting a suicide an offence carrying up to 14 years in prison, and it has never been repealed. Many of the referred cases involve relatives who accompanied someone to Switzerland. Both sides use these numbers. Opponents say the same numbers show a system that already exercises mercy in individual cases while keeping the prohibition that protects everyone else — a deterrent without a punishment.

Context: Switzerland is the oldest and the least medicalized system. Article 115 of the penal code, in force since 1942, criminalizes assisting a suicide only when done from selfish motives, so nothing in Swiss law requires a doctor’s authorization, a terminal prognosis or a waiting period; a physician is needed only to prescribe the drug. The work is done by non-profit associations, principally EXIT and DIGNITAS, and DIGNITAS is the only provider that accepts non-residents, which is why Britons and Germans travel there. Assisted deaths are heavily concentrated in old age: more than 90 percent of the 2023 cases were people aged 65 or over.

Context: Spain matters to the argument because it is a permissive law with very low uptake, which complicates any simple claim that legalization leads inexorably to high volumes. Counts of US jurisdictions differ by one or two depending on how Montana is treated, since assisted dying there rests on a 2009 state supreme court decision rather than a statute: twelve states have statutes — Oregon, Washington, Vermont, California, Colorado, Hawaii, New Jersey, Maine, New Mexico, Delaware, Illinois and New York — plus the District of Columbia, with Montana permitted by case law. The jurisdictions split into two families. The terminal-illness model — Oregon 1997 and the twelve US states that followed, Victoria 2019 and the rest of Australia, New Zealand 2021, Jersey 2026 — requires a prognosis, usually six months, and usually requires the person to self-administer. The unbearable-suffering model — the Netherlands and Belgium 2002, Luxembourg 2009, Canada 2016, Colombia, Spain 2021, Austria 2022, Portugal, Ecuador 2024, Uruguay 2025, France 2026 — turns on the severity of suffering rather than proximity to death, and generally permits a clinician to administer. [Correction 2026-10-05: France does not belong in the unbearable-suffering model. Its Law No. 2026-794 of 18 August 2026 sits between the two families: it requires a serious, incurable illness at an advanced or terminal stage but no prognosis in months, and the patient takes the drug themselves unless physically unable (Library of Congress, 2026).] Uruguay’s muerte digna law passed in October 2025 and was brought into force by presidential decree in April 2026. Cuba’s 2023 public health law recognizes a right to a dignified death but its implementation has been postponed indefinitely, so it should not be counted as in force. Germany is a third case: the Federal Constitutional Court struck down the ban on organized assistance in February 2020, recognizing a right to self-determined death, and the Bundestag has passed no replacement law since.

Note: More than 7,000 Australians have died using a voluntary assisted dying substance since Victoria’s law started in 2019, from about 14,000 applications; activity rose about 40 percent nationally in 2024-25.
Context: Australia is the cleanest natural experiment in the terminal-illness model, because six states legislated within four years of each other with slightly different rules, the Australian Capital Territory’s Act commenced on 3 November 2025, and the Northern Territory passed its Rights of the Terminally Ill legislation on 27 August 2026, for implementation in 2028 — which makes voluntary assisted dying lawful across the whole country. The Northern Territory had legalized it once before, in 1995, and the Commonwealth Parliament overrode that law in 1997. National uptake is about 2 percent of all deaths, around 5 percent of cancer deaths and roughly one in three deaths from motor neurone disease. Victoria’s scheme, the most restrictive, has drawn persistent criticism from its own review board about access barriers — including a federal law that prevents clinicians discussing the subject by telephone or video: in Carr v Attorney-General (Cth), decided 30 November 2023, the Federal Court held that the Commonwealth Criminal Code’s ban on using a carriage service to provide instruction on suicide covers voluntary assisted dying. The provision was written in 2005 against pro-suicide websites, it has not been amended as of September 2026, and it falls hardest on rural patients. The figures are compiled by a campaigning organization from the published reports of each state’s oversight body rather than by a national statistical agency.

## Questions

Question: Does a law written for a narrow group stay narrow?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Holds the line / Widens over time
Why contested: Every jurisdiction that legalized has seen its numbers rise for many years afterward, and several have widened their criteria by legislation or court ruling. Whether that pattern is drift or maturation cannot be read off the numbers alone, because a new service would be expected to grow while awareness spreads and clinicians train, and because some widening was ordered by courts rather than chosen by legislatures.
Case for holding the line: Oregon’s law is 28 years old and has never been widened beyond terminal illness with a six-month prognosis and self-administration; the 2023 removal of the residency requirement was the only substantive change, and it loosened access rather than eligibility. All eight Australian states and territories, New Zealand and every US jurisdiction have kept the terminal-illness frame. Rising numbers in those places reflect a service reaching the people it was written for: Oregon is at 1.0 percent of deaths after nearly three decades, Australia at about 2 percent, New Zealand at 1.3 percent. Where criteria did widen, as in Canada, the mechanism was a court finding that restricting assistance to the dying breached a constitutional equality guarantee — a question of constitutional law in one country, not a property of assisted dying laws.
Case for widening over time: The Netherlands went from 1,882 notifications in 2002 to 10,341 in 2025 and from euthanasia being exceptional to being 6 percent of deaths, while extending in practice to dementia, psychiatric illness, tired-of-life cases folded under accumulated age-related conditions, and children. Belgium removed the age limit entirely in 2014 and rose 12.4 percent in a single year to 2025. Canada wrote a law for the dying in 2016, added the non-dying in 2021, legislated mental illness as a qualifying condition and has postponed the start date twice. The logic that gets a law passed — that it is cruel to refuse relief to someone suffering unbearably — does not contain a stopping point at the prognosis line, which is why the line keeps moving.

Question: Can a system detect pressure on the people most likely to feel it?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Safeguards can screen / Pressure stays invisible
Why contested: Coercion by a relative is rare and criminal, and both sides agree assessors can usually spot it. The harder question is the pressure that nobody applies: a person who believes their care is exhausting their family, or whose home support is inadequate, and who converts that into a settled wish to die. Reporting systems record what the assessor concluded, so they are weak evidence about what the assessor could not see.
Case for screening working: Two independent clinicians assess every case in every jurisdiction, with capacity assessment, a mandated waiting period and a duty to explore alternatives. Where the data exist, they do not look like a picture of the vulnerable being pushed: Health Canada found MAID recipients did not disproportionately come from lower-income neighborhoods; in Oregon, all 2025 recipients had health insurance, 92 percent were in hospice, half had a bachelor’s degree or higher, and 6 percent cited the financial implications of treatment. Refusals are real — Canada found 1,327 people ineligible in 2024 and 692 withdrew; Spain approves fewer than half of requests. And the comparison is not against a pressure-free world: people already refuse treatment, stop eating and drinking, or take their own lives violently and alone, with no assessment at all.
Case for pressure staying invisible: Forty percent of Oregon’s 2025 cases cited being a burden on family, friends or caregivers, and the assessment process has no instrument that can separate a wish arising from a person’s own values from one produced by inadequate care. The people best placed to notice — disabled people who live with low-level messaging that their lives are worse — are the ones warning loudest. Canada’s own figures show 61.5 percent of Track 2 recipients self-identified as having a disability and only 23.2 percent received palliative care. A system that certifies its own compliance at 99.7 percent, and that relies on the reporting clinician’s account of a conversation nobody else witnessed, is not built to find the cases it missed.

Question: Should eligibility turn on how close death is, or on how bad the suffering is?
Kind: value
Poles: Prognosis line / Suffering line
Why contested: This is the choice that separates the two families of law, and both lines are defensible and both are arbitrary at the edges. A six-month prognosis is administrable but medically unreliable and excludes the conditions that often produce the longest suffering. Unbearable suffering tracks the actual reason for the request but cannot be measured from outside.
Case for the prognosis line: Restricting assistance to people already dying keeps the decision inside a situation everyone recognizes — the question is not whether to die but how — and it sharply limits the population in which a mistake is possible. It also makes the safeguard workable: a person with weeks to live cannot be talked into forgoing decades. Oregon has run on this rule since 1997 without widening it, and the rule is what allowed legalization to pass in fourteen US jurisdictions (13 states and the District of Columbia), six Australian states and New Zealand. Six percent of Oregon cases outlive the prognosis, which is an argument for humility about medicine, not for abandoning the line.
Case for the suffering line: A prognosis rule delivers the wrong answer in exactly the cases that motivated the law. A person with advanced multiple sclerosis, locked-in syndrome or severe treatment-resistant illness may face a decade of the suffering the terminal patient faces for a month, and telling them to wait until they are dying enough is a rule about paperwork rather than about mercy. The Canadian court in Truchon found the reasonable-foreseeability requirement unconstitutional on precisely this ground: it made the severity of a person’s suffering irrelevant and used proximity to death as a proxy for it. Countries using this line have not collapsed; Spain permits non-terminal eligibility and assists 0.13 percent of its dying.

Question: Does legal assisted dying weaken or strengthen palliative care?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Crowds it out / Pulls it up
Why contested: Both causal stories are plausible and neither has a clean test, because the countries that legalized were mostly already rich, already had palliative services, and reformed both at once. Palliative care quality is also poorly measured, which is a complaint the Quebec commission makes about its own data.
Case for crowding out: Once a cheap, quick and certain option exists, the pressure to fund a slow, staff-intensive one falls away, and the choice presented to a patient in a system with waiting lists is not a free one. The World Health Organization estimates only about 14 percent of people who need palliative care receive it. Quebec, with the highest assisted-death rate in the world at 7.9 percent of deaths, reports that its own data cannot describe the state of palliative care in the province and asks for reliable indicators — a striking gap in a system that monitors assisted dying to the individual case. In Canada, fewer than a quarter of Track 2 recipients had palliative care.
Case for pulling it up: Legalization has consistently been accompanied by more attention and money for end-of-life care, not less, because the debate forces legislatures to look at how people actually die. Victoria funded palliative care alongside its law; the Westminster bill was paired with a palliative care commission. The usage data do not look like substitution: 74.1 percent of Canadian recipients had palliative care and 92 percent of Oregon recipients were in hospice, so for most people these are sequential, not alternative. Belgium, an early adopter, built one of Europe’s denser palliative networks in the same period. The countries with the worst palliative provision are overwhelmingly countries where assisted dying is illegal.

Question: Can a mental illness be judged irremediable?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Assessable in principle / Never certain enough
Why contested: Eligibility everywhere requires that the condition cannot be relieved in a way the person finds acceptable. For cancer that judgment is routine. For treatment-resistant depression there is no biomarker, remission after many failed treatments is documented, and the desire to die can itself be a symptom of the illness under assessment.
Case for it being assessable: Psychiatrists make irremediability judgments constantly — in deciding to stop active treatment, in long-term institutional care, in capacity assessments for refusing life-saving treatment. The Dutch have done this under review for more than two decades at small and currently falling volumes: 174 cases in 2025, 1.7 percent of notifications, down 20 percent on the year, each reviewed against the due care criteria with a psychiatric second opinion. Refusing categorically means telling people with decades of documented, treatment-resistant suffering that their pain counts less than a cancer patient’s because it is in the brain, which is the discrimination the argument claims to be preventing.
Case for it never being certain enough: There is no reliable way to predict which treatment-resistant patient will recover, and cohort studies find meaningful remission rates even after multiple failed treatments. Canada wrote the provision into law and has now delayed it twice, to March 2027; in April 2026 the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health recommended an indefinite extension for want of evidence that irremediability can be determined, and a parliamentary committee recommended an indefinite pause in June 2026. When the country that legislated the expansion cannot find clinicians willing to say the judgment can be made safely, that is evidence about the judgment, not about political will.

Question: Is helping a patient die compatible with being a doctor?
Kind: value
Poles: Part of care / Breaks the role
Why contested: Medicine’s self-understanding is at stake as much as any patient’s interest, which is why professional bodies have split and several have retreated to neutrality rather than take a side. The same act can be described as the last service a physician renders or as the one thing a physician must never do.
Case for it being part of care: The physician’s job is to relieve suffering for a patient whose own values define what relief means, and medicine already accepts that a competent patient may refuse a ventilator and die. Continuous palliative sedation, legal and uncontroversial, renders a patient permanently unconscious until death — 2.7 percent of Quebec deaths. Drawing the ethical line at the moment of intent rather than at the outcome asks doctors to abandon patients at the point of greatest need in order to keep their own hands clean. The BMA, the Royal College of Physicians and the Royal College of General Practitioners have all dropped opposition, and half of BMA respondents personally supported a change.
Case for it breaking the role: The prohibition on doctors killing patients is not an arbitrary rule but the thing that makes it safe to be a patient, and it has held since the Hippocratic tradition for reasons that have not changed. The American Medical Association’s Opinion 5.7 states that assisted suicide is fundamentally incompatible with the physician’s role as healer and would be difficult or impossible to control; the World Medical Association reaffirmed the same position in 2019. Withdrawing a treatment and administering a lethal drug differ in what the doctor is trying to bring about, and intent is the basis of most moral and legal distinctions we already accept. Doctors’ own behavior tracks this: in the BMA survey only 26 percent said they would be willing to administer life-ending drugs, and 54 percent said they would not.

Question: Should a person be able to request death in advance, for a future self who cannot consent?
Kind: value
Poles: The earlier self binds / The present self rules
Why contested: Advance directives are accepted everywhere for refusing treatment. Extending them to an active death raises the question of whether a person with advanced dementia, who may be content in the moment, is the same person who signed the document and whose wishes should now govern.
Case for the earlier self binding: A competent person’s settled judgment about what they will not live through is the most authentic expression of who they are, and the whole point of writing it down is to reach a time when they cannot speak. If the directive can be overridden whenever the later self no longer objects, it provides no protection at all, and the person has been denied the only mechanism available for the outcome they most feared. Dutch law allows it and the Supreme Court confirmed its lawfulness in 2020; eleven of the 499 Dutch dementia cases in 2025 involved a person no longer competent, so the practice is careful and rare.
Case for the present self ruling: The person in front of the clinician is the person whose life would end, and that person may be enjoying their days and unable to be asked. Acting on a document against a living human being who gives no present assent turns euthanasia from something done at a patient’s request into something done to them on the authority of a stranger they used to be. Canada, Australia and every US state require capacity at the time of death for this reason, and Belgium’s advance directive provision applies only to irreversible unconsciousness. That 492 of 499 Dutch dementia cases involved a patient still competent shows how narrow the acceptable zone really is.

Question: Is the right at stake a right to be left alone, or a right to be helped?
Kind: value
Poles: Freedom from interference / Guaranteed access
Why contested: Framing the claim as a liberty implies the state should stop prosecuting people who help; framing it as a health service implies institutions must provide it, fund it, and make it reachable in rural areas. These lead to different laws, different conscientious objection rules and different arguments about equality.
Case for freedom from interference: The German Federal Constitutional Court struck down the ban on organized assistance in February 2020 on the ground that the right to a self-determined death follows from human dignity and general personality rights, and Germany has passed no replacement statute. Switzerland has operated for eighty years on a penal code provision that simply declines to punish assistance given without selfish motive, with no eligibility criteria, no medical gatekeeper and no state program. On this view, asking the state to run the process is what generates the eligibility criteria, the committees and the pressure to expand — the liberty was the whole claim.
Case for guaranteed access: A right that exists only for people who can find a willing doctor, pay privately or fly to Zurich is a right distributed by wealth and geography. Australia’s federal carriage-service ban still keeps rural patients from using telehealth three years after the Federal Court confirmed it applies. Spain’s uptake sits at 0.13 percent of deaths partly because of conscientious objection registers and uneven regional implementation. If the state decides who is eligible, it takes on a duty to make the decision meaningful — which means funding, training, access standards and an obligation on institutions that refuse to at least transfer care.

## Positions

Camp: Legalize assisted dying for terminally ill adults
Adherents: Dignity in Dying — https://www.dignityindying.org.uk/assisted-dying/ ; Compassion & Choices — https://compassionandchoices.org/our-issues/medical-aid-in-dying ; also Go Gentle Australia, the End of Life Choice Society of New Zealand, Death with Dignity National Center, Kim Leadbeater MP, who carried the 2024-26 Westminster bill, and Lauren Edwards MP, who reintroduced it in 2026.
The case in the camp’s own voice: A small number of people die badly, and no amount of good care changes that. They are already dying, on a timetable nobody chose, and what we are debating is whether the last few weeks belong to them or to the disease. Our proposal is deliberately narrow: an adult, with capacity, with a terminal diagnosis and a prognosis measured in months, who asks more than once, across a waiting period, with two independent doctors satisfied the request is settled and uncoerced, and who takes the medication themselves. That last clause matters — it is the person’s own hand, which is both the strongest safeguard and the clearest statement of whose decision it is. Everything about this sits alongside palliative care rather than against it: more than nine in ten Oregonians who use the law are already in hospice, and about a third of those who obtain the prescription never take it, because knowing the door is unlocked is itself the relief. Meanwhile the status quo is not safety. It is people starving themselves, dying violently and alone, or flying to Switzerland if they have the money and the strength, with their families investigated afterward. A law does not create these deaths; it brings them into the open where they can be assessed, recorded and declined.
Best evidence: Oregon has operated this exact model since 1997 without widening its criteria, and it accounts for 1.0 percent of deaths, with 92 percent of 2025 recipients in hospice and all of them insured. Every Australian state and territory, New Zealand, twelve US states with statutes and the District of Columbia have adopted the same frame; national uptake in Australia is about 2 percent of deaths. Public support is consistently large — 71 percent in Gallup’s US polling, 79 percent in the March 2025 British Social Attitudes survey, 73 percent for the specific terms of the Westminster bill in a May 2025 YouGov poll. In England and Wales, 209 assisted suicide cases were referred to the CPS between 2009 and 2026 and only 6 were prosecuted successfully, which is a law working by discretion rather than by rule.
Answer to the strongest objection — that the line will not hold: Oregon is the test case and the line has held for 28 years, in nearly a dozen US states that copied it and in every Australian state. Where widening happened it had a specific cause: a Canadian constitutional ruling that a prognosis requirement violated an equality guarantee, and a Dutch law that was written around unbearable suffering from the start and therefore never had our line to cross. A statute that names terminal illness and self-administration does not expand itself; a legislature has to vote. If a future parliament wants a different law it can make the argument then, in public, and be judged on it.

Camp: Keep assisted dying prohibited and fund end-of-life care
Adherents: Care Not Killing — https://carenotkilling.org.uk/issues/our-position/ ; Association for Palliative Medicine of Great Britain and Ireland — https://apmonline.org/wp-content/uploads/APM-Position-Statement-on-Assisted-Dying-October-2024-v2.pdf ; also the Catholic Church in Samaritanus bonus, the American Medical Association in Opinion 5.7, the World Medical Association, Baroness Ilora Finlay, and the Evangelical Alliance.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The people who spend their careers at the bedsides of dying patients are the people most opposed to this, and that should count for something. Eighty-two percent of Association for Palliative Medicine members oppose changing the law; in the BMA’s own survey, palliative medicine doctors opposed it 70 to 30 and 84 percent said they would not take part. We are not making an abstract argument. Almost every request we meet is a request for something else — for pain to stop, for a symptom to be controlled, for someone to say the fear is normal, for the family to be looked after. When those things arrive, the request usually goes. What we are asked to legalize is a permanent answer to a temporary state, delivered inside a health system with waiting lists, in a society where only about 14 percent of the people who need palliative care worldwide get it, and where an old person who has read about the cost of their own care can work out what would be convenient without anybody saying a word. The prohibition on doctors deliberately ending patients’ lives is not a piece of religious inheritance. It is the reason a frightened person can say anything to a doctor without wondering which way the conversation is going to turn. Fund hospices properly, make specialist palliative care available everywhere, and the demand this bill claims to serve shrinks to a size the country can look at honestly.
Best evidence: Repeated surveys of palliative specialists find roughly 80 percent opposition across a decade, including the Royal College of Physicians 2019 stratified result of 84.3 percent. The World Health Organization estimates 56.8 million people a year need palliative care and about 14 percent receive it, with 79 percent of the world’s population consuming 13 percent of medical morphine. The Netherlands has gone from 1,882 notifications in 2002 to 10,341 in 2025, and Belgium rose 12.4 percent in the single year to 2025. Forty percent of Oregon’s 2025 cases cited being a burden on family, friends or caregivers, against 37 percent citing pain or fear of it, and in Canada only 23.2 percent of Track 2 recipients received palliative care. Quebec, with the highest rate in the world, reports that it cannot describe the state of its own palliative services.
Answer to the strongest objection — that some suffering is beyond palliative care: It is, and we do not pretend otherwise. Modern palliative medicine has tools most of the public has never heard of, up to and including continuous sedation to unconsciousness, which is legal, used in 2.7 percent of Quebec deaths, and available now. The residue of genuinely unreachable suffering is very small — far smaller than the numbers these laws produce, which is itself the evidence that the laws are not being used by the population they were argued for. And a hard case is a bad reason to change a rule that protects everyone. We are being asked to trade a general protection for a specific mercy, at a price paid by people who are not in the room.

Camp: Extend assisted dying to unbearable suffering without a prognosis rule
Adherents: Dying With Dignity Canada — https://www.dyingwithdignity.ca/advocacy/allow-maid-for-mental-disorders/ ; NVVE, the Dutch Association for a Voluntary End of Life — https://www.nvve.nl/ ; also My Death, My Decision in the UK, Association pour le Droit de Mourir dans la Dignité in Belgium, Derecho a Morir Dignamente in Spain, and the legal scholar Jocelyn Downie.
The case in the camp’s own voice: A six-month prognosis is not a moral principle, it is an actuarial estimate, and it delivers exactly the wrong answer in the cases that motivated this whole debate. The person with advanced multiple sclerosis who will spend eight more years losing function, the person with a spinal injury and intractable pain, the person with a treatment-resistant depression documented across two decades and forty failed interventions — none of them qualify under a terminal-illness law, and all of them are suffering more, and for longer, than the cancer patient who does. Making proximity to death the gate means we assist the people who need it least and refuse the people who need it most. Our criterion is the one the Dutch, the Belgians and the Canadian courts arrived at: a grievous and irremediable condition producing suffering the person finds intolerable and that cannot be relieved in a way they find acceptable. That is assessed case by case, by the person’s own standard, because suffering has no external unit. Vulnerability is also assessed case by case, on the characteristics of the individual in front of the assessor, not by consigning a whole diagnostic group to a category of people presumed incapable of deciding. Refusing on the basis of a category is not protection; it is the same paternalism these groups have been fighting on every other question.
Best evidence: The Quebec Superior Court held in Truchon in 2019 that restricting assistance to those whose death was reasonably foreseeable violated the Canadian Charter, and Parliament accepted the ruling rather than appealing it. In practice the feared flood has not materialized: Track 2 is 4.4 percent of Canadian provisions, Dutch psychiatric cases are 1.7 percent of notifications and fell 20 percent in 2025, and Spain, which has no prognosis requirement at all, assists 0.13 percent of its dying. Oversight finds almost nothing wrong: 7 due care breaches in 10,341 Dutch cases. Refusals are common where assessment is hardest — Track 2 accounts for a quarter of all Canadian ineligibility findings while being 4.4 percent of provisions, which is what a working filter looks like.
Answer to the strongest objection — that this is where coercion and social neglect do their work: The objection is aimed at the right target and points at the wrong remedy. If a person asks to die because their home care is inadequate, the answer is home care, and we campaign for it; several of us have said publicly that a request driven by poverty should be refused and the material need met instead. But the inference that nobody in this group can make an authentic choice does not follow from the fact that some of them face terrible conditions. It says that a disabled person’s judgment about their own life is less trustworthy than a dying person’s — which is the belief the objection claims to be fighting. Assess the individual, refuse where the suffering has a remediable social cause, publish the data, and let the record be judged.

Camp: Reject assisted dying as disability discrimination
Adherents: Not Dead Yet — https://notdeadyet.org/arguments/ ; Inclusion Canada — https://www.inclusioncanada.ca/position-statement/healthcare ; also the Council of Canadians with Disabilities, the UN Special Rapporteur on the rights of persons with disabilities and two other UN experts in a January 2021 statement, https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2021/01/disability-not-reason-sanction-medically-assisted-dying-un-experts , and the UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.
The case in the camp’s own voice: Every argument for these laws is made in the language of choice, and none of it describes the world disabled people live in. The suffering people cite is not mostly pain; in Oregon it is loss of autonomy, loss of dignity, needing help to use the toilet — that is to say, it is disability. Millions of us live with exactly those conditions and consider our lives worth having. When a society writes a statute saying that people in our situation may be helped to die, while the same society will not fund the attendant care that makes our situation livable, it has not expanded our choices; it has told us which of the two options it finds easier to pay for. That message does not need a coercive relative to work. It arrives in the doctor who assumes we would rather be dead, in the four-year waiting list for a wheelchair, in the family we can see getting tired. Canada wrote the argument into law: Track 2 is a provision available, by construction, only to people who would otherwise go on living, and 61.5 percent of the people who use it identify as disabled, while 23.2 percent had palliative care. We did not ask for a better assessment process. We asked why the state found it easier to offer us death than to offer us support, and the answer is the oldest one there is — it is cheaper, and our lives were never valued the same.
Best evidence: In January 2021 three UN human rights experts warned Canada against legislating on the basis that disability could make life not worth living, saying the reasoning would never be accepted for any other protected group. The UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, in concluding observations on Canada released on 26 March 2025, recommended repealing Track 2 and the scheduled 2027 mental illness expansion, finding the Truchon reasoning based on negative, ableist perceptions of the quality and value of disabled lives, and reserving its phrase “extreme concern” for Track 2 alone. Health Canada’s own data supply the numbers: 61.5 percent of Track 2 recipients self-identified as having a disability, Track 2 grew 17.1 percent in a year, and only 23.2 percent received palliative care. Oregon’s reported concerns are dominated by loss of autonomy, loss of enjoyable activities and loss of dignity, with 40 percent citing being a burden and pain trailing at 37 percent.
Answer to the strongest objection — that this denies disabled people the autonomy claimed for everyone else: We are not saying a disabled person cannot decide. We are saying you cannot measure a decision made inside conditions you created and then call the result a preference. The same society that would treat a non-disabled person’s wish to die as a crisis requiring intervention treats ours as a considered judgment requiring paperwork, and that asymmetry is the discrimination — it is in the response, not in the person. Offer us the supports first. If a state that funds real home care, real income security and real palliative access still meets people in our community who want to die, we will have a different conversation, with evidence we do not have now.

Camp: Decriminalize assistance without making it a medical service
Adherents: DIGNITAS — https://www.dignitas.ch/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=4&Itemid=44&lang=en ; the German Federal Constitutional Court, which struck down the ban on business-like assistance in February 2020 — https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/EN/2020/bvg20-012.html ; also EXIT Deutsche Schweiz, Sterbehilfe Deutschland, and legal scholars who read the European Court of Human Rights’ Haas judgment as recognizing a right to decide the manner and timing of one’s death. [Correction 2026-10-05: the German Federal Constitutional Court is not an adherent of this camp; its February 2020 judgment is evidence the camp cites, not a position it holds. The page lists the proponents as DIGNITAS, EXIT Deutsche Schweiz and Sterbehilfe Deutschland.]
The case in the camp’s own voice: The question everybody is arguing about — who should qualify — only exists because you decided to build a program. Switzerland never did. Article 115 of our penal code, unchanged since 1942, says that assisting a suicide is a crime only when the assister acts from selfish motives. That is all. There is no eligibility list, no six-month rule, no committee, no state register of the dying. The consequence is that nobody has to be certified as suffering enough to be allowed to go, and no doctor is asked to be a gatekeeper over a decision that was never medical in the first place. The German Constitutional Court reached the same conclusion from the other direction in 2020: the right to a self-determined death is part of the general right of personality, it belongs to every person in every phase of life, and it does not depend on a diagnosis or on anyone else’s assessment of the reasons. Where the medical model has been built instead, look what it produces: criteria that immediately come under pressure to widen, clinicians made responsible for a judgment no training prepares them for, and a debate about whether the state should pay for it. Decriminalize the help. Require that it be given without profit motive and that the person act themselves. Then stop legislating.
Best evidence: Switzerland has run on this rule for more than eighty years, with 1,774 assisted suicides among residents in 2024, and while the number has risen with the age structure of the population the legal framework has never had to be revised. The German Federal Constitutional Court’s judgment of 26 February 2020 struck down section 217 of the criminal code and stated that the right includes the freedom to seek and accept help from others, and the Bundestag has failed to agree a replacement in the six years since, leaving assistance lawful. The European Court of Human Rights in Haas v Switzerland accepted that an individual’s right to decide how and when to end their life falls within Article 8. Unassisted suicide in Switzerland has fallen by roughly 40 to 50 percent since the late 1990s, against the prediction that permissiveness would raise it.
Answer to the strongest objection — that a system with no gatekeeper cannot protect anyone: Our gate is a different one, and it is not obviously weaker. A person must obtain a prescription from a physician who is professionally accountable, must take the drug with their own hand, and the assister must be able to show no selfish motive, which is checked by police at every death and prosecuted when it fails. What we have not done is create an authority that decides whose reasons are good enough, because that authority is exactly what generates the drift the critics are worried about: once the state runs a list, the argument is permanently about who else belongs on it. We accept the fair criticism, which is about our visitors — people arrive from countries that have not settled this, at their own expense, which is a failure of their law rather than ours.

## Crux

Under every argument on this page sits one question: when a person who is suffering asks for help to die, how much of that request belongs to them and how much belongs to the conditions they are asking from. Nobody disputes that both are present. A dying person’s wish is shaped by their pain, and also by whether a nurse comes twice a day or twice a week, by whether their partner is exhausted, by whether the hospice bed exists, by what they think their family can afford, and by a lifetime of absorbing what a society thinks a life like theirs is worth. Supporters hold that the request is still the person’s, that we do not disqualify anyone else’s decisions for having a context, and that refusing to act on it leaves a real person in real pain in order to protect a hypothetical one. Opponents hold that a law converts those conditions into an option, that an option becomes an expectation quickly and quietly, and that a system which cannot reliably distinguish the two should not be authorized to act on either. Settle which of those is closer to true and most of the rest follows — the eligibility line, the safeguards, whether drift is a design flaw or a feature, whether a doctor should be involved at all.

What evidence could settle: A good deal of this is testable and some of the tests are running now. Whether requests fall when support rises is measurable: Canada records self-perceived burden, palliative care receipt and disability support receipt for every case, and a jurisdiction that materially expands home care or hospice provision would produce a natural experiment nobody has yet run cleanly. Whether the numbers stabilize is measurable and partly answered: Canadian growth has fallen from 36.8 percent a year to 6.9 percent, Dutch psychiatric cases fell 20 percent in 2025, and Oregon has sat near 1 percent of deaths after 28 years. Whether a narrow law widens is answerable by waiting: Oregon and the Australian states are now long enough running to be evidence, and Canada’s mental illness provision, due 17 March 2027 and recommended for indefinite pause by a parliamentary committee in June 2026, will test whether legislatures can stop. Whether assessment detects coerced or socially-produced requests is the hardest item, because current monitoring records the assessor’s conclusion rather than auditing it; it would need independent review of refused and accepted cases, interviews with families, and comparison with the deaths that happen anyway — the violent suicides, the flights to Zurich, the people who stop eating — which no jurisdiction systematically collects. Quebec’s unexplained regional spread, from the provincial average of 7.9 percent of deaths to 13.4 percent in one region, is the clearest open empirical puzzle on the table.

What no data can close: Even with perfect information, two questions remain choices. The first is what makes a decision authentically a person’s own. If someone would not want to die had they received the care they were owed, one person sees a preference that must be respected because it is the one they actually hold, and another sees a society taking credit for a decision it manufactured; no measurement adjudicates between those, because they disagree about what a choice is, not about what happened. The second is the exchange rate. Any workable law will occasionally assist someone who would have wanted to live, and any prohibition will certainly leave some people to die in a way they found unbearable. Deciding which of those errors a society should prefer, and whether the risk is fairly distributed when it falls mainly on people who are old, ill or disabled, is a moral judgment that better statistics sharpen but do not make. And for two of the camps here the crux is not a crux at all: on the view that a doctor must never deliberately cause a patient’s death, and on the view that a competent adult’s decision about their own life is simply not the state’s to license, the empirical question is interesting but not decisive, and saying so is not evasion — it is the position.
