# Government Debt — research

## Overview

Government debt is what a national government owes its lenders after years of spending more than it collects. This topic covers the public debt of rich democracies, above all the United States but also Japan, the United Kingdom, France, Italy, Germany and Canada, and the question of what actually limits how much such a government can borrow and spend. The candidate limits are rising interest bills that crowd out other spending, inflation, a loss of investor confidence that forces an abrupt correction, and the burden left to future taxpayers. The topic covers the main answers in the debate: stabilize the debt soon through a mix of spending cuts and tax increases; cut spending and rely on growth rather than higher taxes; put public investment and social programs ahead of debt reduction and fix the debt gradually; and the view of Modern Monetary Theory that a government issuing its own currency is limited by inflation and real resources, not by its debt. It deliberately excludes debt crises in developing countries and IMF lending to them, household and corporate debt, and the mechanics of the US debt ceiling except where they bear on sustainability. Taxing the very rich and the future of money and crypto have their own pages and appear here only where they bear on how a deficit is closed or financed.

Why now: on 23 September 2026 the yield on the 10-year US Treasury note closed at 5.11 percent, which CNN reported as its highest level since 2007 (CNN, https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/investing/us-bond-market-fed), and it closed at 5.29 percent on 30 September, the highest close since 2002 in the Treasury’s daily yield series (US Treasury, https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?type=daily_treasury_yield_curve&field_tdr_date_value=2026). The immediate drivers reported at the time were inflation from an oil shock tied to the US war with Iran, a strong economy, and the Federal Reserve’s first rate increase in more than three years, on 16 September (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/16/us-fed-raises-interest-rates-as-inflation-weighs-on-economy). The rise lands on a gross federal debt that crossed $40 trillion on 18 August 2026 (Treasury, Debt to the Penny, https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/debt-to-the-penny/) and on net interest costs that the Congressional Budget Office projects at about $1.04 trillion for fiscal 2026, more than the federal government spends on defense. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent responded on 19 August by at least doubling the Treasury’s buybacks of long-dated bonds and said “there’s nothing magic about the $40 trillion number” and “we can grow our way out of that” (Fortune, https://fortune.com/2026/08/20/scott-bessent-national-debt-40-trillion-nothing-magic/). The same weeks brought matching pressure elsewhere. Japan’s 10-year government bond yield closed at 3.09 percent on 1 October, its highest since August 1996 (Japan Ministry of Finance, https://www.mof.go.jp/english/policy/jgbs/reference/interest_rate/index.htm), after Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s tax-cut and spending plans unsettled the bond market. And on 1 October France’s government presented a 2027 budget that projects public debt rising to nearly 122 percent of GDP despite €54 billion in proposed savings, ahead of a 2027 presidential election in which the left-wing candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon proposes freezing the French debt held by the European Central Bank, an idea ECB President Christine Lagarde called a “pure violation” of the EU treaty (Associated Press via ABC News, https://abcnews.com/International/wireStory/frances-public-debt-economy-takes-center-stage-french-136911008).

The debate is live because the conditions that made high debt look cheap have reversed. Through the 2010s, interest rates sat below economic growth rates, and economists such as Olivier Blanchard argued that debt might then carry little fiscal cost; since 2022, inflation and higher rates have raised governments’ interest bills, and the IMF now projects global public debt to reach 100 percent of world GDP by 2029, a level previously reached only after World War II. Public concern has risen with it: in April 2026, 64 percent of Americans told Pew that the federal budget deficit is a very big problem, up from 57 percent in early 2025, with concern among Democrats jumping once a Republican administration was in charge. The participants are finance ministries and treasuries, central banks, the IMF and OECD, independent fiscal watchdogs such as the US Congressional Budget Office and the UK Office for Budget Responsibility, credit rating agencies and bond investors, budget-focused advocacy groups such as the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, free-market think tanks such as the Cato Institute, progressive think tanks such as the Roosevelt Institute, academic economists, and the small but vocal school of Modern Monetary Theory around Stephanie Kelton and the Levy Economics Institute.

The 1946 peak came from financing World War II; the current level was reached in peacetime with low unemployment.

Note: A widely cited 2010 study reported that rich countries with debt above 90 percent of GDP averaged slightly negative growth; a 2013 replication found coding errors and other choices and put the average at 2.2 percent.
Context: Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff’s 2010 paper “Growth in a Time of Debt” was cited by policymakers in the United States and Europe during the austerity debates of the early 2010s as evidence of a 90 percent danger threshold. Thomas Herndon, a graduate student, with Michael Ash and Robert Pollin, found a spreadsheet error, the exclusion of some available data and an unusual weighting method; correcting them raised average growth above 90 percent debt from minus 0.1 to 2.2 percent. Reinhart and Rogoff acknowledged the spreadsheet error but maintained that the corrected data still show slower growth at high debt levels, and the corrected figures do show high-debt countries growing more slowly than lower-debt ones. The open question, still argued in the literature, is direction: whether high debt slows growth, or slow growth produces high debt.

## Questions

Question: Can a rich country that borrows in its own currency face a debt crisis?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Crisis is possible / Crisis is implausible
Why contested: The two sides draw on the same history and read it differently. There is no agreed debt ratio at which trouble begins: Ireland hit a crisis near 90 percent of GDP, Greece near 150 percent, and Japan has had none above 200 percent. Both Ireland and Greece were euro members, which leaves open whether a country with its own currency and central bank is exposed to anything similar.
Case for “Crisis is possible”: Those who take the risk seriously argue that a crisis for a currency issuer would not look like Greece’s; it would look like the United Kingdom in September 2022, when an unfunded tax-cut plan sent long-term borrowing costs up 160 basis points in days and forced the central bank to intervene, or like a slow squeeze in which investors demand ever higher yields to hold a growing pile of long-term debt. A government can always print money to avoid default, they say, but doing so on a large scale trades a debt crisis for an inflation or currency crisis. They point to US 10-year yields above 5 percent in September 2026, Japan’s highest yields since 1996, and the IMF’s warning that its debt-at-risk measure is elevated, and argue that waiting until the market forces action means adjusting under the worst conditions.
Case for “Crisis is implausible”: Those who doubt the danger argue that a government that owes money in a currency it issues cannot be forced into default, and that its central bank can always act as a buyer of last resort, as the Bank of England did within days in 2022 and the Bank of Japan has done for a decade. Paul Krugman, for one, says he has found no historical example of a Greek-style crisis in a country borrowing in its own currency. On this view, rising yields reflect inflation and central bank policy, not fear of default, and market measures of default risk remain low. The real costs of high debt, they argue, are gradual: higher interest bills and perhaps some crowding out of private investment, which call for steady management rather than emergency austerity.

Question: Will interest rates stay below economic growth rates?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Rates stay below growth / Rates now exceed growth
Why contested: When the interest rate a government pays is below the growth rate of the economy, debt can shrink as a share of GDP even while the government runs modest primary deficits; when it is above, debt compounds faster than the economy. Rates were below growth for most of the past several decades, but have risen sharply since 2022, and no one can forecast the gap with confidence.
Case for “Rates stay below growth”: Olivier Blanchard showed in his 2019 presidential address to the American Economic Association that safe interest rates below growth rates have been “more the historical norm than the exception” in the United States, and the forces that held rates down, such as aging populations, high saving in Asia, and strong global demand for safe dollar assets, have not gone away. The IMF’s April 2026 Fiscal Monitor still describes the global interest-growth differential as favorable. On this reading, the current rise in yields reflects an inflation shock and a central bank tightening cycle, and the average rate on the debt, about 3.5 percent in August 2026, remains below nominal growth.
Case for “Rates now exceed growth”: Those who expect a lasting change argue that the era of near-zero rates was the exception, created by the financial crisis and central bank bond buying, and that heavy government borrowing worldwide, the end of central bank purchases, higher defense and energy spending, and persistent inflation will keep real rates higher. The IMF notes that the favorable margin “is narrow given countries’ relatively high interest burdens,” and CBO projects interest costs rising from 3.3 to 4.6 percent of GDP over the decade even under its current assumptions. A US 10-year yield above 5 percent, if it persists, would push the average rate on the debt up for years as old low-rate bonds mature.

Question: Should debt be stabilized mainly by cutting spending or by raising taxes?
Tag: value
Poles: Mainly spending cuts / Mainly tax increases
Why contested: The arithmetic can be closed either way or with any mix; the choice depends on how large a government people want, who should bear the cost, and on disputed evidence about which approach harms growth less. In the United States, CBO projects revenue at 17.5 percent of GDP in 2026, slightly above its 50-year average, and spending at 23.3 percent, two points above its average; each side reads that gap as support for its own answer.
Case for “Mainly spending cuts”: Those who favor spending cuts argue that the debt is rising because spending, especially on Social Security, Medicare and interest, is growing faster than the economy while revenue is near its historical norm, so the fix should address the cause. They cite research by Alberto Alesina, Carlo Favero and Francesco Giavazzi finding that consolidations based on spending cuts have done much less damage to growth than those based on tax increases, and argue that higher taxes on work and investment would shrink the economy that has to carry the debt. They add that tax increases tend to be spent rather than used to reduce deficits.
Case for “Mainly tax increases”: Those who favor more revenue argue that US tax revenue is low by rich-country standards, that repeated tax cuts since 2001, including the 2025 reconciliation act, account for much of the rise in debt, and that the largest spending programs are commitments to retirees that most voters of every party want kept. Raising revenue from high earners and corporations, they argue, can close much of the gap with little harm to growth and without cutting benefits for people who planned their retirement around them. They note that most other rich democracies collect considerably more tax as a share of GDP than the United States and use it to pay for pensions and health care.

Question: Should promised retirement and health benefits be trimmed to stabilize the debt?
Tag: value
Poles: Trim promised benefits / Keep and fund benefits
Why contested: Social Security and Medicare are the largest drivers of projected US spending growth, and Social Security’s retirement trust fund is projected to run out in late 2032, after which only 78 percent of scheduled benefits could be paid. Any long-run plan has to say whether the gap is closed with benefit changes, new revenue or both, and that choice divides generations as well as parties.
Case for “Trim promised benefits”: Those who favor benefit changes argue that the programs were designed for a younger population, that benefits for future retirees are scheduled to grow faster than wages, and that closing the gap entirely with payroll taxes would mean a large tax increase on younger workers to fund older and, on average, wealthier households. [Correction 2026-10-05: scheduled Social Security benefits grow faster than prices, not wages; it is Medicare costs per person that outgrow wages. The page states this as the camp’s argument, without a separate source.] Gradual changes now, such as raising the retirement age for people still decades from it, slowing benefit growth for higher earners, or competition in Medicare, would protect the most vulnerable while avoiding an abrupt 22 percent cut when the trust fund runs dry.
Case for “Keep and fund benefits”: Those who would keep scheduled benefits argue that they are earned through a lifetime of payroll taxes, that most retirees depend on Social Security for most of their income, and that raising the retirement age falls hardest on people in physical jobs with shorter life expectancies. The shortfall, about 1.5 percent of GDP over 75 years, can be closed with revenue, for instance by applying the payroll tax to earnings above the current cap. A rich and aging society, they argue, should expect to spend more on its elderly, and should pay for it rather than treat it as a debt problem.

Question: Is inflation, rather than debt, the real limit on government spending?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Inflation is the limit / Debt itself constrains
Why contested: This is the central claim of Modern Monetary Theory. Mainstream economists accept part of it, that a currency issuer cannot be forced to default, but reject the conclusion that debt levels and interest costs therefore do not matter. The 2021 to 2023 inflation, which followed heavy pandemic borrowing and supply shocks at the same time, is read by both sides as support.
Case for “Inflation is the limit”: Economists in the Modern Monetary Theory tradition, such as Stephanie Kelton and L. Randall Wray, argue that a government issuing its own currency can always pay bills in that currency, so the only real constraint is whether the economy has the workers, materials and capacity to supply what the government buys without driving up prices. Deficits, on this view, add to private sector savings, and interest rates are set by the central bank, not by the market. Policy should therefore target full employment and stable prices directly, using taxes to cool demand when needed, and stop treating a debt ratio as a goal in itself. They read the post-2021 inflation as confirmation that inflation is the constraint to watch, and attribute much of it to supply shocks.
Case for “Debt itself constrains”: Most economists, including those who favor more public spending, argue that debt constrains policy through channels that inflation alone does not capture. Interest costs must be paid and grow with the debt, which crowds out other spending or requires higher taxes. If a central bank has to hold rates down or buy bonds to keep the government funded, it gives up its independence to fight inflation, a situation economists call fiscal dominance. And once investors start to doubt a government’s commitment to stable money, inflation can arrive faster than taxes can be raised to stop it. In a 2019 University of Chicago survey of 38 leading US economists, none agreed that countries borrowing in their own currency “should not worry about government deficits.”

Question: Does cutting deficits hurt the economy more than it helps?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Cuts backfire / Cuts pay off
Why contested: The evidence depends heavily on circumstances: how deep the slack in the economy is, whether the central bank can cut rates to offset, whether cuts come from spending or taxes, and whether the debt was frightening investors in the first place. The European austerity of 2010 to 2013 produced research on both sides.
Case for “Cuts backfire”: Those who warn against austerity cite Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh’s 2013 IMF study, which found that forecasters had badly underestimated how much fiscal tightening would shrink European economies early in the crisis, implying that fiscal multipliers were substantially larger than assumed. When cuts shrink GDP, the debt ratio can rise rather than fall, as it did in several euro countries. They argue that cutting deficits makes sense only when the economy is at full employment and the central bank has room to offset.
Case for “Cuts pay off”: Those who favor consolidation cite research by Alberto Alesina and colleagues finding that well-designed, spending-based consolidations have often been followed by mild slowdowns or even growth, as lower borrowing reduced interest rates and raised business confidence, with examples such as Canada in the 1990s and Ireland and Denmark in the 1980s. They note that the United States in 2026 is at low unemployment with inflation above target, exactly the conditions under which even critics of austerity accept that deficit reduction is appropriate, and that higher deficits now add to the inflation the Federal Reserve is fighting.

Question: Should governments borrow more to invest in infrastructure, defense and the energy transition while debt is high?
Tag: value
Poles: Borrow to invest / Fund from current budgets
Why contested: Public investment can raise future output and pay back part of its cost, but estimates of returns vary widely by project and country, and many argue that “investment” labels are stretched to cover current spending. Europe has made the choice concrete: in March 2025 Germany amended its constitution to exempt defense spending above 1 percent of GDP from its debt brake and create a €500 billion infrastructure fund outside it (European Commission, https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/economic-forecast-and-surveys/economic-forecasts/spring-2025-economic-forecast-moderate-growth-amid-global-economic-uncertainty/potential-economic-impact-reform-germanys-fiscal-framework_en).
Case for “Borrow to invest”: Those who favor borrowing for investment argue that it is how households and firms finance long-lived assets, and that a government should do the same: debt that builds roads, power grids, research capacity or military deterrence leaves future taxpayers with assets as well as liabilities. Underinvestment has its own costs, from failing infrastructure to lost competitiveness and security. Fiscal rules that treat all borrowing alike, they argue, push governments to cut investment first because it is the easiest to delay.
Case for “Fund from current budgets”: Those who resist new borrowing argue that the distinction between investment and consumption is easily gamed, that the returns promised by politicians often fail to materialize, and that a government already carrying high debt and rising interest costs should fund new priorities by cutting lower-value spending or raising taxes. Exemptions for favored categories, on this view, erode the credibility of fiscal rules and simply add to debt that future taxpayers must service whatever the money was spent on.

Question: Should central banks stand behind government debt markets?
Tag: value
Poles: Backstop the market / Keep central banks out
Why contested: Central banks bought government bonds on a vast scale after 2008 and in 2020, and they intervened in specific crises such as the UK’s in 2022. Whether such support should be routine, and whether it compromises their independence, is disputed, especially now that inflation is above target and governments are under pressure to keep borrowing cheap.
Case for “Backstop the market”: Supporters of a backstop argue that the market for government debt is the foundation of the financial system, that self-fulfilling panics can push a solvent government into crisis, and that a central bank willing to act as buyer of last resort prevents that, as the European Central Bank’s 2012 pledge and the Bank of England’s 2022 intervention showed. Modern Monetary Theory economists go further and argue that the central bank and treasury are, in practice, one government sector whose coordination is normal.
Case for “Keep central banks out”: Opponents argue that a central bank that routinely finances its government loses the ability to fight inflation, because raising rates would raise the government’s costs, and that investors who expect to be rescued stop disciplining governments. They point to the European Union’s treaty ban on central bank financing of governments, which ECB President Christine Lagarde invoked in September 2026 against a French proposal to cancel ECB-held debt, and to political pressure on the Federal Reserve from a president demanding interest rates of “1%, or less” while the Fed was raising them to fight inflation.

## Positions

Camp: Stabilize the debt soon with both spending cuts and tax increases
Adherents:
- Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (institution), statement by its president Maya MacGuineas when gross debt reached $40 trillion, 19 August 2026, calling for “No New Borrowing,” a 3 percent of GDP deficit target and a bipartisan fiscal commission to fix the Social Security and Medicare trust funds: https://www.crfb.org/press-releases/gross-national-debt-reaches-40-trillion
- Ray Dalio, founder of Bridgewater Associates and author of How Countries Go Broke (2025), proposing his “3% 3-part solution”: cut the deficit to 3 percent of GDP through spending cuts, tax revenue increases and lower interest rates together: https://raydalio.substack.com/p/how-countries-go-broke-in-a-5-minute
Case: The United States is borrowing close to 6 percent of GDP a year at full employment and in peacetime, something it has never done before, and the bill is now arriving. Interest already costs more than the entire defense budget and is on track to double within a decade; every dollar of it is a dollar not spent on schools, troops or tax relief. Debt held by the public is about the size of the whole economy and, under current law, heads toward 175 percent. No one can say on which day lenders will balk, but the warning signs are visible: 10-year yields above 5 percent, a credit downgrade from every major agency, and a bond market that needed Treasury buybacks to steady it. A country that waits for a crisis to act ends up adjusting all at once, under duress, in the middle of a recession. A country that acts now can phase changes in gently, protect the most vulnerable and let people plan. The gap is too large to close with any single tool. Cutting only discretionary spending cannot do it; cutting only Social Security and Medicare would break promises voters will not let break; raising only taxes would require rates that slow growth. A durable fix needs both sides of the ledger, agreed across parties so that it survives the next election, and most likely negotiated through a fiscal commission with a clear target such as a 3 percent deficit.
Best evidence: CBO’s February 2026 projections of debt rising from 101 to 120 percent of GDP by 2036 and 175 percent by 2056, with net interest exceeding defense; the IMF’s projection that US gross debt rises to about 142 percent of GDP by 2031 with deficits of 7 to 8 percent of GDP that would be “unprecedented in peacetime”; Social Security’s retirement trust fund running out in late 2032; Moody’s May 2025 downgrade; and the UK’s 2022 gilt crisis as proof that markets can punish even a country with its own currency and central bank.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a currency issuer cannot have a Greek-style crisis and that past warnings of one, around 2010 to 2013, proved wrong. This camp answers that it is not predicting a default. The danger is a slow squeeze of rising interest costs that crowds out everything else, punctuated by sudden market stress like the UK’s in 2022, and the fact that low rates rescued earlier forecasts is no guarantee they will again: long-term rates are at their highest since the early 2000s, and net interest, which averaged 2.1 percent of GDP over the past 50 years, is already 3.3 percent and rising. To the objection that cutting deficits hurts growth, it answers that the case against austerity applies in a slump, and the US economy in 2026 is at low unemployment with inflation above target, exactly when deficit reduction does the least harm.
Variation: Members of this camp differ on speed and mix. CRFB favors legislated targets and a commission; Dalio stresses that lower interest rates, earned through credible deficit reduction, would do much of the work. Some economists, such as Jason Furman and Lawrence Summers in their 2020 paper on fiscal policy at low rates, proposed judging debt by real interest costs rather than the debt ratio, a standard that higher rates have since made more pressing. Outside the United States, the same view underlies the European Union’s fiscal rules and the UK’s fiscal targets, and it is broadly the stance of the IMF’s Fiscal Monitor.

Camp: Cut spending and grow the economy instead of raising taxes
Adherents:
- Cato Institute (institution), policy analysis by Ryan Bourne, “A Case for Federal Deficit Reduction: Spending Cuts to Avoid a Fiscal Crisis” (Policy Analysis No. 973, April 2024): https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/case-federal-deficit-reduction-spending-cuts-avoid-fiscal-crisis ; and testimony by Cato’s director of budget and entitlement policy, Romina Boccia, to the Joint Economic Committee, 12 March 2024, arguing that spending, above all on Medicare and Social Security, drives the debt and that “spending-based deficit reduction, especially targeted at social and entitlement programs, is most effective” (her testimony notes that the views are her own): https://www.jec.senate.gov/public/_cache/files/da3f4764-25b6-428d-9eda-cd27e2f33c8c/boccia-testimony-3.12.pdf
- Scott Bessent, US Treasury Secretary, on 20 August 2026: “There’s nothing magic about the $40 trillion number. And we can grow our way out of that,” arguing for spending restraint and faster growth rather than tax increases: https://fortune.com/2026/08/20/scott-bessent-national-debt-40-trillion-nothing-magic/
Case: The debt is a spending problem. Federal revenue is at or above its 50-year average as a share of the economy; spending is two points of GDP above its average and climbing, driven by entitlement programs that grow automatically and by the interest those deficits generate. Raising taxes would treat the symptom while weakening the cure: higher taxes on work, saving and investment shrink the economy whose growth is the most painless way to bring the debt ratio down, and the history of budget deals is that tax increases are spent while promised spending cuts evaporate. The countries that fixed their finances, from Canada in the 1990s to Ireland and Denmark in the 1980s, did it mostly by restraining spending, and their economies grew through the adjustment. The path forward is to slow the growth of Social Security and Medicare for future retirees, cap and cut lower-value discretionary programs, root out waste and improper payments, and pair that restraint with deregulation, cheaper energy and pro-investment tax policy so that the economy outgrows its debt.
Best evidence: CBO’s projections of revenue at 17.5 percent of GDP in 2026 against a 50-year average of 17.3, and outlays at 23.3 percent against 21.2; research by Alberto Alesina, Carlo Favero and Francesco Giavazzi (Austerity: When It Works and When It Doesn’t, Princeton University Press, 2019) finding that spending-based consolidations are far less costly to output than tax-based ones; and the late 1990s, when federal spending fell as a share of GDP and strong growth produced budget surpluses.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the debt rose because of tax cuts, most recently the 2025 reconciliation act, which CBO scored as adding $4.7 trillion to deficits, so a camp that rules out tax increases is not serious about debt. This camp answers that revenue has recovered to its historical average even after those cuts, while spending is well above its own, so the long-run gap comes from the spending side; extending lower tax rates prevented a tax increase that would have slowed growth. Within the camp there is a frank disagreement about growth: Bessent argues that 3 percent growth plus restraint can stabilize the debt, while Boccia warns that “economic growth won’t get us out of the spending-driven debt crisis” without entitlement reform. Both reject tax increases as the main tool.
Variation: Libertarian economists at Cato emphasize entitlement reform and oppose tariffs as a hidden tax, while the Trump administration has relied on tariffs for revenue, used buybacks to steady the bond market, and pledged not to cut Social Security benefits. Some conservatives support a balanced budget amendment; others, including Boccia, prefer a commission modeled on the military base-closing process.

Camp: Put public investment and social programs ahead of debt reduction
Adherents:
- Roosevelt Institute (institution), brief by David Stein, “The Deficit-Hawk Takeover: How Austerity Politics Constrained Democratic Policymaking” (September 2024), arguing that a focus on deficits constrained social spending and that progressive policymakers “need a new approach” to managing deficits: https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/the-deficit-hawk-takeover/
- Paul Krugman, Nobel laureate economist, “The Case Against Bond Market Panic” (20 August 2026), arguing that rising rates are “a real problem” but that there is no evidence of a looming Greek-type debt crisis and that a future Democratic administration should not let debt warnings block its agenda: https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/the-case-against-bond-market-panic
Case: The debt deserves attention, but it is not an emergency, and treating it as one has done more harm than the debt itself. The United States borrows in the currency it issues, so it cannot be forced into a Greek-style default; the warnings of an imminent crisis issued around 2010 were wrong, and the austerity they justified slowed the recovery for years and was later found to have cost far more output than its advocates predicted. The real costs of debt are higher interest bills and, at full employment, some pressure on private investment; they are reasons to manage the debt steadily, not to cut the programs that build the future or protect the vulnerable. Much of the recent rise in debt comes from tax cuts that went mostly to the wealthy, so the fair and effective fix is to raise revenue from those who gained most, not to cut Social Security, Medicare or public investment. Underinvesting in infrastructure, research, child care and the energy transition leaves a poorer and less productive country, which makes any debt harder to carry.
Best evidence: Olivier Blanchard’s finding that interest rates below growth rates have been the historical norm in the United States, which means debt can be rolled over at little fiscal cost; Blanchard and Leigh’s 2013 IMF finding that austerity multipliers were substantially underestimated in Europe; the correction of the Reinhart and Rogoff 90 percent threshold; Japan carrying debt above 200 percent of GDP without a crisis; and market measures, cited by Krugman, showing little investor fear of US default or deliberate inflation even as yields rise.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the low-rate world on which this case was built has ended: yields above 5 percent and interest costs above $1 trillion mean debt is no longer cheap. This camp answers that higher rates are a real cost but not a crisis, and that the rise in 2026 is driven largely by inflation from an oil shock and a central bank tightening cycle rather than by fear of default. Higher interest costs strengthen the case for raising revenue from top earners and corporations, not for cutting programs. Krugman acknowledges the higher rates are “a real problem”; the dispute is over what follows from it.
Variation: Members differ on how much deficit reduction they would accept and when. Some, like the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, favor reducing deficits gradually through progressive tax increases while protecting programs; others, like the Groundwork Collaborative, emphasize that domestic discretionary spending is already historically low. In Europe, the parallel view supports Germany’s 2025 reform of its debt brake to finance infrastructure and defense, Mario Draghi’s 2024 call for large-scale, partly joint EU investment, and loosening EU fiscal rules for investment.

Camp: Budget for full employment and stable prices, not for a debt target
Adherents:
- Levy Economics Institute of Bard College (institution), Working Paper No. 996, Éric Tymoigne, “Seven Replies to the Critiques of Modern Money Theory” (December 2021): “when monetary sovereignty prevails, the fiscal position and the public debt are poor metrics for judging the viability of a public purpose and its pace of implementation”: https://www.levyinstitute.org/publications/seven-replies-to-the-critiques-of-modern-money-theory/
- Stephanie Kelton, Stony Brook University economist and author of The Deficit Myth (2020), “The Deficit Myth Turns 5” (June 2025), declining to join “the chorus of Wall Street worrywarts” warning of a debt crisis and calling for a debate “less focused on the size of the so-called ‘national debt’ and more focused on inflation risk”: https://stephaniekelton.substack.com/p/the-deficit-myth-turns-5
Case: The question “how much debt can we afford?” is the wrong question for a country that issues its own currency. The US government spends dollars into existence and cannot run out of them; its bonds are an interest-bearing alternative to holding dollars, and the interest rate on them is ultimately a policy choice of the central bank. What a government can run out of is real resources: workers, materials, energy and productive capacity. Spend beyond what the economy can supply and prices rise; that is the true limit, and it is a serious one. So fiscal policy should be judged by its effects on employment, inflation and the things it builds, not by whether a debt ratio goes up or down. A deficit is not a sign of failure; it is the counterpart of the private sector’s savings, and a budget that balances while millions are unemployed is the real waste. When inflation threatens, the remedy is to target the specific bottleneck or to use taxes to withdraw spending power, not to panic about the debt. A job guarantee would provide a standing buffer that expands in slumps and shrinks in booms.
Best evidence: No country that borrows only in its own floating currency has been forced into involuntary default on that debt; Japan has run large deficits for three decades with debt above 200 percent of GDP while battling deflation; central banks bought trillions of dollars of government bonds after 2008 and in 2020 without the runaway inflation critics predicted at the time; and the 2021 to 2023 inflation, in this camp’s reading, came mainly from supply shocks and receded as supply chains healed, without the mass unemployment many forecast was needed.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the 2021 to 2023 inflation proved the camp wrong: big deficits produced the worst inflation in 40 years. This camp answers that it has always said inflation is the binding constraint and that pandemic spending was designed without regard to it; much of the inflation came from supply shocks and energy prices, and the lesson is to manage real resources better, not to target debt. To the objection that rising interest costs are a burden, Warren Mosler, in an exchange on Japan that Kelton published in January 2026, argues that higher rates are a choice made by the central bank and that interest payments add income to the private sector, so rate increases can themselves feed inflation (https://stephaniekelton.substack.com/p/warren-mosler-to-robin-brooks-on). The camp accepts that its framework does not apply to countries that borrow in a currency they do not issue, such as euro area members, whose constraints it regards as real.
Variation: Modern Monetary Theory economists differ on how to manage inflation in practice, with some favoring targeted measures and others relying on a job guarantee as an automatic stabilizer. A distinct European proposal, sometimes confused with this camp, would cancel or freeze government bonds held by the European Central Bank; it was urged in a February 2021 open letter signed by about 150 European economists and politicians, including Thomas Piketty (Al Arabiya, https://english.alarabiya.net/business/economy/2021/02/05/European-economists-publish-letter-urging-debt-cancellation), and in 2026 by the French presidential candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon, and rests on the separate claim that central-bank-held debt is costless to cancel.

## Crux

The question under the others is how much danger rising debt poses to a rich country that borrows in its own currency, and how soon: whether the debt now on course in the United States, Japan, the United Kingdom and France risks forcing a sudden and painful adjustment through spiking interest costs, inflation or a loss of investor confidence, or whether its costs will stay gradual enough to manage over decades.

What evidence could settle: Part of this is an empirical question about how interest rates, growth and inflation respond to debt. Evidence bears on it from several directions: whether the gap between interest rates and growth rates stays favorable as debt rises, or whether investors demand a growing premium to hold long-term government bonds; how much of the 2026 rise in yields reflects inflation and central bank policy rather than the supply of debt; how strongly deficits at full employment feed inflation; how large the economic cost of cutting deficits is in different conditions; and how countries such as Japan and the United Kingdom fare as central banks shrink their bond holdings. Long-run fiscal projections from CBO, the OBR and the IMF, and the behavior of bond markets over the next few years, will narrow this, though no data can say precisely when confidence would break.

What is a value choice: The rest is a choice no data can close. Even with the risk known, people differ on how much present benefit, in public investment, retirement and health benefits, or lower taxes, is worth trading for a smaller chance of a future crisis; on whether the cost of adjustment should fall on today’s taxpayers, today’s retirees or tomorrow’s workers; on how large a government they want; and on whether a debt ratio deserves to be a goal of policy at all, or only a by-product to be watched while policy aims at employment and stable prices.
