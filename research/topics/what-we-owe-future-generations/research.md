# What We Owe the Future — research

## Overview

This topic is about what people alive now owe to people who are not yet born: how much their interests should count in today’s decisions, what form that obligation takes, and what it implies for priorities. It covers three linked disputes. The first is philosophical: whether a person’s well-being matters less because they will live later, whether a future with more good lives in it is better than one with fewer, and whether people who do not yet exist can have rights at all. The second is technical but carries those philosophical choices inside it: the discount rate that governments and economists use to compare costs paid now with benefits that arrive decades or centuries later, which decides most of what a cost-benefit analysis says about climate policy. The third is longtermism, the view associated with the philosophers William MacAskill and Toby Ord that because the future could contain vastly more people than the present, reducing the risk of human extinction or permanent collapse is among the most important things anyone can do — and the backlash to it. The topic also covers the institutions built to represent future generations: commissioners, constitutional clauses, declarations and court doctrines of intergenerational equity. It deliberately excludes the substance of the individual risks (whether AI, engineered pandemics or nuclear war are as dangerous as claimed belongs to other topics), the details of climate policy and fossil fuel phaseout, whether population size itself should grow or shrink, the case for effective altruism as a philanthropic movement, and government debt as an intergenerational burden; each of those is its own page. Numbers about risks and climate appear here only to show what rides on the question of how much the future counts.

Why now: on 5 March 2026 the European Commission adopted its first Strategy on Intergenerational Fairness, setting out to “further develop the reflex of long-term thinking in EU policymaking” with an Intergenerational Fairness Index, foresight tools and a “youth check” on new policy, and promising a progress report in early 2028 to feed the United Nations’ future-generations process (European Commission press release IP/26/535, https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_535). The same year showed how unsettled the commitment is elsewhere. On 22 January 2026 the Scottish Parliament rejected the Wellbeing and Sustainable Development (Scotland) Bill, which would have created a Future Generations Commissioner for Scotland, by 91 votes to 25 at Stage 1 (Scottish Parliament bill page, https://www.parliament.scot/bills-and-laws/bills/s6/wellbeing-and-sustainable-development-scotland-bill). The UN Special Envoy for Future Generations promised in the 2024 Declaration on Future Generations was left out of the UN’s 2026 budget after a member state–led UN budgetary committee rejected the Secretary-General’s proposal for the post in June 2025 (Adam Day, IPI Global Observatory, October 2025, https://theglobalobservatory.org/2025/10/how-a-special-envoy-for-future-generations-could-help-rescue-un80-from-itself/). And in the United States, Executive Order 14154 in January 2025 disbanded the interagency group that set the federal social cost of carbon, Executive Order 14192 of 31 January 2025 directed the Office of Management and Budget to revoke the 2023 guidance that had lowered the government’s default discount rate to 2 percent and to reinstate the 2003 version with its 3 and 7 percent rates, and an Office of Management and Budget memorandum of 5 May 2025 told agencies to stop monetizing climate damages except where a statute requires it (Harvard Environmental & Energy Law Program tracker, https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/the-social-cost-of-carbon/; Executive Order 14192, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/02/06/2025-02345/unleashing-prosperity-through-deregulation). Behind these sits the International Court of Justice’s unanimous advisory opinion of 23 July 2025 on climate change, which held that intergenerational equity is “a guide for the interpretation of applicable rules” and that “due regard for the interests of future generations and the long-term implications of conduct” must be taken into account when states carry out their climate obligations (paragraph 157, https://www.icj-cij.org/case/187).

The debate is live because three things moved at once. Governments are building, funding or abandoning institutions meant to speak for the unborn, and they are going in opposite directions. The number that most climate cost-benefit analysis turns on, the discount rate, swung in the United States from 7 and 3 percent to 2 percent in November 2023 and back again in early 2025, while the government’s estimate of the harm from a ton of carbon dioxide went from $51 to $190 and then to no figure at all. And longtermism went from an Oxford seminar idea to a movement with large philanthropic money behind it, much of it now directed at AI risk, then drew a sharp backlash after the November 2022 collapse of the FTX exchange, whose founder had pledged large sums to longtermist causes. The participants are moral philosophers (Derek Parfit’s work on future people underlies most of the modern debate), climate economists divided since the 2006 Stern Review over how to discount, treasury and budget officials who set official discount rates, the effective altruism and existential-risk research community, youth climate litigants and their lawyers, religious leaders, commissioners for future generations in Wales and elsewhere, and critics who see longtermism as an ideology that diverts attention from harms happening now.

Context: The social cost of carbon is the present value of all the future damage caused by emitting one more ton of carbon dioxide; it is the number cost-benefit analysis uses to decide how much cutting emissions is worth. Because most of the damage arrives decades to centuries after the emission, the discount rate used to convert future harm into today’s dollars dominates the answer. This table comes from DICE, Nordhaus’s long-standing climate-economy model, rerun with constant discount rates from 1 to 5 percent. In the model’s standard run, which uses a near-term real return of 4.5 percent a year declining over time, the figure is $66 per ton; in the run that maximizes net benefits it is $50, and if warming is held below 2°C it is about $75. The authors write that the assumptions about discounting “are highly controversial and have major implications for the SCC and for policies,” and that estimates from different models are “quite close conditional on the discount rate.” The 2°C target passes their cost-benefit test only with low discounting (2 percent) or a more pessimistic damage function. That is why disputes about what we owe the future so often become disputes about one parameter.

Note: At a 3 percent discount rate, $1,000 of benefit to people living 200 years from now is worth $2.71 today; at 4 percent, 39 cents.
Context: Discounting is compound interest run backward. At ordinary market-like rates, anything that happens to people more than a century or two away shrinks to almost nothing in present-value terms, which is the arithmetic behind the claim that standard cost-benefit analysis cannot see the far future. The explainer distinguishes two ways of choosing a rate. The “prescriptive” approach sets it from ethical judgments about how to weigh the well-being of different generations; the “descriptive” approach reads it from observed market interest rates. The explainer calls the choice between them “a contentious issue, with different analysts having differing opinions about which is best.” Those favoring the descriptive approach answer that a low rate does not help future people if it steers money away from investments that would have left them richer.

Context: Every major government discounts, but they disagree about how much, and the gap matters most for anything with effects spanning generations. The UK Treasury’s rate is a “social time preference rate” built from how much people prefer present to future consumption, and it declines for distant years to reflect growing uncertainty; health and life effects get a lower 1.5 percent because the “wealth effect” (future people being richer) is excluded for them. The US 7 percent figure is, in the words of the 2003 guidance, “an estimate of the average before-tax rate of return to private capital in the U.S. economy,” used as the base case, with 3 percent as a consumption rate (https://www.reginfo.gov/public/jsp/EO/fedRegReview/CircularA4.pdf). That 2003 guidance also notes that “some believe … that it is ethically impermissible to discount the utility of future generations” and allows a further sensitivity analysis at a lower rate for intergenerational effects. The 2023 revision set a single default of 2.0 percent (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/11/13/2023-24819/issuance-of-revised-omb-circular-no-a-4-regulatory-analysis); Executive Order 14192 of 31 January 2025 directed its revocation and the reinstatement of the 2003 version (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/02/06/2025-02345/unleashing-prosperity-through-deregulation). Rows come from different documents and years, as labeled.

Source label: Moritz Drupp, Mark Freeman, Ben Groom and Frikk Nesje, “Discounting Disentangled,” American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 2018 (overall rate, from the abstract); pure time preference figures as reported in US EPA, Report on the Social Cost of Greenhouse Gases, 2023, section 2.4
Source URL: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20160240
Note: Surveyed experts on discounting put the pure rate of time preference — how much less a future person’s well-being should count simply because it comes later — at a median of 0.5 percent a year, and the most common answer was zero.
Context: A social discount rate bundles two different things. One is pure time preference: treating well-being as less important just because it occurs later. The other is the expectation that future people will be richer, so an extra dollar will matter less to them. Many moral philosophers, following Henry Sidgwick and Frank Ramsey, hold that pure time preference toward future generations is indefensible, and the survey suggests most economists give it little weight: the EPA report gives the experts’ median as 0.5 percent, mean 1.1 percent and mode zero. The same survey of more than 200 experts found “a surprising degree of consensus,” with “more than three-quarters finding the median risk-free SDR of 2 percent acceptable.” For comparison, the 2006 Stern Review used a pure rate of time preference of 0.1 percent, justified by the small chance that humanity does not survive (EPA report, footnote 127). Disagreement about the overall rate therefore turns less on whether the future counts than on how much richer it will be and what returns investment can earn in the meantime.

Note: The probabilistic projections used in the US government’s 2023 climate-damage estimates have average global economic growth per person of about 1.6 percent a year from 2030 to 2100, before climate damages are subtracted.
Context: The main economic reason for discounting the future is that people later will probably be richer than people now. The projections in the EPA report start at a mean growth rate of 1.4 percent in 2021, are “relatively flat between 2030 and 2100 at 1.6%,” then decline, leveling off around 1.1 percent after 2200; they are lower than the IPCC’s older scenarios, which assumed near-term growth near 3 percent. Sustained for 70 years, 1.6 percent growth would roughly triple average income. Those who put the present first take this as the decisive fact: a transfer from today’s poorer people to tomorrow’s richer ones needs a strong reason. Those who put more weight on the future answer that averages hide the cases that matter — the low-growth or catastrophic paths in which future people are not richer — and that the projections are uncertain over such spans.

Context: The longtermist argument rests on this asymmetry. Roser’s baseline assumes 11 billion people living to 88, a stable population and a species lifespan of about a million years, the rough average for mammals; under those assumptions future births outnumber everyone who has ever lived by several hundred to one. Longer horizons give larger numbers: about 125 quadrillion births if humanity lasted as long as Earth remains habitable, about a billion years. Roser stresses these are illustrations of what is possible, not forecasts, and the size of the future depends on humanity avoiding catastrophe. The Population Reference Bureau’s latest estimate puts the number ever born at about 117 billion as of 2022, of whom the 8 billion alive then are “nearly 7%” (https://www.prb.org/articles/how-many-people-have-ever-lived-on-earth/). Critics of longtermism do not dispute the arithmetic; they dispute whether merely possible people should count in it, and whether numbers this large should be allowed to swamp everything else.

Critics treat the precision as false; supporters say making a guess explicit is better than leaving it implicit.

One in 14,000 per year compounds to under 1 percent per century, far below both the expert forecasts and Ord’s one-in-six.

## Questions

Question: Should a person’s well-being count for less because they will live later?
Tag: value
Poles: Count everyone equally / Weight the present more
Why contested: Almost everyone agrees that a dollar in the future is worth less than a dollar now if the future is richer or uncertain. The live dispute is about the remaining component, pure time preference: whether a unit of well-being, a life or a harm counts for less merely because of when it happens. That parameter is a moral choice, and at the timescales of climate change and extinction risk it decides most of the answer.
Case for counting everyone equally: Location in time looks like location in space: a child who will be born in 2150 will suffer as much from a flood as a child alive today, and nothing about the date makes that suffering matter less. Philosophers from Henry Sidgwick to Derek Parfit, and economists from Frank Ramsey to Nicholas Stern, have argued that discounting well-being for time alone is a bias, not a value — impatience written into policy. Even at a modest 1 percent pure discount, a death 500 years from now counts for less than a hundredth of a death today, which few would accept if asked directly. Surveyed experts on discounting mostly agree: their most common answer for pure time preference is zero.
Case for weighting the present more: Morality is not only impartial arithmetic; people have special obligations to those near them, including their own generation and its poor, and every generation has given its own needs priority while still leaving successors better off. A pure discount of zero, combined with a long future, implies that the present should save almost everything and sacrifice heavily for people who will probably be far richer — a conclusion economists such as Kenneth Arrow and Partha Dasgupta have called unreasonable. Democratic governments act for the people who elect them, and observed savings and interest rates show how real people weigh the present against the future. Respecting those choices is itself a value.

Question: Will future generations be much richer than we are, even with climate change?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Much richer / Growth at risk
Why contested: The strongest economic reason for discounting is that future people will be richer, so a dollar matters less to them. Central projections say they will be; publics in rich countries largely doubt it, and the case for heavy present sacrifice depends on the scenarios in which growth fails.
Case for much richer: Global income per person has grown for two centuries through wars, pandemics and depressions, and the probabilistic projections used in the US government’s 2023 climate-damage estimates have it growing about 1.6 percent a year through 2100, roughly tripling average income. On those projections, climate damages slow growth rather than reverse it. Asking today’s poorer world to sacrifice for a much richer one is a transfer from the poor to the rich, and investment in growth, health and institutions is itself one of the best gifts to the future.
Case for growth at risk: Averages hide the tail. The cases that matter for future generations are the ones where growth stalls or reverses — severe warming, ecological collapse, a catastrophic war or pandemic — and those are exactly the cases where discounting by expected growth undervalues protection. Projections over a century are guesses; the IPCC’s older scenarios already proved too optimistic about near-term growth. In 36 countries surveyed by Pew in 2024, the median adult expected today’s children to be worse off financially than their parents.

Question: How likely is an extinction-level or unrecoverable catastrophe this century?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Uncomfortably likely / Very remote
Why contested: There is no track record to check against; every estimate is a structured judgment. The best-known numbers differ by more than an order of magnitude, and the longtermist case for priority depends on the risk being large enough to be worth reducing and not so large that efforts are hopeless.
Case for uncomfortably likely: Humanity now has technologies its ancestors never had — nuclear arsenals, the ability to engineer pathogens, and increasingly capable AI — and natural risk alone is estimated at well under 1 percent per century, so most current risk is new and human-made. The median domain expert in the 2022 Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament put extinction by 2100 at 6 percent, and Toby Ord’s best guess for existential catastrophe this century is one in six. Even at the lower end, a risk of a few percent to everything that could ever exist is far larger than risks societies routinely spend heavily to avoid.
Case for very remote: The same tournament’s superforecasters, the people with the best records at predicting uncertain events, put extinction by 2100 at 1 percent, and much of that rests on speculative AI scenarios. Humanity is spread across every continent, adaptable, and has survived plagues, world wars and supervolcanoes; killing everyone, or even preventing recovery, is very much harder than killing many. Specialists who devote careers to a risk may be selected for believing in it, and dramatic estimates have a history of not coming true.

Question: Can what we do now predictably improve the far future?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Influence persists / Effects wash out
Why contested: Even if future people count fully, actions only matter for them if their effects last and can be foreseen. Ripple effects multiply and become unpredictable over centuries, which philosophers call the problem of cluelessness.
Case for influence persists: Some effects are durable and foreseeable. Extinction is permanent; carbon dioxide stays in the air for millennia; constitutions, institutions and technologies lock in for generations. Reducing the probability of an irreversible catastrophe has a predictable sign even if its exact size is unknown. History also shows choices with very long shadows, from the founding of universities to the abolition of slavery.
Case for effects wash out: Most interventions’ long-run effects are swamped by countless other causes within decades, and well-meant actions can backfire in ways no one predicted. Those who argue this point to forecasters, whose accuracy falls sharply beyond a few years. If the far-future consequences of almost everything are unknowable, then the far future cannot guide choices, and people should act on what they can see: the welfare of people alive now and soon.

Question: Is a future with more good lives in it better, or does only the welfare of whoever will exist matter?
Tag: value
Poles: More good lives better / Neutral about numbers
Why contested: This is the core dispute of population ethics. Whether human extinction is an astronomical loss or mainly a loss to the people who die depends on whether never-born people who would have had good lives count as lost.
Case for more good lives better: A world that goes on for a million years, full of people living good lives, is better than one that ends this century, and the difference is not only the deaths in the ending. If a life worth living is good for the person who lives it, then preventing that life is a loss, and preventing trillions of them is an enormous one. Denying this has its own strange implications: it can make it a matter of indifference whether humanity continues at all, as long as no one currently alive is harmed.
Case for neutral about numbers: Morality is about making people better off, not about making more people; as the philosopher Jan Narveson put it, we are in favor of making people happy but neutral about making happy people. A merely possible person who never exists is not deprived of anything. The total view also leads to Parfit’s “Repugnant Conclusion,” that a vast population of lives barely worth living could be better than a smaller flourishing one, which many take as proof that something has gone wrong. On this view, what we owe the future is that whoever does exist lives well, not that as many as possible exist.

Question: Should tiny chances of astronomically large outcomes drive priorities?
Tag: value
Poles: Follow the expected value / Discount long shots
Why contested: When the stakes are trillions of possible lives, even a one-in-a-billion change in the probability of extinction can outweigh saving thousands of lives now in expected-value terms. Whether to accept that reasoning, or resist it as “fanaticism,” is unresolved in decision theory.
Case for following the expected value: Expected-value reasoning is how insurers, engineers and public health agencies handle rare disasters, and refusing to apply it at large scales is arbitrary. The risks in question are not one-in-a-trillion long shots but, on many estimates, percentage points this century, so the argument does not depend on extreme numbers. Ignoring small probabilities of catastrophe is how societies end up unprepared for pandemics and financial crises.
Case for discounting long shots: Reasoning that lets any tiny probability of a vast payoff override all other considerations is vulnerable to manipulation and leads to conclusions nearly everyone rejects, the problem known as Pascal’s mugging. The probabilities involved are not measured but invented, and multiplying an invented probability by an astronomical number produces an astronomical number of no reliability. Prudent decision-makers set aside very small, unverifiable probabilities and attend to the harms they can see.

Question: Should future generations have legal standing that courts and commissioners can enforce?
Tag: value
Poles: Give them standing / Leave it to voters
Why contested: Democracies answer to current voters, and people not yet born cannot vote, lobby or sue. Some countries have responded with constitutional clauses, commissioners and court doctrines of intergenerational equity; others reject these as unelected bodies deciding contested questions.
Case for giving them standing: Elections reward short-term results, and the people most affected by long-lived decisions about carbon, debt, nuclear waste and infrastructure have no voice in them. Giving future generations formal representation — a commissioner with powers to review policy, a constitutional duty, a court that will hear a claim — corrects a structural bias rather than overriding democracy. Wales has required its public bodies to consider long-term well-being since 2015, 81 national constitutions mention future generations, and the International Court of Justice held in 2025 that their interests must be taken into account.
Case for leaving it to voters: What future people will want is unknown, so anyone claiming to speak for them is speaking for their own views about the future. Trade-offs between present and future are among the most contested questions in politics and belong to elected legislatures, not to commissioners or judges. Voters already care about their children and grandchildren and act on it. Scotland’s Parliament rejected a future generations commissioner in January 2026 after a majority of its lead committee recommended against the bill, citing overlap with existing commissioners and public bodies (Social Justice and Social Security Committee, Stage 1 report, December 2025).

Question: Does a strong focus on the far future help or harm people alive today?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Complements the present / Crowds out the present
Why contested: Money and attention are limited. Longtermists say the measures they favor, such as pandemic preparedness and AI safety, also protect people alive now; critics say the far-future frame redirects resources and power away from present needs and toward a small community’s priorities.
Case for complementing the present: Most longtermist priorities are also near-term priorities: a pandemic preparedness system, nuclear risk reduction or safer AI protects people living today, and the Carl Shulman and Elliott Thornley analysis argues that standard cost-benefit analysis, counting only present people, already justifies far more spending on catastrophe prevention. Thinking about the long term also strengthens action on climate and institutions that ordinary politics neglects.
Case for crowding out the present: Philanthropic money moved toward speculative risks is money not spent on malaria, poverty or the documented harms of current technologies. Critics such as Timnit Gebru and Émile Torres argue that framing AI policy around hypothetical extinction has drawn regulators’ attention away from present harms like labor exploitation, surveillance and discrimination, and that a philosophy that can justify almost any present sacrifice for the sake of astronomical future value is dangerous in the hands of the powerful.

## Positions

Camp: Protect future generations through law and policy
Adherents:
- Office of the Future Generations Commissioner for Wales (institution), which oversees the Well-being of Future Generations (Wales) Act 2015 requiring public bodies to meet “the needs of the present … without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.” https://www.futuregenerations.wales/about-us/future-generations-act/
- Pope Francis, Laudato si’ (2015), paragraph 159: “Intergenerational solidarity is not optional, but rather a basic question of justice, since the world we have received also belongs to those who will follow us.” https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/encyclicals/documents/papa-francesco_20150524_enciclica-laudato-si.html
- Also in this camp: the European Commission, whose March 2026 Strategy on Intergenerational Fairness aims to ensure “that current decisions are made with the future in mind” (https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_535); the UN General Assembly, which adopted the Declaration on Future Generations as part of the Pact for the Future in September 2024 (https://www.un.org/en/summit-of-the-future/declaration-on-future-generations); the economist Nicholas Stern, whose 2006 review used a near-zero pure rate of time preference; and Our Children’s Trust, which brings constitutional climate cases on behalf of young people and future generations (https://www.ourchildrenstrust.org/).
Distinct proposition: Present generations owe future ones a fair, livable inheritance as a matter of justice, and because ordinary politics systematically neglects people who cannot vote, that duty should be written into law, institutions and the way governments evaluate policy.
Case: Every generation inherits a world it did not make and passes on one its successors cannot refuse. The carbon dioxide emitted this century will still be warming the planet a thousand years from now, nuclear waste must be kept safe for millennia, and infrastructure, debt and depleted ecosystems all outlast the people who chose them. Those who will bear these costs have no vote, no lobby and no standing to sue, and elected governments working to four- or five-year cycles predictably discount them to nothing. The answer is not a utilitarian calculation about trillions of possible people but an old principle of fairness: leave the world at least as good as you found it, and do not impose harms on others who cannot consent. Discounting future harms at market interest rates treats a child born in 2100 as worth a fraction of a child born today, which no one would defend if asked directly. The remedy is institutional. Give future generations someone who speaks for them, as Wales has done since 2015; write their interests into constitutions, as 81 countries have; count their losses at a low discount rate in policy appraisal; and let courts hold governments to their duties, as the International Court of Justice did in 2025 when it called present generations “trustees of humanity.”
Best evidence: The International Court of Justice’s unanimous 2025 advisory opinion that “due regard for the interests of future generations and the long-term implications of conduct” must inform states’ climate obligations (paragraph 157). The IPCC’s assessment that 15 to 40 percent of carbon dioxide emitted to 2100 will remain in the atmosphere for over 1,000 years. The 2025 Nature study finding that 52 percent of children born in 2020 face unprecedented lifetime heatwave exposure even if warming is held to 1.5°C, against 16 percent of those born in 1960. The spread of future-generations clauses to 41 percent of national constitutions by 2021. Surveyed experts on discounting, whose most common answer for pure time preference is zero.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that future people will be richer and can look after themselves, this camp answers that growth is not guaranteed and that some losses — a stable climate, extinct species, a habitable coastline — cannot be bought back with higher incomes. To the objection that unelected commissioners and judges are taking decisions that belong to voters, it answers that legislatures themselves created these bodies, that their usual powers are to advise, review and require reporting rather than to decide, and that correcting a known bias in democratic decision-making strengthens democracy rather than overriding it. To longtermists who say this camp is too modest, it answers that duties of justice to the people who will actually live are firm ground, while calculations about trillions of possible people are not needed to justify action and can be used to justify almost anything.

Camp: Discount the future at market rates
Adherents:
- The Heritage Foundation (institution), whose 2025 report on the social cost of carbon argues that the 7 percent historical real return on stocks “argues strongly for its use” as the discount rate and that “choosing too low a rate for discounting environmental benefits … would harm those in the future by ignoring investments that would provide greater benefits.” https://www.heritage.org/climate/report/calculating-the-social-cost-carbon-the-give-model-epa-model-not-ready-prime-time (PDF: https://www.heritage.org/sites/default/files/2025-01/SR308_0.pdf)
- William Nordhaus, Yale economist, whose 2007 review of the Stern Review argued that its conclusions depend “decisively on the assumption of a near-zero time discount rate” and “will not survive the substitution of assumptions that are consistent with today’s marketplace real interest rates and savings rates.” https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.45.3.686
- Also in this camp: Bjørn Lomborg and the Copenhagen Consensus Center, who argue that money spent on decarbonization in poor countries returns far less than spending on health, nutrition and education now, and that by UN forecasts climate change reduces income growth this century from 450 percent to 434 percent rather than reversing it (Hoover Institution, April 2024, https://www.hoover.org/news/world-bank-climate-change-policies-leaving-global-south-worse-bjorn-lomborg); and the US federal government since 2025, which reinstated the 2003 guidance with its 7 and 3 percent rates.
Distinct proposition: Benefits to future generations should be discounted at rates that reflect what investment actually earns and how people actually trade the present against the future, because the future is likely to be richer and resources spent now have other uses that also benefit it.
Case: Discounting is not a judgment that future people matter less. It is a recognition that resources have alternative uses. A dollar invested at the historical return on capital becomes many dollars for our grandchildren; a climate or catastrophe policy that returns less than that leaves them poorer than they would otherwise be. The future will very likely be much richer than the present — even with climate change, average incomes are projected to multiply this century — so asking today’s poorer people, including billions in developing countries, to make large sacrifices for richer descendants is a transfer from the poor to the rich. Market interest rates and savings behavior reveal how real people weigh the present against the future; overriding them with a philosopher’s near-zero rate imposes one group’s ethics on everyone. And a near-zero rate proves too much: it implies that the present should save almost all its income for the distant future, which no society has done or would accept. The responsible course is to fund the measures that pass a sound cost-benefit test — which includes some climate policy, such as a moderate carbon price — and to spend the rest where it does the most good, which is often on the world’s poorest people now.
Best evidence: Nordhaus’s DICE-2023 model, in which a cost-benefit optimal policy limits warming to about 2.6°C by 2100 and the 2°C target passes the cost-benefit test only with low discounting or a more pessimistic damage function. The 2003 Circular A-4’s estimate that 7 percent approximates the average before-tax return to private capital. Projections used in the US government’s own 2023 climate-damage estimates, with global income per person growing about 1.6 percent a year through 2100. The 2003 guidance’s argument that, if future generations are expected to be better off, giving them a lower discount rate “would in effect transfer resources from poorer people today to richer people tomorrow.”
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that market rates make catastrophes centuries away invisible, this camp answers that the 2003 US guidance already allows lower-rate sensitivity analysis for intergenerational effects, and that the right response to a catastrophic tail is to model the risk explicitly, not to change the discount rate for everything. To the charge that it is anti-climate-action, it notes that Nordhaus himself has argued for decades for a carbon tax; the dispute is over how much and how fast, not whether. Within the camp there is variation: Nordhaus and Lomborg accept the social cost of carbon as a tool and argue about its size, while Heritage’s analysts argue that the estimates are too uncertain to guide regulation at all.

Camp: Make the long-term future a top priority
Adherents:
- 80,000 Hours (institution), career-advice organization: “Improving the prospects for all future generations is among the most morally important things we can do,” with reducing risks from AI, pandemics, nuclear war and climate change as the main routes. https://80000hours.org/articles/future-generations/
- William MacAskill, Oxford philosopher and author of What We Owe the Future (2022): “Staggering numbers of people will lead lives of flourishing or misery or never live at all, depending on what we do today.” https://whatweowethefuture.com/
- Also in this camp: Toby Ord, author of The Precipice, who puts the chance of existential catastrophe this century at about one in six (https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/toby-ord-the-precipice-existential-risk-future-humanity/); and Forethought, MacAskill’s research institute, whose 2025 Better Futures series argues that improving the quality of the future is in “the same ballpark of priority” as ensuring survival (https://www.forethought.org/research/better-futures).
Distinct proposition: Because the future could contain vastly more people than have ever lived, the effects of our actions on the long-term future are among the most important things about them, and reducing the risk of human extinction or unrecoverable collapse should be a leading global priority.
Case: Future people count. There could be an enormous number of them: if humanity survives as long as a typical mammal species, more than a hundred trillion people may yet be born, hundreds for every person who has ever lived. And we can affect how their lives go, most clearly by making sure they get to exist at all. Humanity has survived natural risks for 200,000 years, but in the last century it acquired the power to destroy itself — nuclear weapons, then engineered pathogens, now increasingly powerful AI — without acquiring the wisdom or institutions to manage that power. Serious estimates put the chance of a civilization-ending catastrophe this century in the range of percentage points, not millionths, and the global effort devoted to preventing it is tiny compared with what is at stake. Because extinction is irreversible and would foreclose everything that could ever follow, even small reductions in its probability are worth a great deal. This is not a call to ignore the present: the same measures — pandemic preparedness, nuclear risk reduction, safe development of AI — protect people alive today. It is a call to take seriously that we may be living at one of the most consequential moments in human history.
Best evidence: The bound that natural extinction risk is almost certainly below 1 in 14,000 per year, which means most current risk is human-made and so reducible. The 2022 forecasting tournament, in which even the lower superforecaster median put extinction by 2100 at 1 percent and the expert median at 6 percent. The rapid rise of AI capabilities since 2020 and statements by leading AI researchers that extinction risk from AI should be a global priority. The Schubert, Caviola and Faber finding that people come to see extinction as uniquely bad once prompted to consider the long-term consequences.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that the argument depends on tiny probabilities multiplied by huge numbers, this camp answers that the risks are estimated at percentage points this century, not one in a trillion, so ordinary prudence suffices; Carl Shulman and Elliott Thornley argue that “the case for preventing catastrophe does not depend on longtermism” and that “standard cost-benefit analysis implies that governments should spend much more on reducing catastrophic risk” (https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:2f6b2c70-aaa6-42ee-a758-6ae19f98c18d). To the objection that it diverts attention from present suffering, it answers that the movement’s members continue to fund global health and that its main priorities protect people alive now. To the objection that the far future is unpredictable, it answers that extinction is the one outcome whose long-run effects are certain. Within the camp there is variation in strength: “strong” longtermists hold that the far future dominates the value of most choices, while others, including Shulman and Thornley, hold only that catastrophe prevention is badly underfunded on any reasonable accounting.

Camp: Reject longtermism and focus on present harms
Adherents:
- Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR) (institution), in a March 2023 statement by Timnit Gebru, Emily M. Bender and Angelina McMillan-Major responding to the call for a pause on advanced AI: hypothetical risks “are the focus of a dangerous ideology called longtermism that ignores the actual harms resulting from the deployment of AI systems today,” and “the harms from so-called AI are real and present and follow from the acts of people and corporations.” https://www.dair-institute.org/blog/letter-statement-March2023/
- Émile P. Torres, philosopher and historian of existential risk, in Aeon (October 2021): “elevating the fulfilment of humanity’s supposed potential above all else could nontrivially increase the probability that actual people suffer extreme harms, even death.” https://aeon.co/essays/why-longtermism-is-the-worlds-most-dangerous-secular-credo
- Also in this camp: the philosopher Kieran Setiya, whose 2022 Boston Review essay on What We Owe the Future defends “the intuition of neutrality” — that adding an extra good life to the world is in itself no better than not doing so — and concludes that if neutrality is right, “the longtermist’s mathematics rest on a mistake” (https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/the-new-moral-mathematics/).
Distinct proposition: Longtermism’s claim that the welfare of vast numbers of possible future people should set today’s priorities is mistaken and dangerous; obligations to the future are best met by addressing injustice and documented harms to people alive now, not by ranking speculative extinction scenarios above them.
Case: Nobody in this camp is against caring about the future. The objection is to a specific ideology that turns the future into an expected-value calculation in which hypothetical trillions of digital or space-faring beings outweigh any number of real people today. A moral framework that can justify almost any present sacrifice in the name of astronomical future value is dangerous, especially when its leading funders and advocates are a small, wealthy, mostly Western technology elite whose own businesses are among the sources of the risks they warn about. In practice the longtermist agenda has redirected philanthropic money and regulatory attention toward speculative AI scenarios while the harms of current systems — exploitation of data workers, surveillance, discrimination, misinformation, concentration of power — go under-addressed. The probabilities attached to existential risks are not measurements; they are guesses dressed as numbers, and multiplying them by imagined populations produces conclusions with no real authority. And the population ethics underneath, which counts merely possible people as losses, is rejected by many philosophers. The best thing this generation can do for future ones is to build a just and livable world now, through democratic means, for the people who are actually here.
Best evidence: The collapse of FTX in November 2022, whose founder had pledged large sums to longtermist causes, cited by critics as evidence of how the movement’s money and reasoning can go wrong. The Schubert, Caviola and Faber finding that most people do not consider extinction uniquely bad unless prompted toward a long-term framing, suggesting longtermism departs from common moral intuition. The wide disagreement between experts and superforecasters on extinction risk (6 percent against 1 percent by 2100), which critics read as evidence that the numbers are not reliable enough to set priorities.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that it ignores real catastrophic risks such as nuclear war, pandemics and climate change, this camp answers that these are real present-day dangers worth addressing on their own terms, and that it opposes not catastrophe prevention but a framework that ranks hypothetical futures above actual people. To the objection that a present focus is just short-termism, it answers that justice now — a stable climate, accountable technology, reduced inequality — is the most reliable legacy any generation can leave, and that the longtermist frame has itself been used to argue for accelerating the very technologies that create risk.

Note on camp structure: The first and fourth camps overlap in practice — many who want future generations protected through law also distrust longtermism — but their defining claims differ: the first is a positive program of institutions and rights for future generations; the fourth is a rejection of longtermist priority-setting, and several of its adherents do not take a position on commissioners or constitutional rights. A hybrid view runs inside the third camp, the long-term future one: Shulman and Thornley’s argument that catastrophe prevention is justified by ordinary cost-benefit analysis counting only people alive today, which supports the same policies without the longtermist premise. A second hybrid runs between the first and second camps: the declining discount rates used by the UK Treasury, which discount ordinary costs and benefits at 3.5 percent but let the rate fall to 2.5 percent for effects more than 75 years away. The view that people who do not yet exist cannot have rights at all, so that nothing is owed to them as a matter of justice (argued by the economist Wilfred Beckerman and the philosopher Joanna Pasek in Justice, Posterity, and the Environment, Oxford University Press, 2001), is a further rejection of the question as posed, but it has little institutional support today and is better treated inside the questions.

## Crux

How much should people who will live long after us shape what we do now, when helping them costs something today?

Every camp agrees that future people exist in the morally relevant sense that matters for most decisions — they will be born, and they will be affected by what we leave them — and none says their suffering is unimportant. They divide over how much weight that gives the future against present needs, and why.

What evidence could settle: Part of the answer is factual, and better evidence would move people in every camp. How likely is a catastrophe that ends humanity or permanently derails it, this century and beyond, and how much can spending reduce that probability? How much richer will future generations be, and how likely are the scenarios in which they are not? How long do the effects of today’s choices — emissions, institutions, technologies — actually persist, and can anyone forecast them well enough to act on? What do investments in growth, health and resilience return compared with investments in emissions cuts or catastrophe prevention? If catastrophic risk turned out to be very small, or impossible to reduce, the case for far-future priority would weaken for nearly everyone; if growth were shown to be fragile, the economic case for discounting would weaken; if far-future effects were shown to be forecastable, the objection that they wash out would weaken.

What no data can close: The rest is a choice about values. Whether a person’s well-being may count for less merely because it comes later. Whether a future containing more good lives is better than one with fewer, so that extinction is a loss to the never-born, or whether what matters is only that whoever does exist lives well. How to act when a very small probability attaches to a very large outcome. How much present generations, and especially the poorest people alive now, may be asked to give up for people who will probably be richer. And who should decide these trade-offs: voters and legislatures, or institutions designed to stand outside the electoral cycle on the future’s behalf. People who agree on every fact can still answer these differently, and most of the distance between the camps lies here.
