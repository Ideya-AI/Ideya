# Democratic Backsliding — research

## Overview

This topic asks whether democracy is eroding from within, how much of that erosion populism in power causes, and what, if anything, should be done about it. “Democratic backsliding” (political scientists also say “autocratization” or “democratic erosion”) names the gradual weakening of the institutions that let citizens choose and constrain their rulers, carried out not by tanks but by elected governments: packing or defying courts, capturing public media and pressuring private outlets, purging or politicizing the civil service and prosecutors, harassing opposition, universities and civil society, and rewriting constitutions to entrench the incumbent. “Populism” is used here in the sense most scholars share: a politics that divides society into a virtuous “people” and a corrupt or self-serving “elite,” and claims that the people’s will should prevail over institutions that stand in its way. It comes in right-wing forms (Viktor Orbán’s Fidesz, Poland’s Law and Justice, Donald Trump’s movement, Germany’s Alternative for Germany) and left-wing forms (Hugo Chávez, Evo Morales, Podemos, La France Insoumise). The topic covers the global measurement of democratic decline and its critics, whether populist governments erode democracy more than others, what drives populist support, how democracies should respond (courts, prosecutions, party bans, European Union sanctions, cordons sanitaires, or simply beating populists at the polls), and whether the institutions populists attack are guardians of democracy or obstacles to it. It deliberately excludes electoral rules and voting access, meaning proportional versus majoritarian systems, voter ID and ballot access (covered in the electoral rules topic); the normative case for democracy against technocratic or epistocratic alternatives (covered in the “Who should rule?” topic); and the specifically American constitutional fight over presidential control of the administrative state (covered in the executive power topic), which appears here only as one national case of a wider pattern.

Why now: on 6 September 2026 Alternative for Germany (AfD), which Germany’s domestic intelligence agency classified in May 2025 as a “confirmed right-wing extremist” organization (a label a Cologne court suspended in February 2026 pending a full ruling), won 43.8 percent of the vote in the Saxony-Anhalt state election, its best result in any German state, and three seats short of a majority (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Saxony-Anhalt_state_election; France 24 on the court ruling, https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20260226-german-court-rules-spy-service-may-not-label-afd-extremist-for-now). The next day Hendrik Wüst, the Christian Democratic premier of North Rhine-Westphalia, called for a federal and state working group to examine the party and “the possible legal consequences,” reopening the argument over whether to seek a ban (Brussels Signal, https://brusselssignal.eu/2026/09/german-state-premier-wust-breaks-cdu-ranks-over-afd-ban/). Five months earlier the question ran the other way: on 12 April 2026 Hungarian voters removed Viktor Orbán, whose sixteen years in power had become the reference case for backsliding, giving Péter Magyar’s Tisza party 53.2 percent of the vote and 141 of 199 seats, a two-thirds majority; Orbán conceded on election night (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Hungarian_parliamentary_election). On 23 September 2026 the European Commission, judging that the new government had addressed the rule-of-law breaches behind its 2022 sanctions, proposed unlocking €4.2 billion in frozen cohesion funds and restoring Hungary’s access to Erasmus+ and Horizon Europe (Eunews, https://www.eunews.it/en/2026/09/23/hungary-von-der-leyen-important-reforms-implemented-urges-the-eu-to-unlock-4-2-billion-euros-from-erasmus-and-horizon/). Meanwhile in March 2026 the V-Dem Institute, for the first time in over fifty years, stopped classifying the United States as a liberal democracy, reporting that its Liberal Democracy Index fell by 24 percent in one year and its world rank from 20th to 51st of 179 countries (V-Dem Democracy Report 2026, https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf); a White House spokeswoman called it “a ridiculous claim made by an irrelevant, blatantly biased organization” (OPB, https://www.opb.org/article/2026/03/20/trump-has-damaged-democracy-at-remarkable-speed-reports-find/).

The debate is live because the same events support opposite readings. To democracy monitors such as V-Dem, Freedom House and International IDEA, and to scholars like Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt, elected populists are now the main way democracies die, and the evidence is a two-decade slide in their indices, with the United States the latest and fastest case. To national conservatives in Europe and the Trump administration, the real threat to self-government comes from unelected judges, bureaucrats, regulators and Brussels, which override voters, censor speech and try to bar popular parties and candidates from the ballot; the United States State Department now accuses Europe of “assaults on democratic self-governance.” Between and beside them sit reformers who think populism grows out of real economic and cultural grievances that mainstream parties ignored and must now answer, left populists who see the danger in oligarchy rather than in populism as such, and a smaller group of scholars who argue that expert-coded indices overstate the decline and that elections, as in Poland in 2023 and Hungary in 2026, are still correcting course. Hungary’s turnover, AfD’s surge and the United States’ November 2026 midterm elections will test each reading within a single year.

Note: V-Dem classifies 74 percent of the world’s people, about 6 billion, as living in autocracies at the end of 2025, up from 50 percent in 2005.
Context: V-Dem sorts countries into four regimes: liberal democracies, electoral democracies, electoral autocracies (which hold multiparty elections that fall short of free and fair) and closed autocracies. The report measures the level of democracy enjoyed by the “average global citizen” (a population-weighted average of its Liberal Democracy Index) as back to where it was in 1978, and down 30 percent from its 2003 peak. Because these classifications rest on expert coding, they are also the figures most contested by scholars who think the decline is overstated.

## Questions

Question: Do populists in power erode democracy more than other governments do?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Populists erode democracy / Populists renew democracy
Why contested: The comparative studies point one way, but they depend on who gets coded as “populist,” a label applied by scholars whose judgments the populists themselves reject, and on democracy scores built partly from expert opinion. Populism’s defenders also point to cases where populist outsiders broke open closed or corrupt party systems and brought excluded voters into politics.
Case for populists erode democracy: The evidence from very different datasets converges. Kyle and Mounk found that 23 percent of populist leaders elected in democracies between 1990 and 2018 presided over significant backsliding, against 6 percent of other elected leaders, and that half rewrote or amended their constitutions (Tony Blair Institute, https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://institute.global/insights/geopolitics-and-security/populist-harm-democracy-empirical-assessment). International IDEA found 22 of 28 aspects of democracy declining under populist governments (https://www.idea.int/sites/default/files/publications/populist-government-and-democracy-impact-assessement-using-gsod-indices.pdf), and Funke, Schularick and Trebesch found that populists stay in office twice as long, rarely leave by losing elections and preside over “the erosion of institutions” (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20202045). The mechanism is built into the idea: if one leader or party alone speaks for “the real people,” then courts, journalists, opposition parties and civil servants who resist are not legitimate rivals but enemies of the people, and there is no principled reason to leave them their independence. The cases, from Venezuela under Chávez and Maduro to Hungary under Orbán, Turkey under Erdoğan and Serbia under Vučić, follow the same sequence of capturing referees, silencing critics and tilting the field.
Case for populists renew democracy: Populist movements arise where established parties have stopped representing large parts of the electorate, on immigration, trade, sovereignty or inequality, and they bring those voters back into politics; International IDEA’s own data show electoral participation improving more under populist governments than under others. Many of the institutions populists challenge, from constitutional courts to central banks, independent agencies and European bodies, have taken decisions away from voters over decades, and contesting that is a democratic demand, not an authoritarian one. The coding is also partisan: governments that restrict speech, prosecute opposition candidates or annul elections in the name of fighting populism are seldom counted as backsliding. And the record since 2018 includes many populists who lost and left office, Bolsonaro, Law and Justice in Poland and Orbán himself, which is how democracy is supposed to work.

Question: Is democracy really declining worldwide, or do expert-coded indices overstate it?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real global decline / Overstated by experts
Why contested: The best-known indices (V-Dem, Freedom House) rest largely on expert judgment, and indicators that do not, such as whether incumbents lose elections and hand over power, show much less change. The EIU’s index, also expert-based, reported a halt in the decline for 2025 while V-Dem and Freedom House reported acceleration.
Case for real global decline: V-Dem counts 44 countries autocratizing in 2025 against 12 in 2005 and finds 74 percent of humanity living under autocracy, and Freedom House has recorded more declines than gains for twenty straight years (https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf; https://freedomhouse.org/article/new-report-global-freedom-declined-20th-consecutive-year-2025). The decline is concentrated in the things modern autocrats actually do, censoring media, harassing civil society and packing courts, precisely because they have learned to keep holding elections. V-Dem researchers have tested whether shifting expert bias explains the trend and conclude it is “highly unlikely” (V-Dem Working Paper 140, https://www.v-dem.net/media/publications/wp_140.pdf), and its measurement model aggregates thousands of country experts, including many who live in the countries they rate.
Case for overstated by experts: Andrew Little and Anne Meng surveyed objective indicators, such as incumbent vote margins, turnover in power and the rate at which ruling parties lose, and found “little evidence of global democratic decline during the past decade”; they argue that expert coders immersed in a pessimistic media climate may have changed their standards over time (PS: Political Science & Politics, 2024, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/measuring-democratic-backsliding/9EE2044CDA598BD815349912E61189D8). Daniel Treisman finds the share of democracies still near its historical high and the recent rate of breakdown unremarkable (Comparative Political Studies, 2023, https://www.danieltreisman.org/s/ms-JAN-2023-FINAL-with-online-appendix.pdf). Freedom House rates 88 countries Free today against 87 in 2005 (https://freedomhouse.org/report/freedom-world/2026/growing-shadow-autocracy). The headline population figures are driven by a handful of very large countries, especially India, and by judgment calls about where a country crosses a line.

Question: When elected majorities and unelected institutions clash, who should have the final word?
Tag: value
Poles: Elected majorities / Independent institutions
Why contested: Every democracy balances majority rule against courts, independent prosecutors, central banks, public broadcasters and a protected civil service, and the balance has shifted toward the unelected side in most countries since 1945. What one side calls capture of neutral referees, the other calls restoring democratic control over bodies that escaped it.
Case for elected majorities: Democracy means the people rule through those they elect and can remove. Over decades, judges, regulators, career officials, international courts and European institutions have taken over decisions on immigration, speech, social policy and the economy that voters never delegated to them, and they cannot be voted out. When a newly elected government finds that it cannot carry out the program it won on, because unelected officials block it, the problem is not the government. Replacing officials, reforming courts and reasserting control over public institutions are ordinary acts of democratic government, which the other side performs quietly when it holds power and denounces loudly when it does not.
Case for independent institutions: A majority today can use power to make sure it stays the majority tomorrow, by capturing the courts that would check it, the prosecutors who would investigate it, the media that would expose it and the officials who run elections. Independent institutions exist so that the next election can still be fair and so that the rights of those who lose are protected; they are what make it safe to lose. Constitutional courts and independent civil services were built after the 1930s precisely because elected majorities had voted away democracy before. The test is not who wins a given clash but whether the reform would still look acceptable if the other side held the power it creates.

Question: Should democracies ban parties or bar candidates they judge to be antidemocratic?
Tag: value
Poles: Ban the threat / Let voters decide
Why contested: German and several other European constitutions provide for banning parties that seek to abolish the democratic order, and courts have barred or convicted popular candidates, including Marine Le Pen in France, Călin Georgescu in Romania and Jair Bolsonaro in Brazil. Each move is either the constitution defending itself or incumbents removing a rival, depending on who is judging.
Case for ban the threat: Democracy is not a suicide pact. The Weimar Republic let a party that promised to abolish it compete, and that party won power legally. Germany’s Basic Law was written to allow a “militant democracy” to ban parties that aim to overthrow the constitutional order, and its constitutional court has used the power before. When members of a party meet to discuss expelling millions of people, including citizens of immigrant origin, as AfD politicians did at a Potsdam meeting in November 2023 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Potsdam_far-right_meeting), waiting for it to win power and then hoping institutions survive is reckless. Prosecuting leaders who attempted coups, as Brazil did with Bolsonaro, sentenced on 11 September 2025 to 27 years and 3 months (https://www.npr.org/2025/09/11/nx-s1-5535658/bolsonaro-brazil-coup-trial), and South Korea did with Yoon Suk Yeol, sentenced to life on 19 February 2026 for his December 2024 martial-law decree (https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/19/south-korea-ex-president-yoon-suk-yeol-life-sentence-guilty-insurrection-death-sentence-martial-law-verdict.html), is the rule of law applying to the powerful.
Case for let voters decide: Banning a party that won a fifth of the national vote in February 2025 and 43.8 percent in Saxony-Anhalt in September 2026 does not remove its voters; it tells them their votes do not count and confirms the story that elites will not accept an outcome they dislike. A ban would take years, might fail in court, and would hand the party martyrdom. Annulling an election, as Romania’s constitutional court did in December 2024, or barring a leading candidate from the ballot through a criminal case, sets a precedent any future government can use against its own opponents. Chancellor Merz’s view, that a ban would not “solve the problem the party’s voters are seeing,” is shared by many who consider AfD dangerous (deutschland.de, https://www.deutschland.de/en/news/merz-banning-the-afd-will-not-solve-the-problem). Coups and martial law, which are crimes, are a different matter from popular parties with objectionable programs.

Question: Is populist support driven mainly by economic loss or by cultural backlash?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Economic grievance / Cultural backlash
Why contested: Both have strong evidence behind them, and they interact: economic shocks often work through identity, and cultural anxieties grow where economies stagnate. The answer matters because it points to different remedies, redistribution and industrial policy on one side, immigration and cultural policy on the other.
Case for economic grievance: Regions hit hardest by import competition from China, by automation, and by austerity after 2008 moved most toward populist parties, in the United States, Britain, France, Germany and Italy; Dani Rodrik reviews “compelling evidence that globalization shocks, often working through culture and identity, have played an important role” in raising populist support (NBER Working Paper 27526, https://www.nber.org/papers/w27526). Decades of stagnant wages for workers without degrees, the concentration of good jobs in big cities and the 2008 bailouts of banks gave people real reasons to distrust the elites who promised them globalization would benefit everyone. Funke, Schularick and Trebesch show populist waves following financial crises going back a century.
Case for cultural backlash: Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart argue that support for authoritarian populism is better predicted by age cohort, education and values than by income or job loss, and reflects a reaction by older and less educated voters against rapid liberal change in values, immigration and national identity (Cultural Backlash, 2019, https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/cultural-backlash-trump-brexit-and-authoritarian-populism). Populist parties are strong in rich, low-unemployment countries such as Switzerland, Austria and the Netherlands, and many populist voters are not poor. Survey after survey finds immigration and national identity, not personal finances, at the top of populist voters’ concerns.

Question: Do mainstream parties defeat populists by adopting some of their demands or by refusing to?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Adopt the demands / Hold the line
Why contested: Denmark’s Social Democrats adopted restrictive immigration policies and the Danish People’s Party collapsed, while in other countries center-right parties that moved toward populist positions saw populists grow. Cross-national studies disagree with the most famous single case.
Case for adopt the demands: Voters turn to populists when mainstream parties refuse to act on issues they care about, above all immigration. When Mette Frederiksen’s Social Democrats embraced strict immigration controls, the Danish People’s Party fell from 21.1 percent in 2015 to 8.7 percent in 2019 (InfoMigrants, https://www.infomigrants.net/en/post/17414/denmark-frederiksens-immigration-model-key-factor-in-election). Addressing a legitimate grievance removes the populist’s monopoly on it; ignoring it tells voters the system will never listen, which is the populist’s core claim.
Case for hold the line: A study of mainstream party strategies across Western Europe found no evidence that accommodating radical right positions reduces radical right support, and if anything more voters defected to the radical right (Krause, Cohen and Abou-Chadi, Political Science Research and Methods, 2023, https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5C3476FCD26B188C7399ADD920D71770/S2049847022000085a.pdf/does-accommodation-work-mainstream-party-strategies-and-the-success-of-radical-right-parties.pdf). Adopting populist language legitimizes it and makes the original more credible than the copy. Germany’s CDU moved right on migration and AfD still rose to 43.8 percent in Saxony-Anhalt, with the CDU at 17.2; Denmark’s model may reflect Danish conditions rather than a general law.

Question: Can populist-led erosion be reversed at the ballot box?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Voters can reverse it / Entrenchment wins
Why contested: Recent years offer both kinds of case. Opposition coalitions defeated entrenched populists in Poland in 2023 and Hungary in 2026, while in Venezuela, Turkey, Russia and Nicaragua elected leaders entrenched themselves beyond electoral reach. [Correction 2026-10-05: the page no longer lists Turkey among leaders beyond electoral reach, since its 2023 presidential runoff was competitive; it names Venezuela, Russia and Nicaragua.] Even where power changes hands, undoing the institutional changes is slow.
Case for voters can reverse it: V-Dem counts 10 “U-turn” democratizers reversing earlier decline, among them Poland, Brazil, Guatemala and Zambia, and reports that 70 percent of autocracies that begin a democratization episode succeed (https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf). Hungary is the strongest recent case: after sixteen years in which Fidesz redrew districts, consolidated friendly media and reshaped the courts, Tisza still won a two-thirds majority on record turnout, Orbán conceded the same night, and within five months the European Commission judged enough reforms made to propose releasing frozen funds. Oppositions win when they unite behind one candidate, campaign on the economy and corruption, and mobilize young voters.
Case for entrenchment wins: The populists who were voted out were caught early or never fully consolidated control. In Venezuela, Nicaragua, Turkey and Russia, leaders who first won real elections now hold elections the opposition cannot win. Even in Poland, the government elected in 2023 to repair the courts has been blocked by a president aligned with the former ruling party: Karol Nawrocki vetoed its bill to restore judges’ control over the National Council of the Judiciary on 19 February 2026 (Notes from Poland, https://notesfrompoland.com/2026/02/19/president-vetoes-bill-reforming-judicial-body-at-heart-of-polands-rule-of-law-crisis/), and by June 2026 had issued a record 37 vetoes (https://notesfrompoland.com/2026/06/12/nawrocki-issues-record-37th-veto-more-than-any-other-president-in-polish-history/). Captured institutions outlast the governments that captured them.

Question: Is the United States undergoing democratic backsliding?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Backsliding under way / Hardball within democracy
Why contested: Monitors that use expert judgment all recorded US declines for 2025, of very different sizes (V-Dem sharply, Freedom House and the EIU modestly), while elections, courts and a competitive opposition continue to function. The answer divides almost perfectly along party lines, which makes it hard to separate evidence from allegiance.
Case for backsliding under way: V-Dem recorded the largest one-year fall in American democracy since 1789, citing the rapid concentration of power in the presidency, the collapse of congressional constraints, pressure on universities and media, and executive orders targeting law firms that had represented the president’s political opponents (https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf). Freedom House cut the US score to its lowest ever, and political scientists surveyed by Bright Line Watch rated US democracy 53 to 60 out of 100 throughout 2025 and 2026, below any reading under Biden (https://brightlinewatch.org/a-partial-democratic-rebound/). Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way argue the country is moving into “competitive authoritarianism,” in which elections continue but the incumbent’s abuse of power tilts the field (Foreign Affairs, https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/path-american-authoritarianism-trump).
Case for hardball within democracy: The administration won a national election on a promise to take control of an unelected bureaucracy, and it is doing so; courts, including the Supreme Court, have ruled against it on major questions, such as, in February 2026, the tariffs it had imposed under emergency powers, and it has turned to other legal authorities rather than openly defying those rulings (V-Dem, which reports the tariff episode, calls the response “in effect circumventing the court”). Dima Kortukov and Julian Waller argue that what is happening is “not a question of regime, but one of state reform or state capture (depending on one’s normative viewpoint),” and that calling it authoritarianism misapplies the concept (American Affairs, https://americanaffairsjournal.org/2025/08/authoritarianism-reform-or-capture-democracy-in-trumps-america/). The 2026 midterm elections will be held under state administration, and opposition parties are well funded and competitive. Expert surveys drawn from a profession that leans heavily to one party were also alarmed in 2017 to 2020, and the republic did not fall.

Question: Should the European Union punish member states for democratic backsliding?
Tag: value
Poles: Enforce common standards / Respect national sovereignty
Why contested: EU treaties commit members to democracy and the rule of law, and since 2021 a regulation lets the EU withhold budget funds when rule-of-law breaches threaten them. It froze billions for Hungary under Orbán, and the funds are now being released to a new government.
Case for enforce common standards: Countries joined the EU voluntarily and signed up to its values, and the EU’s single market, courts and budget only work if every member has independent judges and honest procurement. Taxpayers elsewhere should not fund governments that funnel EU money to allies and dismantle the courts that would catch them. The Hungarian case shows the tool works: the new government strengthened its Integrity Authority, asset declarations and judicial review of prosecutors, and the Commission proposed on 23 September 2026 to unlock €4.2 billion and restore Hungary’s access to Erasmus+ and Horizon Europe (Eunews, https://www.eunews.it/en/2026/09/23/hungary-von-der-leyen-important-reforms-implemented-urges-the-eu-to-unlock-4-2-billion-euros-from-erasmus-and-horizon/).
Case for respect national sovereignty: The EU’s definition of “rule of law” has grown to cover contested national choices on family law, migration and media, and it is applied selectively: governments of the right have been sanctioned while others with similar problems were not. Withholding funds from a country’s students and regions punishes citizens for the government they elected and lets Brussels influence national elections. The timing of the Hungarian release, months after a change of government, confirms to critics that the conditions were about who governed, not only about what the laws said.

## Positions

Camp: Defend the checks on elected power
Adherents:
- V-Dem Institute at the University of Gothenburg (institution), whose Democracy Report 2026 finds that “the speed with which American democracy is currently dismantled is unprecedented in modern history” and counts 44 countries autocratizing, most through elected incumbents concentrating power: https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf
- Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way, political scientists at Harvard and Toronto, who wrote in Foreign Affairs in February 2025 that US democracy “will likely break down during the second Trump administration” into “competitive authoritarianism—a system in which parties compete in elections but the incumbent’s abuse of power tilts the playing field against the opposition”: https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/path-american-authoritarianism-trump (full text via Harvard’s Ash Center: https://ash.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Levitsky-Way-2025-The-Path-to-American-Authoritarianism-What-Comes-After-Democratic-Breakdown-3.pdf)
- Also in this camp: Freedom House; International IDEA; the European Commission and European Parliament in their rule-of-law proceedings against Hungary and Poland; Protect Democracy and other US pro-democracy groups; Daniel Ziblatt; Jan-Werner Müller, author of What Is Populism?; Anne Applebaum; Germany’s Social Democrats and Greens, who support examining an AfD ban.
Variation inside the camp: The camp divides over tools. Its militant-democracy wing supports banning parties that aim to abolish the constitutional order, prosecuting leaders who attempted coups or abused office, and barring convicted candidates, as in the cases of Bolsonaro, Le Pen and Georgescu; others in the camp accept prosecutions for crimes but oppose party bans as counterproductive and prefer a cordon sanitaire that keeps populists out of government coalitions. Europeans in the camp favor supranational enforcement such as EU funding conditionality; Americans rely more on courts, states, civil society and elections. Most treat populism itself as the danger because it denies the legitimacy of opponents; others see the danger in any incumbent, populist or not, who captures referees.
Case: Democracies rarely die in coups anymore. They die at the hands of leaders who win elections and then use the powers of office to make sure they cannot lose the next one: capturing the courts that would check them, the prosecutors who would investigate them, the regulators who license broadcasters and the officials who run elections, and harassing the journalists, universities and civil society groups that would expose them. Each step is legal or nearly so, and each is defended as the will of the people against a corrupt elite. That is why populism in particular is dangerous: a movement that claims to be the only authentic voice of the people cannot accept that its opponents, or the institutions that constrain it, are legitimate. The checks on elected power are not obstacles to democracy but what makes it possible to lose an election safely and to win the next one. Hungary under Orbán showed how far the process can go inside the European Union, and the United States now shows how fast it can move in the world’s oldest constitutional democracy. The response must be to defend those checks early, while elections can still decide, through courts, legislatures, federalism, a free press, civil society and, where the law allows, sanctions on those who break it.
Best evidence: Kyle and Mounk’s finding that 23 percent of populist leaders elected in democracies presided over significant backsliding against 6 percent of other elected leaders, and that only 8 of 47 populists who took office from 1990 to 2014 stepped down after losing a free election (Tony Blair Institute, https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://institute.global/insights/geopolitics-and-security/populist-harm-democracy-empirical-assessment). V-Dem’s record of Hungary, whose Liberal Democracy Index fell 0.451 points from 2009, the largest decline of any current autocratizer, and of the United States, whose score fell from 0.79 in 2023 to 0.57 in 2025. The experimental finding that voters punish undemocratic candidates of their own party only weakly, about 3.5 percent of vote share in realistic scenarios, which means voters alone cannot be relied on to stop erosion (Graham and Svolik, https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/campuspress.yale.edu/dist/6/1038/files/2020/06/Graham-and-Svolik-2020-APSR.pdf).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that this camp defends the power of unelected elites against voters and uses “democracy” as a label for its own policy preferences. But the checks it defends are not partisan: an independent judiciary, a free press and neutral election officials protect conservatives when progressives govern just as they protect progressives now, and the camp’s own record includes finding backsliding under left-wing governments in Venezuela, Nicaragua and Mexico. The test the camp applies is procedural, not ideological: does a government leave its opponents a fair chance to replace it? Hungary’s 2026 election is not a refutation but a warning of how narrow the margin became: a united opposition needed a landslide under districts drawn by the incumbent to win.

Camp: Return power from unelected institutions to voters
Adherents:
- United States Department of State (institution), whose Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights, and Labor published “The Need for Civilizational Allies in Europe” by senior adviser Samuel Samson on 27 May 2025, arguing that “Europe has devolved into a hotbed of digital censorship, mass migration, restrictions on religious freedom, and numerous other assaults on democratic self-governance” and that the “global liberal project” is “trampling democracy” for “a decadent governing class afraid of its own people”: https://statedept.substack.com/p/the-need-for-civilizational-allies-in-europe
- Vice President J.D. Vance, at the Munich Security Conference on 14 February 2025: “the threat that I worry the most about vis-à-vis Europe is not Russia, it’s not China… what I worry about is the threat from within,” and on firewalls against populist parties, “Democracy rests on the sacred principle that the voice of the people matters. There is no room for firewalls”: https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-the-vice-president-the-munich-security-conference-0
- Also in this camp: Viktor Orbán and Fidesz; the Patriots for Europe group in the European Parliament, including France’s National Rally and Austria’s Freedom Party; Alternative for Germany; Poland’s Law and Justice and President Karol Nawrocki; the Heritage Foundation; the Danube Institute and Mathias Corvinus Collegium in Budapest; national-conservative writers such as Yoram Hazony and Patrick Deneen; the White House, which called V-Dem’s 2026 finding “a ridiculous claim made by an irrelevant, blatantly biased organization” (https://www.opb.org/article/2026/03/20/trump-has-damaged-democracy-at-remarkable-speed-reports-find/).
Variation inside the camp: Some in this camp, like Orbán, openly reject liberal democracy in favor of what he called in July 2014 “an illiberal state, not a liberal state,” one that “does not deny the fundamental values of liberalism, such as freedom” but does not make liberalism the central organizing principle of the state (American Rhetoric transcript, https://www.americanrhetoric.com/speeches/viktororbanilliberaldemocracyspeech.htm); others, including most American conservatives, say they are defending liberal democracy itself, free speech and fair elections, against a progressive establishment that has abandoned it. The camp divides on how far executive power should reach: a unitary-executive wing wants presidents and prime ministers to control the bureaucracy, prosecutors and independent agencies fully, while others want power returned mainly to legislatures, states and nations rather than to executives. On Europe it ranges from those who want to leave or dismantle the EU to those who want a looser “Europe of nations.”
Case: Democracy means that the people choose their government and the government then governs. Over the past half century, more and more power has moved to bodies no voter can remove: constitutional and international courts, career bureaucracies, central banks, regulators, NGOs funded from abroad, the European Commission, and the media and academic institutions that decide which opinions are respectable. When voters elect governments that try to reclaim that power, to control immigration, protect national culture or reform captured courts, the same establishment labels them “populists” and “autocrats,” and the indices that measure democracy are compiled by the establishment’s own academics. Meanwhile it is the establishment that censors speech online, annuls elections it dislikes, as Romania did in 2024, prosecutes opposition leaders and bars them from the ballot, as France did with Marine Le Pen, and asks its intelligence services to classify the largest opposition party as extremist, as Germany did with AfD. Those are the real threats to democratic self-government. A populist government that loses an election and leaves, as Orbán did in April 2026 and Law and Justice did in 2023, has shown more respect for democracy than a system that decides in advance which parties voters may choose.
Best evidence: Romania’s Constitutional Court annulled the first round of its presidential election in December 2024 and the leading candidate, Călin Georgescu, was then barred from the rerun (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Romanian_presidential_election). Germany’s domestic intelligence agency labeled AfD extremist in May 2025, and a court suspended the label in February 2026, finding that the party as a whole could not be described as anti-constitutional (France 24, https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20260226-german-court-rules-spy-service-may-not-label-afd-extremist-for-now). Orbán conceded defeat on election night in April 2026 and Fidesz went into opposition (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Hungarian_parliamentary_election). Little and Meng’s finding that objective indicators, as opposed to expert judgments, show little global decline (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/measuring-democratic-backsliding/9EE2044CDA598BD815349912E61189D8).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that populist governments use their majorities to capture the institutions that would let voters remove them, so that “returning power to the people” ends with the people unable to take it back. But every government appoints judges, replaces officials and changes laws; the question is whether elections remain real, and in Hungary, Poland, Brazil, the United States and Slovakia populist parties have both won and lost. Orbán’s defeat is the clearest proof that the “electoral autocracy” label was wrong. Independent institutions are not neutral simply because they are unelected; a judiciary or civil service that is overwhelmingly of one political outlook is itself a partisan actor, and bringing it under democratic control is not an attack on democracy.

Camp: Answer the grievances that fuel populism
Adherents:
- Germany’s federal government under Chancellor Friedrich Merz (institution), who on 10 September 2026, after AfD’s victory in Saxony-Anhalt, called a ban “a very high obstacle” and said, “I do not think that banning this party would solve the problem the party’s voters are seeing in Germany,” calling instead for bringing voters back to the political center with a convincing program: https://www.deutschland.de/en/news/merz-banning-the-afd-will-not-solve-the-problem
- Jake Sullivan, then national security adviser, in the Biden White House’s April 2023 Brookings speech on a new economic policy, which named “the challenge of inequality and its damage to democracy” and said deindustrialization and globalization “had frayed the socioeconomic foundations on which any strong and resilient democracy rests”: https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-national-security-advisor-jake-sullivan-renewing-american-economic-leadership-the
- Also in this camp: Dani Rodrik, whose review finds “compelling evidence that globalization shocks, often working through culture and identity, have played an important role in driving up support for populist movements” (https://www.nber.org/papers/w27526); Denmark’s Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Social Democrats; Britain’s “Blue Labour” current; arguably Hungary’s Péter Magyar, whose victory came, in one analyst’s reading, from voters “angry about a stagnating economy, high inflation, a dysfunctional health care system and a government riddled with scandals and corruption” (LSE European Politics blog, https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/europpblog/2026/04/15/hungary-election-tisza-party-fidesz-victory/).
Variation inside the camp: The camp divides over which grievances matter most. Its economic wing (Rodrik, Sullivan and the Biden administration’s industrial policy) stresses deindustrialization, regional decline and inequality, and wants industrial policy, trade protection and investment in left-behind places; its cultural wing (Frederiksen, parts of the European center-right) stresses immigration and national identity, and wants mainstream parties to adopt stricter immigration controls themselves. Center-right members such as Merz combine a tougher line on migration with pro-growth economics. Most in the camp still support courts, prosecutions for crimes and EU enforcement, but they oppose banning popular parties and treat institutional defense as necessary but not sufficient.
Case: Populism is a symptom before it is a threat. For decades mainstream parties of left and right delivered stagnant wages for people without degrees, the hollowing out of industrial towns, bailouts for banks, and high immigration that voters repeatedly said they did not want, and then told those who objected that their concerns were illegitimate. Populists won because they were the only ones listening. Defending institutions matters, but a democracy that relies on courts, intelligence agencies and bans to keep a third of its voters from power is not secure; it is postponing a reckoning and confirming the populist story that the system is rigged. The durable answer is for democratic parties to deliver: rebuild the industrial base, raise wages, control borders, fight corruption and govern competently, so that voters no longer need an outsider to be heard. When that happens, as in Denmark after 2019 or Hungary in 2026, populists lose at the ballot box, which is the only victory that lasts.
Best evidence: The Danish People’s Party fell from 21.1 percent in 2015 to 8.7 percent in 2019 after the Social Democrats adopted strict immigration policies (InfoMigrants, https://www.infomigrants.net/en/post/17414/denmark-frederiksens-immigration-model-key-factor-in-election). Economic studies link populist voting to trade shocks, austerity and financial crises, and Funke, Schularick and Trebesch find populist waves following financial crises across a century (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20202045). Freedom House’s own reading of Hungary’s 2026 result is that “not even entrenched autocrats can ignore the economy” (https://freedomhouse.org/article/after-election-revitalizing-hungarian-democracy-and-lessons-opposition-victory).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that copying populists’ positions legitimizes them and, according to cross-national studies, does not reduce their vote. But the camp’s claim is not that mainstream parties should imitate populist rhetoric; it is that they should solve the problems voters care about, which is different from echoing slogans while doing nothing. Where parties changed policy credibly and delivered, as in Denmark, the populist vote fell; where they borrowed the language without the substance, it rose. Refusing to act on immigration or economic decline for fear of looking populist leaves those issues to the populists.

Camp: Mobilize the many against economic oligarchy
Adherents:
- La France Insoumise (institution), the French party founded by Jean-Luc Mélenchon, which frames politics as “the people” against “the oligarchy” and proposes a citizen-elected constituent assembly for a Sixth Republic with citizen-initiated referendums and the power to recall representatives, because “it is up to the people to write their constitution, not a cabinet of experts”: https://www.france24.com/en/europe/20220321-a-new-republic-leftist-m%C3%A9lenchon-promises-to-topple-france-s-presidential-monarchy
- Senator Bernie Sanders, on 21 September 2026: the climate “of the rich getting richer, of working people in our country struggling… is the reality that demagogues, whether in the United States or any place else, are feeding on,” and change “will either come from right-wing demagogues or from political parties that embrace a grassroots movement that fights for a working class agenda”: https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/prepared-remarks-sanders-ahead-of-un-general-assembly-oligarchy-is-ascending-and-democracy-is-in-retreat-working-together-that-must-change/
- Also in this camp: the political theorist Chantal Mouffe, author of For a Left Populism, who argues that left populism “works to extend and radicalize democracy” while right-wing populism “tends to restrict democracy” (Verso, https://www.versobooks.com/blogs/news/3341-chantal-mouffe-we-urgently-need-to-promote-a-left-populism); Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Sanders’s partner on the 2025 Fighting Oligarchy tour; Spain’s Podemos in its founding period; parts of the Democratic Socialists of America.
Variation inside the camp: Some in the camp, following Mouffe, openly embrace the word “populism” and the people-versus-elite framing; others, like Sanders, speak of oligarchy and working-class politics and reject being grouped with right-wing populists. They differ on institutions: La France Insoumise wants a new constitution drafted by an elected or randomly selected assembly, while Sanders works within existing institutions and defends them against the Trump administration. Some in the camp defended Latin American left-populist governments such as Chávez’s Venezuela in their early years; most now distance themselves from that record.
Case: The crisis of democracy did not begin with populism; it began with oligarchy. For forty years, governments of center-left and center-right alike handed power to markets, central banks, trade agreements and experts, while wealth concentrated at the top and money came to dominate politics. Citizens could vote, but on the questions that shaped their lives, jobs, wages, housing, public services, the choices on offer were the same. That hollowed-out “post-democracy” is what created the anger that right-wing demagogues now channel against immigrants and minorities instead of against the billionaires who profit. Defending the existing institutions is not enough, because those institutions presided over the decline. The answer is to give that anger a democratic, egalitarian direction: a politics that names the real adversary, concentrated economic power, mobilizes working people across racial and national lines, and extends democracy into the economy and into how constitutions are made.
Best evidence: The top 1 percent of US households held 32.5 percent of the country’s net worth in the second quarter of 2026, according to the Federal Reserve’s Distributional Financial Accounts (FRED, https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134), and Sanders’s 2025 Fighting Oligarchy rallies drew crowds of tens of thousands, including in Republican-leaning places such as Folsom, California and Nampa, Idaho (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fighting_Oligarchy_tour), suggesting that an anti-oligarchy message reaches the same voters right-wing populists do. Funke, Schularick and Trebesch find populist waves following financial crises, the moments when the costs of elite economic management are most visible (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20202045). Graham and Svolik’s finding that polarized partisans tolerate undemocratic behavior from their own side suggests that the way to defeat authoritarian populism is to change the axis of conflict, from culture to class.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that left-wing populism erodes democracy too, as in Venezuela under Chávez and Maduro, and that Kyle and Mounk found left-wing populists curtailing civil liberties almost as often as right-wing ones. The camp answers that a democratic left populism is defined by its commitment to pluralism, minority rights and free elections; the problem in Venezuela was the concentration of power in a leader and the militarization of the state, not the demand to redistribute wealth and power. Those who make this objection rarely apply it to the concentration of power in corporations and in the wealthy, which is the larger and more permanent threat to political equality.

Camp: Reserve the backsliding label for real regime change
Adherents:
- Andrew Little and Anne Meng, political scientists at Berkeley and the University of Virginia, who find in objective indicators “little evidence of global democratic decline during the past decade” and conclude that the claim that the world is becoming less democratic in ways only experts can detect “is not justified by existing evidence”: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/measuring-democratic-backsliding/9EE2044CDA598BD815349912E61189D8
- Dima Kortukov and Julian Waller, in American Affairs (August 2025), who argue that describing the United States as competitive authoritarianism misapplies the concept and that “the ongoing, hostile reformatting of the federal bureaucracy by the current administration is not a question of regime, but one of state reform or state capture (depending on one’s normative viewpoint)”: https://americanaffairsjournal.org/2025/08/authoritarianism-reform-or-capture-democracy-in-trumps-america/
- Also in this camp: Daniel Treisman of UCLA, who finds “the global proportion of democracies remains close to an all-time high” and that “the fear of a global slide into autocracy appears premature” (Comparative Political Studies, 2023, https://www.danieltreisman.org/s/ms-JAN-2023-FINAL-with-online-appendix.pdf); Andrew J. Taylor of North Carolina State University, who argues in National Affairs (Fall 2026) that claims of democratic backsliding in Republican-run US states are “not well supported by the evidence” (https://www.nationalaffairs.com/publications/detail/myth-democratic-backsliding-states). No institution was found that takes this position as an organization; its adherents are individual scholars, published in peer-reviewed journals and in the quarterlies American Affairs and National Affairs.
Variation inside the camp: Some in this camp question the measurement of decline, arguing that expert-coded indices drift with the mood of the experts; others accept that particular countries such as Hungary, Turkey or Venezuela have become less democratic but object to stretching the concept to cover ordinary political conflict, polarization and hardball in established democracies. Some are sympathetic to the populist governments in question; many are not and simply want the diagnosis to be accurate.
Case: “Democratic backsliding” should mean something specific: a change in the rules that makes it harder for voters to remove the government. That has happened in some places. But the term is now applied to almost anything a government’s critics dislike, from firing civil servants and pressuring universities to winning elections on anti-immigration platforms, and the indices that report a global collapse rest on experts whose judgments move with the news. Objective indicators tell a steadier story: incumbents still lose elections at about the same rate, power still changes hands, and populist governments in Poland, Brazil, the United States and Hungary have been voted out. Treating every electoral victory by an outsider as a step toward autocracy has costs. It cheapens the warning for the cases that are real, it invites the extraordinary measures, bans, annulments, prosecutions of candidates, that themselves weaken democracy, and it tells voters that choosing the wrong party is itself illegitimate.
Best evidence: Freedom House counts 88 Free countries today against 87 in 2005 (https://freedomhouse.org/report/freedom-world/2026/growing-shadow-autocracy), and the EIU reported that its global democracy score stopped falling in 2025 (https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/eiu-democracy-index-2025-democracy-stabilises-after-eight-years-of-decline-302734863.html). Hungary, V-Dem’s leading autocratizer, removed its government in an election Orbán conceded on the night. Little and Meng’s comparison of objective and expert-coded indicators, and the gap between V-Dem’s 24 percent one-year fall for the United States and the smaller declines recorded by Freedom House (3 points of 100) and the EIU (0.2 points of 10) for the same year.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that modern autocrats deliberately erode democracy in ways objective indicators cannot see, packing courts, buying media and tilting the field while elections go on, so waiting for incumbents to stop losing means waiting until it is too late. The camp accepts that such erosion is possible and that experts may see it first. But it asks for evidence proportionate to the claim: if democracy is in fact eroding, incumbents should in time become harder to remove, and in the cases most often cited they have not been. A warning system that sounds equally for Hungary under Orbán and for any government its experts oppose will not be heeded when it matters.

## Crux

The disagreement under the disagreements: when an elected government takes control of institutions that were built to constrain it, the courts, prosecutors, civil service, public media, election bodies and regulators, is it returning power to the voters who elected it, or taking away those voters’ ability to replace it? And who can be trusted to tell the difference: the voters at the next election, courts and constitutions, outside bodies such as the European Union, or experts who measure democracy?

Every camp answers this question, usually without stating it. The camp defending checks on elected power believes that such takeovers, once begun, tend to entrench incumbents, and that waiting for the next election to decide may mean waiting until elections no longer can. The camp returning power to voters believes that unelected institutions have themselves become partisan and unaccountable, and that bringing them under elected control restores democracy, as long as elections remain free. The camp answering populist grievances accepts the danger but thinks the institutions will not hold unless mainstream politics wins back the voters who turned against them. The anti-oligarchy camp locates the capture elsewhere, in concentrated wealth, and thinks the fight over courts and bureaucracies misses the larger loss of popular control. The camp reserving the backsliding label asks for proof that removal at the polls has actually become harder before calling any of this a threat to democracy.

What evidence could settle: whether governments that remake these institutions actually become harder to vote out. This is observable over time, in how often such incumbents lose and leave, in the fairness of the elections they hold, and in how often reversals succeed. The record so far is mixed and is still being written: Hungary’s 2026 turnover, Poland’s 2023 turnover and the difficulty of repairing its courts since, Brazil’s prosecution of a former president for a coup plot, and the long entrenchment of elected leaders in Venezuela, Turkey and Russia. [Correction 2026-10-05: the page no longer lists Turkey among leaders beyond electoral reach, since its 2023 presidential runoff was competitive; it names Venezuela, Russia and Nicaragua.] The next tests are close at hand: the United States’ November 2026 midterm elections and the 2028 presidential election, the formation of a government in Saxony-Anhalt and the fate of any AfD ban proceedings, and France’s April 2027 presidential election with Marine Le Pen eligible to run after her appeal ruling of 7 July 2026 reduced her ban from office (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/7/france-appeals-court-opens-door-for-le-pen-presidential-run-with-ankle-tag). Better measurement would help too: indicators that do not depend on expert judgment but can still detect a tilted field, such as media ownership, prosecutions of opposition figures and changes to who controls election administration.

What no data can close: how far a majority’s will should be limited by people it did not elect. A voter who thinks judges, officials and European institutions have taken decisions that belong to elected governments, and a voter who thinks those bodies are the only protection for people who lose elections, can agree on every fact and still disagree about whether a given reform is democratic. So can people who weigh differently the two risks in play: the risk that a popular government entrenches itself if not stopped early, and the risk that stopping it early, by bans, prosecutions of candidates or foreign sanctions, overrides voters in the name of protecting them. Which risk is worse depends on whom one trusts more, one’s fellow voters or the institutions meant to check them, and that is a judgment about values that evidence informs but cannot make.
