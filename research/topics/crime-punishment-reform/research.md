# Crime & Punishment — research

## Overview

This topic is about what justifies punishing people who break the law and what a criminal justice system should be trying to achieve. It covers the main answers in that debate: desert (the guilty deserve to suffer a penalty in proportion to their wrong), deterrence (the threat of punishment prevents crime), incapacitation (people in prison cannot commit crimes outside it), rehabilitation (punishment should change the person who offended), restorative justice (justice means repairing the harm done to victims and communities), and the abolitionist position that prisons, and punishment as the organizing idea of justice, should be replaced altogether. It covers how those answers shape sentence lengths, mandatory minimums and life sentences, prison conditions, parole and “second look” review, and the death penalty, which is the sharpest test of every theory. The debate is centered on the United States, which imprisons a larger share of its people than almost any other country and is the only Western democracy that still carries out executions, but it draws heavily on comparisons with Norway and the other Nordic countries, England and Wales, and El Salvador’s mass imprisonment of suspected gang members. It deliberately excludes how police operate and are held to account (covered in the policing reform topic), whether drugs should be legal (covered in the drug policy topic), firearm regulation (covered in the gun policy topic), and the philosophical question of whether anyone has the free will that moral responsibility requires (covered in the free will topic), though each enters where it bears directly on punishment.

Why now: the United States is executing people at a pace not seen in more than a decade even as public support for the death penalty falls and its largest state empties its death row. On 5 November 2026 California Governor Gavin Newsom announced that he was commuting the death sentences of all 548 people on the state’s death row, writing that “state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California’s values”; about a third of the commutations took effect at once, and the rest, for people with more than one felony conviction, need the concurrence of the California Supreme Court (CalMatters, https://calmatters.org/justice/2026/11/gavin-newsom-death-penalty-commutation/). The Death Penalty Information Center calls it the largest mass commutation in US history, cutting the national death row by nearly 30 percent to about 1,388 people, the fewest since 1984 (https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/news/governor-gavin-newsom-grants-largest-mass-commutation-in-u-s-history-to-death-sentenced-prisoners-in-california); Anne Marie Schubert of the Criminal Justice Legal Foundation called it “atrocious and disrespectful” and pledged a challenge in the state Supreme Court (CalMatters). On 10 November 2026 Gallup reported that 49 percent of Americans favor the death penalty for a person convicted of murder and 45 percent oppose it, the first time support has been below a majority since 1971 and down from 80 percent in 1994 (Gallup, https://news.gallup.com/poll/716483/death-penalty-support-drops-below-majority-level.aspx). At the same time, states carried out 47 executions in 2025, the most since 2009, and 39 more in 2026 through 19 November, 20 of them in Florida (Death Penalty Information Center, https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/executions/2025 and https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/executions/2026). On 30 September 2026 Christa Gail Pike, who would have been the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years, survived two doses of pentobarbital and was taken to a hospital, and Governor Bill Lee halted the state’s remaining execution for the year and ordered “a comprehensive, third-party review to determine exactly what occurred” (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/tennessee-governor-halts-executions-after-christa-gail-pike-survives-lethal-injection-attempt). The Army has set 3 December 2026 to execute Nidal Hasan, who killed 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009, by firing squad, which would be the first US military execution since 1961 (Stars and Stripes, https://www.stripes.com/branches/army/2026-10-06/hasan-firing-squad-execution-23068990.html); on 15 October President Trump overruled Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s plan to livestream it (ABC7, https://abc7news.com/19940656/). Worldwide, Amnesty International recorded 2,707 executions in 2025, the most it has counted since 1981, driven by Iran, and that excludes China’s thousands (https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/05/death-penalty-in-2025-facts-and-figures/). On sentencing more broadly, England and Wales passed the Sentencing Act 2026 on 22 January, making most sentences of 12 months or less suspended by default and tying early release to behavior in prison, which the sentencing minister, Jake Richards, presented as a response to a prison system “bursting at the seams and at breaking point” (Ministry of Justice, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/sentencing-act-ensuring-punishment-cuts-crime-gets-royal-assent), while California voters in November 2024 approved Proposition 36, raising penalties for repeat theft and drug offenses, by 68.4 percent (Berkeley Institute of Governmental Studies, citing the California Secretary of State, https://igs.berkeley.edu/library/california-ballot-proposition-guides/november-5-2024-general-election/november-5-2024-8).

The debate is live because the two halves of the American story point in different directions. US violent crime fell sharply in 2024 and 2025, to a murder rate tied for the lowest the FBI has recorded, while the imprisonment rate has fallen by a quarter in a decade; those who want shorter sentences read this as proof that fewer prisoners need not mean more crime, and those who want firm punishment credit the incapacitation of repeat offenders and warn against loosening further. El Salvador’s homicide rate fell from about 103 per 100,000 in 2015 to an officially reported 1.3 in 2025 after the country imprisoned more than 90,000 people under a state of exception, a result its admirers hold up as proof that incapacitation works and human rights groups describe as purchased with mass arbitrary detention. In the debate are prosecutors and victims’ organizations, crime-control think tanks on the right and decarceration groups on the left, a conservative reform movement that wants less prison for low-risk offenders and more for violent ones, legal philosophers and criminologists who disagree about what punishment is for and what it achieves, religious bodies (the Catholic Church has declared the death penalty “inadmissible”), restorative justice practitioners, and prison abolitionists who reject the premise that punishment produces safety.

## Questions

Question: Should punishment be set by what the offender deserves, or by what will best prevent future crime?
Type: value
Poles: What is deserved / What prevents crime
Why contested: This is the oldest divide in penal philosophy and it still decides concrete cases. A sentence that fits the wrong may be longer than needed to protect anyone (an elderly prisoner who poses no risk) or shorter than prevention would demand (a young, high-risk offender convicted of a minor crime). Most legal systems mix the two, and the argument is over which one governs when they conflict.
Case for “What is deserved”: People who commit crimes have done wrong, and responding to that wrong with a proportionate penalty treats them as responsible agents rather than as hazards to be managed. Desert also protects the guilty: it forbids punishing someone more than their crime warrants because they are thought dangerous, or punishing an innocent scapegoat because it would deter others, which a purely preventive theory cannot rule out on principle. The American Law Institute’s 2017 Model Penal Code: Sentencing adopts this structure, requiring sentences “within a range of severity proportionate to the gravity of offenses, the harms done to crime victims, and the blameworthiness of offenders” and permitting deterrence, incapacitation and rehabilitation only within those limits (Reitz and Klingele, https://www.thealiadviser.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Reitz-Klingele-MPCS-Workable-Limits-CandJ-2019.pdf). Paul Robinson argues that desert is also practical: a criminal law that tracks ordinary people’s shared sense of justice earns the moral credibility that makes them obey it, report crime and cooperate with it (https://scholarship.law.upenn.edu/faculty_scholarship/222).
Case for “What prevents crime”: Punishment deliberately inflicts suffering, and the only thing that can justify doing so is the good it achieves: fewer victims. Desert, on this view, is either a dignified name for retaliation or too vague to guide anyone, since people agree on the rough ranking of crimes but not on how many years each deserves. A system aimed at prevention can be both milder and more protective: it can send a low-risk first offender home with supervision, keep a dangerous one confined, and invest in whatever actually reduces reoffending, from drug treatment to education, instead of in suffering for its own sake. Right on Crime, the conservative reform campaign of the Texas Public Policy Foundation, puts it as “public safety—not punishment for punishment’s sake—must be the central measure of a successful justice system” (https://rightoncrime.com/about/).

Question: At today’s levels of imprisonment, would locking up more people, for longer, reduce crime?
Type: empirical
Poles: Yes, meaningfully / Little or none
Why contested: Everyone agrees that a person in prison cannot commit crimes outside it. The dispute is whether, at the margin where policy now operates, more or longer imprisonment prevents enough crime through incapacitation and deterrence to outweigh the crime it may cause later by cutting people off from work and family and placing them among other offenders. Careful studies reach different answers depending on country, population and what kind of prison stay is measured.
Case for “Yes, meaningfully”: A small share of offenders commits a large share of serious violence, and many of them were free at the time on probation, parole or pretrial release; keeping them confined prevents the crimes they would otherwise commit, which is the case Rafael Mangual of the Manhattan Institute makes for truth-in-sentencing and a points-based three-strikes law (https://manhattan.institute/article/hardening-the-system-three-commonsense-measures-to-help-keep-crime-at-bay). Some of the best evidence on prison’s aftereffects also points this way: a study of Norwegian cases randomly assigned to stricter or more lenient judges found that imprisonment cut the probability of reoffending within five years by 29 percentage points, mostly among people who had not been working before (Bhuller, Dahl, Løken and Mogstad, https://www.nber.org/papers/w22648). El Salvador’s collapse in homicide after mass imprisonment of gang members shows, on this view, that incapacitation at scale works where nothing else has.
Case for “Little or none”: The United States already imprisons at several times the rate of other rich democracies, so the people at today’s margin are less dangerous than those already inside, and their removal prevents less. David Roodman’s 2017 review of the rigorous studies for Open Philanthropy concluded that the best estimate of the net effect of decarceration at typical US policy margins on crime outside prison is about zero: incapacitation benefits are roughly cancelled by the extra crime that longer stays produce after release (https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10268). The National Research Council found in 2014 that the four-decade growth in US imprisonment likely reduced crime but by an uncertain amount that most studies suggest was not large, and urged reducing reliance on incarceration (https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/18613). The fall of US imprisonment by about a quarter since 2013, alongside a murder rate now near its record low, is consistent with that reading.

Question: Does the death penalty deter murder more than life imprisonment does?
Type: empirical
Poles: Deters more / No added effect
Why contested: Deterrence is the most common practical argument for capital punishment, and economists have published estimates both of large effects and of none. The difficulty is that executions are rare, are concentrated in a few states and years, and coincide with many other changes in policing and sentencing, so isolating their effect depends heavily on statistical assumptions.
Case for “Deters more”: People respond to incentives, and the threat of death is the most severe the state can make; it is hard to believe it has no effect on anyone. Several econometric studies of state panel data found that executions are followed by fewer murders, the best known estimating about 18 fewer murders per execution, with a margin of error of 10 (Dezhbakhsh, Rubin and Shepherd, https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/amlawe/v5y2003i2p344-376.html). The executive order the president signed on 20 January 2025 calls capital punishment “an essential tool for deterring and punishing those who would commit the most heinous crimes” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/restoring-the-death-penalty-and-protecting-public-safety/). Even if the evidence is uncertain, supporters argue, the uncertainty cuts both ways: if executions do deter, abolishing them costs innocent lives.
Case for “No added effect”: The National Research Council’s 2012 review concluded that “research to date on the effect of capital punishment on homicide is not informative” about whether it decreases, increases or has no effect on homicide, and recommended that the studies “not be used to inform deliberations” about the death penalty (https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/13363/chapter/2). Reanalyses found that the large deterrence estimates disappear under small, reasonable changes in method (Donohue and Wolfers, https://users.nber.org/~jwolfers/Papers/DeathPenalty%28ALER%29.pdf). The relevant comparison is not death against nothing but death against life imprisonment, executed on average decades after the crime, which is a weak signal to anyone contemplating murder. The broader deterrence literature finds that the certainty of being caught matters far more than the severity of the penalty (National Institute of Justice, https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/five-things-about-deterrence). Most Americans agree: 59 percent told Pew in July 2026 that the death penalty does not deter people from committing serious crimes (https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/08/pp_2026-8-6_death-penalty_report.pdf).

Question: Can a justice system that sometimes convicts the innocent justly put anyone to death?
Type: value
Poles: Yes, for the worst / No, never
Why contested: No one defends executing the innocent; the question is whether a known risk of error rules out the penalty altogether or calls for stronger safeguards. It turns on whether death is different in kind from other punishments, since a mistaken prison sentence can be partly remedied and an execution cannot, and on whether some crimes are grave enough that anything less than death fails to do justice.
Case for “Yes, for the worst”: Every system of punishment makes mistakes, including wrongful imprisonment for decades, and society does not abolish prison for that reason; it demands proof beyond reasonable doubt and a long appeals process, which in capital cases is the most thorough in the law. For a small number of crimes, such as the mass murder of 13 people at Fort Hood, justice requires the gravest penalty, and refusing it tells victims’ families that the worst crime is punished like many lesser ones. The president’s executive order says that “only capital punishment can bring justice and restore order in response to such evil” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/restoring-the-death-penalty-and-protecting-public-safety/). Restricting the penalty to cases with overwhelming evidence, such as DNA or many eyewitnesses, can reduce the risk of error to near zero, and 68 percent of Americans told Pew in July 2026 that the death penalty is morally justified for murder.
Case for “No, never”: Since 1973, 203 people sentenced to death in the United States have been exonerated (Death Penalty Information Center, https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/what-to-know-innocence-and-the-death-penalty), and a careful statistical study estimated that at least 4.1 percent of people sentenced to death are innocent (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24778209/). A state that executes at all will, over time, execute some innocent people, and no compensation is possible afterward. Beyond error, many hold that deliberately killing a restrained, defenseless prisoner violates human dignity whatever he has done; the Catholic Church revised its catechism in 2018 to teach that “the death penalty is inadmissible because it is an attack on the inviolability and dignity of the person” (Holy See Press Office, https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2018/08/02/180802a.html). Life without parole protects the public just as well. By the end of 2025, 145 countries had abolished the death penalty in law or practice.

Question: Would prisons run on Norway’s model produce Norway’s results in countries like the United States?
Type: empirical
Poles: Model transfers / Results don’t transfer
Why contested: Norway runs small prisons designed to resemble ordinary life, with education, work and health services brought in from the community, and has among the lowest reconviction rates in Europe. Whether the model causes those results, or reflects a small, wealthy, low-crime and socially cohesive country, decides whether it is a blueprint or a curiosity.
Case for “Model transfers”: Norway changed its own system deliberately, and its reconviction rate within two years fell from 22 percent for people released in 2016 to 18 percent for those released in 2019, below its Nordic neighbors (Nordic Statistics, https://www.fangelsi.is/media/skjol/Nordic-Statistics-2018_2022_final.pdf). The mechanisms are not specific to Norway: people who leave prison with education, work experience, stable housing and family ties offend less, and the Norwegian judge-assignment study found that prison reduced reoffending largely because it moved people who had not been working into job training and employment programs (https://www.nber.org/papers/w22648). Its correctional service’s principle that “no-one shall serve their sentence under stricter circumstances than necessary for the security in the community” could apply anywhere (https://www.kriminalomsorgen.no/informasjon-paa-engelsk.536003.no.html). With 82 percent of released American state prisoners rearrested within ten years, the current approach is not working.
Case for “Results don’t transfer”: Norway has about 3,000 prisoners and a homicide rate a fraction of America’s, so its prisons hold a different population, mostly people serving short sentences, and its outcomes reflect a strong welfare state, low inequality and high trust that exist outside the prison walls. The comparison flatters Norway because the statistics differ: Norway publishes two-year reconviction, the United States ten-year rearrest. American prisons hold large numbers of people convicted of serious violence, often organized in gangs, for whom open conditions would endanger staff and other prisoners. Spending of the kind Norway makes per prisoner would be hard to justify to voters when victims’ services are underfunded.

Question: How much weight should victims’ wishes carry in deciding punishment?
Type: value
Poles: Victims shape outcome / Public interest decides
Why contested: Modern criminal law treats crime as an offense against the state, prosecuted in the public’s name, with victims as witnesses. Victims’ rights laws, victim impact statements and restorative justice have each given victims more voice, but victims differ: some want the maximum penalty and some want repair, and a system that follows their wishes treats similar crimes differently.
Case for “Victims shape outcome”: The person harmed by a crime has the strongest stake in what happens next, yet the conventional process often leaves victims uninformed and unheard. Restorative justice asks “Who has been hurt? What are their needs? Whose obligations are these?” instead of only “What laws have been broken? Who did it? What do they deserve?” (University of Wisconsin Law School Restorative Justice Project, https://www.law.wisc.edu/fjr/rjp/justice.html). When victims are offered the choice, many take it: in randomized trials, victims who met their offenders in restorative conferences were more satisfied with how their cases were handled and less likely to suffer post-traumatic stress symptoms (Sherman and colleagues, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10940-014-9222-9). Other victims want to be heard in favor of the full penalty, and their voice matters just as much; Right on Crime, for example, calls for “victim-centered policies that elevate the voices of crime victims” (https://rightoncrime.com/about/).
Case for “Public interest decides”: Equal justice requires that the same crime receive similar punishment whoever the victim is; if sentences track victims’ wishes, the forgiving victim’s attacker goes free and the vengeful victim’s goes away for longer, and victims who are poor, unsympathetic or dead have less say. Crime also harms the public, which is why prosecutors, not victims, decide charges. Victims can be heard, informed and compensated without being made sentencers. Restorative processes work only when both sides consent, and the trials showing their benefits included only cases where victims and offenders agreed to meet, so they cannot be the general rule.

Question: Should long prison sentences be reviewed after a fixed number of years, with release possible for people judged no longer dangerous?
Type: value
Poles: Review after years / Sentences should stand
Why contested: Nearly 200,000 Americans are serving life sentences, and many more are serving decades. People tend to commit fewer crimes as they age, which suggests that late years of long sentences do little to protect the public; but a sentence is also a promise to victims and a statement of how grave the crime was.
Case for “Review after years”: People change, and most stop offending as they age; keeping people in prison into their sixties and seventies costs tens of thousands of dollars a year each, with care costs rising, and protects almost no one. The Sentencing Project proposes a universal “second look” review within 10 years of imprisonment and a cap of 20 years on punishment for serious offenses (https://www.sentencingproject.org/about/). The American Law Institute’s Model Penal Code: Sentencing includes a sentence-modification power for very long prison terms that would override mandatory minimums (Reitz and Klingele, https://www.thealiadviser.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Reitz-Klingele-MPCS-Workable-Limits-CandJ-2019.pdf). Almost 70,000 of those serving life were under 25 at the time of their crime, an age at which judgment is still developing.
Case for “Sentences should stand”: A sentence pronounced in open court tells the victim and the public what the crime was worth; revisiting it years later reopens wounds for victims’ families, who must return to argue against release, and turns punishment into a provisional decision managed by parole boards. Predictions that someone is no longer dangerous are fallible, and the cost of error falls on new victims. For the gravest crimes, many hold that a life sentence is what justice requires whatever the person later becomes. Parents of Murdered Children, a national support group for families of homicide victims, runs a Parole Block Program that helps families work to prevent the early release of their loved ones’ killers (Kansas Department of Corrections victim services, https://www.doc.ks.gov/victim-services/links/parents-of-murder-children).

Question: Can communities keep people safe from serious violence without prisons?
Type: empirical
Poles: Alternatives can replace / Prisons remain necessary
Why contested: Abolitionists argue that investment in housing, health, jobs and community-based responses to harm can make prisons unnecessary; their critics argue that a core of dangerous people will always need to be confined. There is evidence on particular alternatives, mostly for less serious offenses or with consenting participants, and almost none on a society without prisons.
Case for “Alternatives can replace”: Prisons have not delivered safety on their own terms: most people released are rearrested, and the communities with the most imprisonment remain the least safe. Ruth Wilson Gilmore argues that “the society that we want to bring into being won’t come into being through a better system of punishment” and that money spent on police, prisons and courts should go to schools, social work and the conditions that prevent harm (The Intercept, https://theintercept.com/2020/06/10/ruth-wilson-gilmore-makes-the-case-for-abolition/). Programs exist that handle even violent felonies without prison when survivors choose them: Common Justice in New York runs what it calls “the first alternative-to-incarceration and victim-service program in the United States” for violent felonies in adult courts (https://www.commonjustice.org/about). Abolition is a direction, not an overnight event, and each step can be tested.
Case for “Prisons remain necessary”: Some people commit repeated serious violence and will not stop when offered help; for them, confinement is the only reliable protection. Alternatives that work, such as restorative conferences, have been tested on consenting participants and modest caseloads, not on the most dangerous offenders. No society of any size has done without some form of secure confinement, and El Salvador’s experience shows how much violence a small number of organized offenders can inflict when they are free. Victims of violence, who are concentrated in poor communities, want protection as well as prevention; to them, removing prisons before alternatives are proven at scale would be an experiment with their safety.

## Positions

Camp: Keep long sentences and the death penalty for serious crime
Adherents: The Trump administration, whose executive order of 20 January 2025, “Restoring the Death Penalty and Protecting Public Safety,” calls capital punishment “an essential tool for deterring and punishing those who would commit the most heinous crimes” and directs federal prosecutors to seek it (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/restoring-the-death-penalty-and-protecting-public-safety/), and whose Defense Department recommended, and the president approved, the firing squad for Nidal Hasan, with the Pentagon’s chief spokesman, Sean Parnell, saying “Judgement day for Hasan has finally come” (Stars and Stripes, https://www.stripes.com/branches/army/2026-10-06/hasan-firing-squad-execution-23068990.html); Rafael A. Mangual of the Manhattan Institute, who argues that incapacitating serious and high-rate offenders prevents the crimes they would otherwise commit and proposes a points-based three-strikes law and truth-in-sentencing rules requiring offenders to serve a minimum share of their terms (https://manhattan.institute/article/hardening-the-system-three-commonsense-measures-to-help-keep-crime-at-bay). The Criminal Justice Legal Foundation, which says it works to ensure criminal law supports public safety and victims’ rights (https://www.cjlf.org/), and Crime Victims United, whose president, Topo Padilla, said of California’s mass commutation, “I don’t think he or any person should undo the sentence rendered by a jury and signed off by a judge” (CalMatters, https://calmatters.org/justice/2026/11/gavin-newsom-death-penalty-commutation/), take the same side. Most Republican officeholders, many prosecutors and sheriffs, and many victims’ families hold this view; California voters approved Proposition 36, raising penalties for repeat theft and drug offenses, with 68.4 percent in November 2024 (https://igs.berkeley.edu/library/california-ballot-proposition-guides/november-5-2024-general-election/november-5-2024-8).
Case: The first duty of a justice system is to protect the innocent from those who would harm them, and the surest protection is to keep people who commit serious violence away from potential victims for as long as their crimes warrant. A small number of repeat offenders commits a large share of the shootings, robberies and rapes; again and again, the person arrested for a terrible crime turns out to have been free on probation, parole or early release despite a long record. Long sentences prevent those crimes directly, and firm, predictable penalties tell would-be offenders that crime will cost them. Punishment is also owed to victims: a murderer who receives a few years has been told his victim’s life was worth little. For the worst crimes, the deliberate killing of children, mass murder, the murder of police officers, justice requires the gravest penalty the law allows, and a society that refuses it fails the dead and their families. Reform experiments of the past decade, from reduced penalties for theft and drugs to shorter terms and lenient prosecutors, produced disorder that voters rejected, even in California.
Evidence: Of state prisoners released in 2008, 82 percent were rearrested within 10 years (Bureau of Justice Statistics, https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/recidivism-prisoners-released-24-states-2008-10-year-follow-period-2008-2018); 62 percent of state prisoners are serving time for a violent offense, so the prison population is mostly people who have hurt others (https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/p23st.pdf); a study of randomly assigned judges in Norway found that imprisonment cut reoffending within five years by 29 percentage points (https://www.nber.org/papers/w22648); econometric studies have estimated that each execution deters several murders, the best known putting it at about 18 (https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/amlawe/v5y2003i2p344-376.html); El Salvador’s official homicide rate fell from about 103 per 100,000 in 2015 to 1.3 in 2025 after mass imprisonment of gang members (InSight Crime, https://insightcrime.org/news/insight-crime-2025-homicide-round-up/); 68 percent of Americans told Pew in July 2026 that the death penalty is morally justified for murder (https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/08/pp_2026-8-6_death-penalty_report.pdf).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that imprisonment at American levels no longer reduces crime at the margin, the camp answers that the margin that matters is not the average prisoner but the high-rate violent offender, and that the studies finding little effect mostly measure lower-level offenders; targeting long sentences on repeat violence captures most of the benefit. To the risk of executing the innocent, it answers that exonerations show the appeals process working, that the penalty can be limited to cases where guilt is beyond any doubt, and that life imprisonment carries its own risk of error without anyone proposing to abolish it. To the charge that harsh punishment falls disproportionately on Black Americans, it answers that the victims of violent crime are disproportionately Black too, and that releasing offenders into the neighborhoods they victimized is no favor to those neighborhoods. To the success of Norway, it answers that Norway imprisons few people because it has little serious crime, not the other way round.
Variation: Adherents differ on the death penalty and on how far to go. Some who back long sentences for violence oppose capital punishment, often on grounds of cost or error, among them some conservatives in the reform movement. Most in the camp support due process and reject El Salvador’s suspension of constitutional rights, citing its outcome only as evidence that incapacitation works; a minority holds it up as a model. Some emphasize deterrence and others incapacitation; some favor mandatory minimums and others leave discretion to judges with higher ranges.

Camp: Cut imprisonment and invest in rehabilitation
Adherents: The Sentencing Project, which “advocates for effective and humane responses to crime that minimize imprisonment and criminalization of youth and adults,” calls for a cap of 20 years on punishment for serious offenses and a universal “second look” review within 10 years of imprisonment, and opposes life without parole (https://www.sentencingproject.org/about/); the Norwegian Correctional Service, whose principle of normality holds that “the punishment is the restriction of liberty; no other rights have been removed by the sentencing court” and that offenders “shall be placed in the lowest possible security regime” (https://www.kriminalomsorgen.no/informasjon-paa-engelsk.536003.no.html); the Catholic Church, whose catechism since 2018 calls the death penalty “inadmissible” and commits the Church to its abolition worldwide (https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2018/08/02/180802a.html). California Governor Gavin Newsom, who commuted all 548 of the state’s death sentences in November 2026, writing that “state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California’s values” (CalMatters, https://calmatters.org/justice/2026/11/gavin-newsom-death-penalty-commutation/), and many Democratic officeholders, public defenders, criminologists and civil liberties organizations hold this view, and a conservative wing of it is organized as Right on Crime (https://rightoncrime.com/about/).
Case: The United States imprisons people at several times the rate of other democracies without being safer, and the extra imprisonment buys little: at today’s levels the people at the margin are less dangerous, long sentences keep people locked up long after they would have stopped offending, and prison itself often makes people more likely to reoffend by severing them from work and family. Almost everyone in prison will come home, so the question is what kind of neighbor they will be. Punishment should therefore be the least severe that protects the public, served in conditions that prepare people for release, with education, treatment and work, followed by real help with housing and jobs. The certainty of being caught deters far more than the length of a sentence, so money moved from long terms to solving crimes and preventing them buys more safety. The death penalty should go: it does not demonstrably deter, it has condemned innocent people, it is imposed unevenly, and life imprisonment protects the public as well.
Evidence: David Roodman’s 2017 review concluded that the net effect on crime of decarceration at typical US policy margins is about zero (https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10268); the National Research Council found in 2014 that the quadrupling of US imprisonment brought an uncertain and probably modest reduction in crime and urged reducing reliance on incarceration (https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/18613); the National Institute of Justice summarizes the research as finding that “the chance of being caught is a vastly more effective deterrent than even draconian punishment” (https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/five-things-about-deterrence); the US imprisonment rate fell 25 percent from 2013 to 2023 (https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/p23st.pdf) while the murder rate fell to 4.1 per 100,000 in 2025, tied for the lowest on record (FBI, https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/fbi-releases-2025-reported-crimes-in-the-nation-statistics); Norway’s two-year reconviction rate was 18 percent for people released in 2019 (https://www.fangelsi.is/media/skjol/Nordic-Statistics-2018_2022_final.pdf); 203 people sentenced to death in the United States have been exonerated since 1973 (https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/what-to-know-innocence-and-the-death-penalty).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that releasing people endangers the public, the camp answers that it distinguishes by risk: it supports confining people who remain dangerous, and its proposals for second-look review release people only after a judge finds they no longer pose a threat, typically in middle age when offending has fallen steeply. To the Norwegian study showing prison reduced reoffending, it answers that the effect came from Norway’s prisons, with job training and short stays, which is precisely the kind of prison it wants; American prisons do not produce that result. To the charge that its ideas produced disorder, it answers that the crime rise of 2020 and 2021 occurred nationwide, in states with and without reforms, and that crime has since fallen to historic lows while imprisonment remained well below its peak. To El Salvador, it answers that a homicide decline bought with more than 90,000 detentions, hundreds of deaths in custody and suspended courts is not a policy a democracy can adopt.
Variation: Adherents differ on how far to go and why. Right on Crime, the campaign of the Texas Public Policy Foundation, holds that “public safety—not punishment for punishment’s sake—must be the central measure of a successful justice system,” favors reducing reoffending among nonviolent, low-risk offenders, reentry programs and record-clearing, but also calls for incapacitating “violent and career criminals”; its statement of principles does not address the death penalty (https://rightoncrime.com/about/). Groups like The Sentencing Project want reform to reach sentences for violent crimes, since most prisoners are serving time for violence. Some in the camp, often called decarceration advocates, aim to cut the prison population by half or more; others seek more modest changes. Restorative justice practitioners overlap with this camp but ground their case in repairing harm rather than rehabilitation.

Camp: Punish in proportion to what the crime deserves
Adherents: The American Law Institute, whose Model Penal Code: Sentencing, approved in 2017, makes proportionality the governing purpose of sentencing, requiring sentences “within a range of severity proportionate to the gravity of offenses, the harms done to crime victims, and the blameworthiness of offenders,” allowing rehabilitation, deterrence and incapacitation only “within the boundaries of proportionality,” and requiring sentences “no more severe than necessary” (text quoted in Kevin R. Reitz and Cecelia M. Klingele, Crime and Justice, 2019, https://www.thealiadviser.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Reitz-Klingele-MPCS-Workable-Limits-CandJ-2019.pdf); Paul H. Robinson of the University of Pennsylvania Law School, who argues that the criminal law gains compliance by earning moral credibility with the community it governs and that sentences should track the community’s shared judgments of desert, which he calls empirical desert (https://scholarship.law.upenn.edu/faculty_scholarship/222). Many legal philosophers and judges, and the sentencing laws of several European countries, which set punishment by the seriousness of the offense, reflect this view.
Case: Punishment is justified because people who commit crimes deserve it, and it is limited by the same principle: no one may be punished more than the crime deserves, however useful that might be. This view takes both crime and the criminal seriously. It condemns the wrong done to victims with a penalty that reflects its gravity, and it treats the offender as a responsible person who is answerable for his choices, not as a dangerous object to be managed or a patient to be treated until officials judge him cured. The two great failures of modern sentencing came from abandoning desert: the rehabilitative era gave parole boards open-ended power to hold people for years based on predictions, and the era of mass incarceration added decades to sentences through three-strikes laws and mandatory minimums in the name of deterrence and incapacitation, giving life terms for shoplifting. Proportionality rules out both. It also keeps the law credible: when punishments match ordinary people’s sense of what crimes deserve, people respect and obey the law, report crimes and serve as witnesses; when sentences seem either cruel or lenient, they stop cooperating.
Evidence: The Model Penal Code’s reporters describe its framework as “utilitarianism within limits of proportionality,” a structure designed to curb disproportionate severity and enforceable in appellate review (https://www.thealiadviser.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Reitz-Klingele-MPCS-Workable-Limits-CandJ-2019.pdf); even Christopher Slobogin and Lauren Brinkley-Rubinstein, testing Robinson’s theory skeptically, found relatively strong consensus on the ranking of traditional crimes by seriousness, though weaker agreement on the punishments they deserve (https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/items/bf664e1d-4087-43ef-ba5f-b552fe270cf2); 68 percent of Americans call the death penalty morally justified for murder while 59 percent say it does not deter, which suggests most supporters reason from desert rather than prevention (Pew, https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/08/pp_2026-8-6_death-penalty_report.pdf); nearly 200,000 Americans are serving life sentences, 56,245 of them without parole (The Sentencing Project, https://www.sentencingproject.org/press-releases/locked-away-for-life-new-report-from-the-sentencing-project-unveils-alarming-data-on-long-term-imprisonment/).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that desert is vengeance in philosophical dress, the camp answers that vengeance is personal, unlimited and indifferent to guilt, while desert is imposed by a neutral court, only on the guilty, and only up to a limit, which is what makes it the best protection against excess. To the objection that people agree on which crimes are worse but not on how many years each deserves, it answers that a sentencing system needs only a consistent scale anchored by public deliberation, which sentencing commissions already build. To those who want punishment set purely by prevention, it answers that prevention alone would justify punishing the innocent if it deterred others, or holding the harmless indefinitely if they were predicted dangerous, which almost everyone rejects. To abolitionists, it answers that ordinary people overwhelmingly believe serious wrongs deserve a response and that a justice system that refuses to give one invites private revenge.
Variation: Adherents split on the death penalty. Some retributivists, following Kant, hold that murder deserves death; many others hold that desert sets a maximum but not a requirement, so the state may choose milder penalties, and that the risk of executing the innocent forbids death even where it is deserved. They also differ on how much room to leave for prevention: strict retributivists would set punishment by desert alone, while the Model Penal Code’s “limiting” approach, after Norval Morris, lets judges pursue rehabilitation or incapacitation within a deserved range. On sentence length, this camp often sides with the decarceration camp against long terms for minor repeat offenses, and with the firm-punishment camp against very short terms for grave violence.

Camp: Abolish prisons and build community safety instead
Adherents: Critical Resistance, which “seeks to build an international movement to end the prison industrial complex,” holds that the system is not broken but works as designed, will not support reforms that extend its life or scope, and works toward communities that address harm without imprisonment or punishment (https://criticalresistance.org/mission-vision/); Ruth Wilson Gilmore, geographer and co-founder of Critical Resistance, who says “the society that we want to bring into being won’t come into being through a better system of punishment” and argues for moving money from police, prisons and courts to schools, social work and other foundations of well-being (The Intercept, https://theintercept.com/2020/06/10/ruth-wilson-gilmore-makes-the-case-for-abolition/). Organizers such as Mariame Kaba, the scholar and activist Angela Davis, and many grassroots groups working on transformative justice share this view.
Case: Prisons do not produce safety; they produce harm. They take people already damaged by poverty, abuse and neglect, subject them to violence and isolation, and return them more damaged to the same neighborhoods, which is why most are rearrested. The communities most heavily policed and imprisoned are not the safest but among the least safe, because what actually prevents violence, stable housing, health care, decent jobs, good schools and strong relationships, is what imprisonment strips away. The system also falls hardest on the poor and on Black, Latino and Native Americans, as it has since it grew out of the control of freed slaves after the Civil War. Reform has been tried for two centuries and each generation’s reforms became the next generation’s scandal. The question is not how to punish better but how to address harm and prevent it: by investing in the conditions of safety, by responding to violence through community accountability processes that center the people harmed, and by shrinking police, prisons and surveillance step by step until they are no longer needed. Abolition is not only tearing down but building.
Evidence: Of state prisoners released in 2008, 82 percent were rearrested within 10 years (https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/recidivism-prisoners-released-24-states-2008-10-year-follow-period-2008-2018); Black adults are imprisoned at five times the rate of White adults (https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/p23st.pdf); since 1973, 203 people sentenced to death have been exonerated, with official misconduct in 70.5 percent of the cases (https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/what-to-know-innocence-and-the-death-penalty); in El Salvador, at least 470 people have died in state custody under the state of exception (Amnesty International, https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/07/el-salvador-el-costo-humano-del-regimen-de-excepcion-podria-constituir-crimenes-de-lesa-humanidad/); the National Research Council found no strong evidence that the growth of US imprisonment produced large reductions in crime (https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/18613); Common Justice in New York handles violent felonies through survivor-centered accountability without incarceration when survivors choose it (https://www.commonjustice.org/about).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the question “what about murderers and rapists?”, the camp answers that today’s system already fails victims of serious violence, many of whose cases are never solved, and that prison has not made rape or murder rare; abolition asks what would actually prevent such harms and help survivors heal, and that question cannot be answered by a system designed around punishment. It concedes that abolition is a long project and that some form of separation may be needed for some people along the way, but holds that such measures should be guided by safety and care, not punishment. To the charge that abolition is utopian, it answers that every major change in how societies treat people, from the end of slavery to the end of public executions, was called utopian until it happened, and that each step it proposes, from ending cash bail to closing a jail and funding services, can be tested on its own.
Variation: Adherents differ on pace and on reform. Critical Resistance says it will not support work that extends the system’s life or scope, and abolitionists following Gilmore distinguish “non-reformist reforms” that shrink the system, such as closing prisons or cutting budgets, from reforms that legitimize or expand it, such as building new jails described as humane. Some abolitionists accept a transitional role for secure facilities for a small number of people; others reject any such framework. Most focus on the United States; some apply the analysis globally. The camp overlaps with the restorative justice camp in practice, through transformative justice processes, but rejects working inside the criminal legal system where restorative practitioners often accept it.

Camp: Repair harm through restorative justice
Adherents: The University of Wisconsin Law School’s Restorative Justice Project, which describes restorative justice as “a set of principles and practices that create a different approach to dealing with crime,” focused on the harm a crime causes, repairing it and holding the person responsible accountable, and contrasts the questions “What laws have been broken? Who did it? What do they deserve?” with “Who has been hurt? What are their needs? Whose obligations are these?” (https://www.law.wisc.edu/fjr/rjp/justice.html); Common Justice in New York, which aims to “build practical strategies to hold people accountable for harm” and runs what it calls “the first alternative-to-incarceration and victim-service program in the United States” for violent felonies in adult courts (https://www.commonjustice.org/about). The criminologist Howard Zehr, whose framework these programs use, New Zealand’s youth justice system of family group conferences, and many victim-offender mediation programs inside police, courts and prisons share this view.
Case: Crime is first a violation of people and relationships, and only secondarily of the state’s law. The conventional process asks which law was broken and what the offender deserves, then hands the answer to strangers in a courtroom; the person harmed is reduced to a witness, and the person responsible never has to face what he did. Justice should instead ask who was hurt, what they need and who is obliged to meet those needs. In a restorative process, with the consent of both sides, the person harmed can tell the offender what the crime did to them, ask the questions only he can answer, and help decide what he must do to make things right, from restitution to work to changes in his life. That is a more demanding form of accountability than sitting in a cell, and the evidence shows it helps victims more and reduces reoffending modestly at low cost. Prison may remain for people who refuse responsibility or remain dangerous, but it should not be the default response to harm.
Evidence: A review of ten randomized trials of restorative conferences found modest reductions in repeat offending over two years, an estimated 3.7 to 8.1 times as much benefit as cost in the UK trials, and higher victim satisfaction, more apologies and fewer post-traumatic stress symptoms among victims (Sherman, Strang and colleagues, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10940-014-9222-9); Common Justice reports using this approach for violent felonies in adult courts in New York as an alternative to incarceration, with survivors’ consent (https://www.commonjustice.org/about), and Harvard’s Wiener Center describes such restorative and transformative processes as able to “promote safety and disrupt cycles of violence while reducing or eliminating reliance on incarceration” (https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/wiener/programs/criminaljustice/news-events/abolition-restorative-justice); 73 percent of Americans see some risk of wrongful conviction in even the gravest cases (Pew, https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/08/pp_2026-8-6_death-penalty_report.pdf).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that restorative justice is soft on crime, the camp answers that facing the person you harmed and making amends is harder than serving time in silence, and that the randomized evidence shows it reduces reoffending, which prison often does not. To the concern that it pressures victims, it answers that participation is voluntary for victims and that it is offered only when they choose it. To the objection that it cannot handle the most serious crimes, it answers that several of the randomized trials involved robbery and violent offenses, and that programs like Common Justice show it can be used for violent felonies when survivors want it; it accepts that it will not fit every case. To abolitionists who see it as a reform that props up the system, it answers that people harmed today cannot wait for the system to disappear.
Variation: Adherents differ on how central restorative justice should be. Some, including many courts and police services, use it as a supplement to conventional sentencing, for youth offenses or as diversion for minor crimes; others, like Common Justice, use it for violent felonies as a full alternative to prison; others still want it to replace punishment as the organizing principle of the system, a position that shades into abolition. Faith-based groups such as Prison Fellowship International run restorative programs inside prisons alongside conventional sentences, notably the Sycamore Tree Project, in which victims meet groups of unrelated offenders (https://my.pfi.org/ministry-resources/open-programs/sycamore-tree-project-new-leaf).

## Crux

Crux: Is punishment justified by the wrong already done or by the harm it prevents, and how much harm do prison, long sentences and the death penalty actually prevent compared with the alternatives?

Evidence could settle: The size of the practical trade-offs. That means better estimates of how much crime longer sentences prevent at today’s margins through incapacitation and deterrence, measured separately for high-rate violent offenders and for everyone else; how much prison raises or lowers reoffending after release, and under what conditions, since studies in Norway and the United States point in different directions; whether the death penalty deters murder beyond life imprisonment, a question the National Research Council found the existing studies could not answer; how often innocent people are convicted and sentenced to death; whether restorative conferences, Norway-style prisons, second-look release and community-based alternatives produce their results at the scale of a whole American state and for serious violence rather than in selected cases; and how much of El Salvador’s fall in homicide came from mass imprisonment rather than from earlier truces, emigration or changes in how deaths are counted. Settling these would move many people, because each camp makes predictions about them: the firm-punishment camp predicts that shorter sentences bring more victims, the decarceration camp that they do not, and the restorative and abolitionist camps that alternatives can deliver safety the prison does not.

Value choice: What punishment is for, and what it may cost. One view holds that the guilty deserve to suffer in proportion to their wrong, that justice for the victim and the community requires it whether or not it prevents anything, and that for the gravest crimes nothing short of death or lifelong imprisonment is adequate. A second holds that deliberately inflicting suffering is justified only by the harm it prevents, so any punishment beyond what protects the public is waste or cruelty. A third holds that justice means repairing harm and restoring relationships, with punishment a last resort. A fourth rejects punishment as the foundation of safety altogether. Each also weighs differently the two kinds of error the system makes: punishing too harshly, which falls on people convicted and sometimes on the innocent, and punishing too leniently, which falls on future victims. No data can say how many years a robbery deserves, whether the state may ever kill a prisoner, or how much risk to future victims is an acceptable price for releasing someone who may have changed.
