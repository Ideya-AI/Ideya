# Birth-Rate Decline — research

## Overview

This topic is about sub-replacement fertility — countries where women, on current patterns, will have fewer than about 2.1 children each, the level at which each generation roughly replaces the one before it without migration — and about what, if anything, governments should do in response. It covers the size and spread of the decline, why it is happening, what it means for aging, public finances and the workforce, and the record of policies meant to raise birth rates: baby bonuses and child allowances, parental leave, childcare, housing help for young families, tax breaks for mothers, marriage incentives, fertility treatment, and the cultural campaigns that accompany them. It also covers the main alternatives to raising births: adapting pensions and labor markets, relying on immigration, and treating lower fertility as acceptable or welcome. It deliberately excludes the separate argument about the optimal size of the world’s population and whether humanity as a whole should be larger or smaller, which belongs to Population Growth & Decline: The Optimum; it touches that argument only where environmental views shape a camp’s answer to national birth-rate policy. Immigration appears here only as a response to low fertility, not as a topic in its own right.

Why now: on 23 September 2026 South Korea’s Ministry of Data and Statistics reported that 24,275 babies were born in July, up 11.1 percent from a year earlier and the 25th consecutive month of year-on-year gains, with 170,079 births in January to July, up 14.7 percent (Korea Times, https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/society/20260923/births-up-11-in-july-extending-gains-to-25th-consecutive-month). A month earlier, on 26 August 2026, the ministry’s final figures put Korea’s 2025 total fertility rate at 0.80, up from 0.75 in 2024 and a record low of 0.72 in 2023 (Korea Times, https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/society/20260826/koreas-births-rise-67-to-254300-in-2025). The country that became the symbol of the problem is now the test case for whether it can be reversed. A June 2026 analysis by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs found that higher fertility among married women in their thirties was the largest single contributor to the rise in births in 2024, but that a temporarily larger cohort of women aged 30 to 34 also helped and will shrink after 2030 (Seoul Economic Daily, https://en.sedaily.com/society/2026/06/17/koreas-birth-rate-rebounds-but-driven-by-demographics). Elsewhere the direction is still down: Japan’s 2025 fertility rate fell to a record 1.14 (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, June 2026), China recorded 7.92 million births in 2025 against 9.54 million in 2024 (National Bureau of Statistics, January 2026), France recorded more deaths than births in 2025 for the first time since the end of the Second World War (INSEE, January 2026), and Hungary’s fertility rate fell to 1.31 in 2025 after a decade of heavy family spending under a government that lost power in April 2026.

The debate is live because almost every rich country and a growing number of middle-income ones are now below replacement, and because governments are acting on it. The United States launched Trump Accounts on 4 July 2026, a $1,000 federal deposit for eligible children born from 2025 to 2028 (NPR, https://www.npr.org/2026/07/06/nx-s1-5880466/government-launches-trump-accounts-offering-1-000-to-eligible-babies); China began paying a national childcare subsidy of 3,600 yuan a year per child under three in 2025; the Heritage Foundation published a family-policy blueprint built around marriage and birth rates in January 2026. The participants are governments across East Asia and Europe, a new pronatalist movement in the United States spanning social conservatives, parts of the technology industry and some progressive economists, demographers and UN agencies who argue the goal should be reproductive choice rather than a target, economists who argue societies should adapt to fewer births rather than fight them, and environmental and reproductive-rights groups who see lower fertility as a good outcome and pronatalism as a threat.

Recent measures include a 6+6 parental-leave scheme that pays the second parent close to full wages, doubled paid paternity leave, and priority housing and cheaper mortgages for newlyweds and new parents. Whether the rebound reflects those policies, a post-pandemic marriage catch-up, or the larger early-1990s birth cohort now in its thirties is the central argument about it.

A systematic review of 35 quasi-experimental studies from Europe, North America and Australia found that effects of cash transfers are likely transitory, mostly shifting when births happen, while large reforms of public childcare and parental leave had lasting and substantial effects (Bergsvik, Fauske and Hart, Population and Development Review, 2021, summarized at https://www.population-europe.eu/research/policy-insights/can-policies-stall-fertility-fall).

## Questions

Question: Can government policy raise fertility by a meaningful amount, for long enough, at a cost a country would accept?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Policy can move it / Effects stay small
Why contested: Every serious study finds some effect from family policy, and none finds an effect large enough to return a low-fertility country to replacement. The argument is about whether the modest effects add up to something worth paying for, and whether they change how many children people have or only when they have them.
Case for policy can move it: The quasi-experimental evidence is consistent that money and services change behavior. Quebec’s baby bonus raised the probability of a birth by 10.7 to 25.0 percent depending on birth order; Poland’s Family 500+ raised the annual probability of a birth by 1.5 percentage points in the short run; Hungary’s fertility rose by about a third between 2011 and 2021 while it built the most generous family package in Europe; and a systematic review of 35 studies found lasting and substantial effects from large childcare and parental-leave reforms. The failures are failures of scale and design, not of principle: most countries spend a small share of what it costs to raise a child, and a benefit worth a few percent of that cost should be expected to move fertility a few percent. Korea’s rebound since 2024, with births rising 25 months in a row after the country began paying the second parent near-full wages on leave and prioritizing young families for housing, suggests that concentrated effort can turn a trend. Proposals like Lyman Stone’s large birth-linked savings accounts are designed around the elasticities the literature actually measures.
Case for effects stay small: The countries that tried hardest are the best evidence against. Korea spent a sum reported at more than 360 trillion won between 2006 and 2023 while fertility fell from about 1.1 to 0.72, and much of its recent rise tracks a larger cohort of women in their thirties and a post-pandemic marriage catch-up. Hungary’s gains reversed after 2021 and its rate was 1.31 in 2025, below where it stood in 2015; Poland is lower than before Family 500+; France and the Nordic countries, which built the model family-policy systems, are falling too. The systematic review finds cash effects are likely transitory, mostly moving births earlier rather than adding them, and they are largest among better-off families who need help least. Each additional birth bought this way costs tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars, and the UN notes that in countries already shrinking the number of women of childbearing age is falling so fast that any policy’s effect on population size diminishes over time.

Question: Is low fertility mainly the result of barriers that stop people having the children they want, or of preferences that have genuinely changed?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Barriers block wants / Wants have changed
Why contested: The answer decides what kind of problem this is. If people want more children than they have, removing obstacles is uncontroversial and should raise fertility. If people simply want fewer, closing the gap would do little, and raising fertility would mean changing what people want.
Case for barriers block wants: People consistently say they want about two children or more. Americans put the ideal family at 2.7 children in 2025 against actual fertility near 1.6; in UNFPA’s 14-country survey two was the most commonly desired number everywhere, 31 percent of people over 50 said they ended up with fewer children than they wanted, and the most common reason given for having fewer was money, cited by 39 percent of those who had or wanted children, followed by job insecurity. Housing costs, the length of education, late partnering and the difficulty of combining two careers with small children all push first births later, and later first births mean fewer second and third ones and more involuntary childlessness as fertility falls with age. Korea’s surge in births among married women in their thirties after marriages recovered shows that when the path to family formation reopens, people take it.
Case for wants have changed: Stated ideals are cheap and measured behavior is not. Gallup asks about an ideal for families in general, not about the respondent’s own plans, and when Pew asked American non-parents under 50 directly, 47 percent said they were unlikely ever to have children and 57 percent of those said a major reason was simply not wanting to, well ahead of affordability at 36 percent. Kearney, Levine and Pardue found that measurable economic and policy changes explain little of the US decline since 2007, which they attribute to shifting priorities. The fall is also greatest in the richest, most secure and most gender-equal societies, and fertility has fallen as incomes rose, which is hard to square with a story about affordability. On this reading, careers, leisure, travel, higher standards for what a child needs, and the loosening of religious and social expectations have changed what people want, and that is a durable feature of modern life rather than a friction to be removed.

Question: How much economic harm does a shrinking, aging population do once a country adapts?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Serious lasting drag / Manageable with adaptation
Why contested: No modern economy has lived through decades of steep population decline, so the evidence is projections, cross-country comparisons and a few early cases such as Japan. Both sides agree aging raises pension and health costs; they disagree about whether higher productivity, later retirement and more women and older people in work can absorb them.
Case for serious lasting drag: The arithmetic is severe. The OECD projects the working-age population of the OECD to fall 13 percent over four decades and by more than 35 percent in Italy, Korea, Poland and others, and Korea to go from 29 to 85 people over 65 per 100 of working age by 2054. Pay-as-you-go pensions and health systems depend on the ratio of workers to retirees, and there is a limit to how far retirement ages can rise or benefits can fall before the political system refuses. Beyond public finances, new ideas come from people, and a shrinking, older population has fewer inventors, entrepreneurs and young workers taking risks; economists Dean Spears and Michael Geruso argue in After the Spike that a depopulating world would see progress slow. Japan’s decades of weak growth, an emptying countryside and a shortage of care workers are a preview, and adaptation measures can buy time but cannot change the direction of a population shrinking by a third or more each generation.
Case for manageable with adaptation: Living standards depend on output per person, not on the number of people, and they depend more on the share of people in work and on their productivity than on the age structure alone. IIASA demographers Guillaume Marois and Wolfgang Lutz argue that better-educated, healthier populations with higher labor-force participation can offset and even outweigh the effect of fewer births, and that under plausible conditions low fertility can be economically advantageous because each child receives more investment. Most rich countries still have large untapped labor in older workers and women, retirement ages have already begun to rise with life expectancy, and automation and AI are likely to raise output per worker. Higher fertility would also be a poor fix even on its own terms: new babies are dependents for twenty years before they are workers, so a baby boom now would worsen the dependency ratio through the 2040s.

Question: Should governments set a goal for how many children their citizens have?
Tag: value
Poles: Legitimate collective goal / Private choice only
Why contested: Almost everyone supports helping parents; the dispute is whether a birth-rate target is a proper aim of the state at all. History weighs on both sides, from forced sterilization and one-child policies to Romania’s abortion ban under Ceaușescu, and from Nordic family policy to France’s long-standing pronatalism.
Case for legitimate collective goal: A society has an interest in its own continuation, and the number of children born determines whether pensions are paid, whether towns survive and whether a culture and language carry on. Governments already pursue collective goals that depend on private choices — savings rates, vaccination, education — through incentives rather than commands, and a birth-rate goal pursued through money, housing and time for parents is no different. Parents also produce a public good: the taxpayers who will fund everyone’s old age, including the childless, so there is a straightforward fairness case for transferring more of the cost of children from parents to society. Declining to set a goal is itself a choice, one that lets a country shrink by default.
Case for private choice only: How many children to have is among the most personal decisions a person makes, and once the state treats births as a national output, the pressure tends to fall on women’s bodies and careers. The record of population targets, in both directions, includes coerced sterilization, forced abortion, restricted contraception and bans on abortion. UNFPA’s position is that the goal should not be to engineer fertility rates but to give individuals the information and means to decide freely, and that approaches that do not put choice at the center have repeatedly failed. Support for parents is justified on its own terms — child welfare, fairness, reducing poverty — and should not be judged by whether it raises births; if it does, that is a side effect, and if it does not, it was still worth doing.

Question: Can immigration substitute for the births a country is not having?
Tag: value
Poles: Immigration fills gap / Births must do it
Why contested: The demographic arithmetic is not in dispute: immigration already keeps many rich-country populations from shrinking, and the UN projects it will be the main driver of growth in 52 countries through 2054. The disagreement is whether that is a sustainable and desirable answer, which turns on economics, integration and national identity.
Case for immigration fills gap: Immigration delivers working-age adults immediately, already educated at another country’s expense, while a baby born today will not work for twenty years. It has lowered the dependency ratio noticeably in Canada and Iceland, and CBO projects that from 2030 it will be the only source of US population growth. Much of the world, especially sub-Saharan Africa, will have large young populations for decades, so the global supply of potential migrants is large. Countries that have closed themselves off, such as Japan until recently, show what the alternative looks like. For economists in this camp, a country that wants more workers can have them far more cheaply and quickly through well-managed immigration than through pronatal spending.
Case for births must do it: Immigration postpones rather than solves the problem, because immigrants age too and their fertility tends to converge on that of the host country within a generation, so a country needs ever-rising inflows to hold its age structure steady. The sending countries are themselves heading below replacement: India reached 1.9 in 2023 and Latin America and much of Asia are already there, so the pool is shrinking. Large-scale immigration also raises questions of integration, housing and social trust that voters in many countries have made clear they care about. For Hungary’s government from 2010 to 2026 the choice was explicit: family policy instead of immigration. Many pronatalists who welcome immigration still argue that a society that cannot reproduce itself has a deeper problem that importing workers does not touch.

Question: Is South Korea’s rebound evidence that pronatal policy works, or a temporary echo of a larger generation?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Policy is working / Temporary cohort echo
Why contested: Korea is the extreme case, and its turn since 2024 is the most closely watched demographic data in the world. The same numbers support both readings, and the decomposition that could settle it depends on assumptions about what would have happened otherwise.
Case for policy is working: Births have risen for 25 consecutive months and the monthly fertility rate reached 0.89 in July 2026, well above the 0.72 of 2023. The Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs found that the largest single contributor to the 2024 increase was higher fertility among married women in their thirties, not merely more women of that age. The rise came after a burst of measures: near-full wage replacement for the second parent on leave, doubled paid paternity leave, cheaper mortgages and priority housing for newlyweds and new parents, employer bonuses, and a public campaign that treated low fertility as a national emergency. Marriages were still rising in mid-2026, up 9.3 percent year on year in July, which points to more births ahead rather than a one-off bump.
Case for temporary cohort echo: Women born in the mid-1990s, one of the last relatively large Korean birth cohorts, are now in their early thirties, the peak age for Korean births, and that alone raises births; the same analysis found the cohort effect positive and expects it to reverse after 2030 as much smaller cohorts follow. Marriages postponed during the pandemic were then held, and about 95 percent of Korean births follow marriage, so a marriage catch-up produces a birth catch-up a year or two later. Mothers are older than ever, with 37.3 percent of 2025 births to women 35 or over, which is consistent with postponed births being realized rather than more births overall. At 0.80 in 2025 Korea is still below every other country on record, and Hungary’s decade of gains shows how quickly a rebound can reverse.

Question: Is falling fertility a sign that something has gone wrong in society, or a result of people having more freedom?
Tag: value
Poles: Symptom of failure / Result of freedom
Why contested: The same facts — later marriage, fewer marriages, more childless adults, smaller families — can be read as the collapse of institutions that once made family life possible or as the widening of choice, especially for women. The reading shapes whether the response should be to restore something or to accept an outcome.
Case for symptom of failure: When most people say they want two or more children and a growing share end up with fewer or none, something is failing them. Marriage rates have fallen, young men and women report difficulty finding partners, housing in the places with jobs is unaffordable, and work is organized as if no one had children. The Heritage Foundation and other social conservatives argue that the decline of marriage, religion and community is the root cause, and point to religious communities where larger families remain normal because the culture and civic infrastructure support them. Progressive pronatalists reach a similar diagnosis from the other direction: a society that makes parenting exhausting, expensive and professionally punishing, especially for mothers, has made a choice, and the birth rate is the evidence.
Case for result of freedom: Fertility falls wherever women gain education, contraception, careers and legal equality, and it has fallen furthest in the countries where women’s lives changed fastest. That is not a malfunction but a direct result of people, and especially women, getting to choose, and many who have fewer children or none report being content with that choice. Treating the result as a crisis implies that the old pattern — earlier marriage, more children, fewer options for women — was the healthy baseline. Reproductive-rights and environmental organizations such as Population Matters argue that falling birth rates are a positive result of greater reproductive choice, and warn that a crisis framing invites policies that restrict contraception, abortion or women’s independence.

Question: Should family policy favor marriage and parents caring at home, or gender equality in paid work and care?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Marriage and home care / Equal work and care
Why contested: Pronatalists on the right and the left propose very different packages, and each cites cross-country evidence. The long-standing finding that more gender-equal rich countries had higher fertility has weakened as Nordic fertility has fallen, and East Asia’s very low rates are attributed by some to rigid gender roles and by others to the decline of marriage.
Case for marriage and home care: Married couples have more children in every rich country, and in East Asia almost all births occur within marriage, so the fall in marriage is the fall in births. The Heritage Foundation’s 2026 blueprint therefore proposes removing marriage penalties from benefit programs, a savings grant for couples who marry by 30, a large tax credit per newborn for married parents and a credit for children under five cared for at home. Subsidies that only pay for institutional childcare favor two-earner households and penalize families where one parent stays home, and Lyman Stone and others argue that neutral support such as larger child tax credits respects the arrangements families actually prefer. The Nordic countries, which built the most gender-equal family systems, have seen some of the steepest recent falls in fertility.
Case for equal work and care: The lowest-fertility countries in the world — South Korea, Japan, Italy, Spain — are those where women are expected to do most of the child care and housework while also holding jobs, and where taking time out damages careers. Women there are postponing or forgoing children because the terms on offer are bad. The systematic review of policy effects found the most lasting results from large expansions of public childcare and parental leave, and Korea’s latest measures pay fathers near-full wages to take leave. Policies that push women back into the home run against what most young women want and risk further delaying marriage. The UN recommends a more equal division of caregiving as a response to low fertility for exactly this reason.

## Positions

Camp: Raise birth rates toward replacement
Adherents: Institute for Family Studies, whose Pronatalism Initiative is directed by Lyman Stone, who argues that parents are the ones making Social Security work and proposes larger child tax credits that start in pregnancy, caregiver credits in Social Security and an end to marriage penalties — https://ifstudies.org/blog/pronatal-policy-ideas-for-2025 ; the Heritage Foundation, whose January 2026 report Saving America by Saving the Family treats below-replacement fertility as part of a family crisis and proposes marriage-linked savings accounts, a $17,670 tax credit per newborn for married parents and a $2,000 credit per child under five cared for at home — https://www.heritage.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/SR323.pdf ; also economists Dean Spears and Michael Geruso, authors of After the Spike (2025), who argue that global depopulation would slow human progress and could be hard to stop once under way, and who favor making parenting easier and better supported while rejecting coercion (https://time.com/7299524/birth-rate-after-the-spike-depopulation-interview/); the governments of South Korea and China, and Hungary’s government under Viktor Orbán from 2010 until its defeat in April 2026; and the Trump administration, which launched $1,000 Trump Accounts for children born from 2025 to 2028.
Variation inside the camp: The camp shares the goal and splits on means. Fiscal pronatalists such as Stone want large, simple cash support sized to what the elasticities say it takes. Social conservatives such as Heritage put marriage, religion and support for parents who stay home at the center and doubt that money works without cultural change. Progressive pronatalists such as Spears and Geruso want the goal pursued through paid leave, childcare, housing and gender equality, and would accept stabilizing at a much lower population than today’s. State-led versions in East Asia and Hungary combine cash, housing and campaigns, and Hungary framed family policy explicitly as an alternative to immigration. A technology-minded wing puts more weight on fertility treatment and on removing the biological deadline that postponement creates.
The case in the camp’s own voice: A society in which each generation is a third smaller than the last, or in Korea’s case two thirds smaller, is not on a sustainable path, and no adjustment to retirement ages or immigration quotas changes that. Children are the one input every other public good depends on: the workers who pay pensions, the scientists and founders who produce new ideas, the soldiers, nurses and caregivers of the next generation. And this is not a case of the state imposing preferences on people. Most people say they want two or more children and a large share end up with fewer, because housing is unaffordable, careers punish parents, marriage has become harder to reach, and the financial cost of children has shifted almost entirely onto parents while the benefits are shared by everyone. Closing that gap is both good for families and the only real answer to demographic decline. The policies work in proportion to their size: every credible study finds that money, time and housing for parents raise births, and the reason most countries have not seen a turnaround is that they have spent a small fraction of what raising a child costs. Korea’s 25 straight months of rising births since it began paying fathers to take leave and giving young families priority for homes show that the trend is not destiny.
Best evidence: Quasi-experimental studies consistently find positive effects: Quebec’s baby bonus raised the probability of a birth by 10.7 to 25.0 percent depending on birth order, and Poland’s Family 500+ raised the annual probability of a birth by 1.5 percentage points in the short run. A systematic review of 35 studies found lasting and substantial fertility effects from large reforms of public childcare and parental leave. Hungary’s fertility rose from 1.23 in 2011 to 1.61 in 2021 during its family-policy build-up. Korea’s fertility rose from 0.72 in 2023 to 0.80 in 2025 and to a monthly 0.89 in July 2026, and the largest contributor to the 2024 increase was higher fertility among married women in their thirties. Surveys show a persistent gap between desired and actual family size: Americans put the ideal at 2.7 children, and in UNFPA’s 14-country survey 31 percent of people over 50 said they had fewer children than they wanted.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that the effects are small, temporary and very expensive, and that the countries that tried hardest — Korea, Hungary, Poland, even France — have still fallen. We accept that no country has yet returned to replacement, and that cash alone mostly moves births earlier. But the comparison is wrong: what would Korean or Hungarian fertility be without these policies, given that most comparable countries were falling over the same years? Most programs were small relative to the cost of a child, poorly targeted or unstable, and a policy that people expect to be withdrawn does not change a twenty-year decision. The honest conclusion from the evidence is that pronatal policy must be larger, more durable and aimed at the real barriers — housing, marriage, the timing of careers — not that it is futile. And the alternative on offer, permanent adaptation to a shrinking population, is not a free option; it is a bet that productivity and immigration can carry burdens no society has yet tried to carry.

Camp: Help people have the children they want
Adherents: UNFPA, the UN sexual and reproductive health agency, whose State of World Population 2025 report The Real Fertility Crisis argues that the goal should not be to engineer fertility rates but to give individuals the information and means to decide freely — https://www.unfpa.org/swp2025 ; Karen Benjamin Guzzo, sociologist and director of the Carolina Population Center at the University of North Carolina, who argues that young adults still want children but are waiting for stable conditions, that $5,000 bonuses cannot offset the cost of a child, and that childcare, paid leave and immigration matter more than birth-rate targets — https://www.cpc.unc.edu/news/whats-behind-the-pronatalist-movement-to-boost-the-birth-rate-an-interview-with-cpc-director-karen-guzzo/ ; also the UN Population Division, whose World Population Prospects 2024 recommends that governments wishing to help people achieve their desired family size consider paid parental leave, flexible work, affordable childcare and housing and an equal division of caregiving (https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Files/WPP2024_Summary-of-Results.pdf).
Note: This is the hybrid camp and the one that rejects the question as posed. It supports much of the same family policy as the first camp but denies that the birth rate is the right target: the question it asks is whether people can have the children they want, including fewer or none, not whether the country has enough babies.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The real crisis is not too few or too many babies. It is that people everywhere are unable to have the families they want — too few children for some, too many or too soon for others. Our survey across 14 countries found that nearly one in five adults of reproductive age expect not to reach their desired family size, that nearly one in three people have had an unintended pregnancy, and that money, job insecurity and housing are the most common reasons people have fewer children than they want. Those are real problems with real solutions: affordable childcare, paid leave for both parents, secure work, housing young people can afford, access to fertility care, and a fair division of work at home. They are worth solving for their own sake. What we reject is turning births into a national target. Once a government decides it needs a particular number of children, the history is clear about where the pressure lands — on women, through restrictions on contraception and abortion, stigma for the childless, and policies that push women out of work. Population panics, whether about explosion or collapse, have repeatedly produced coercion, and approaches that do not put choice at the center have repeatedly failed on their own terms.
Best evidence: In the UNFPA/YouGov survey two was the most commonly desired number of children in every one of the 14 countries, and 39 percent of about 10,000 people who had or wanted children cited financial limitations as a reason they had or would have fewer. Unfulfilled wishes run in both directions: 11 percent of reproductive-age adults expected fewer children than they wanted and 7 percent more, and in South Africa 17 percent expected to have more children than they ideally wanted. The systematic review of policy effects found that childcare and health services support families across incomes, while cash transfers have their largest fertility effects among well-off families and mostly shift timing. The UN’s own analysis finds that in countries already shrinking, fertility-raising policies will have diminishing effects on population size, which is a reason to judge family policy by whether it helps families, not by births.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that this is pronatalism without the courage of its convictions: if every one of these measures were adopted, fertility would still be far below replacement, so the camp is either quietly accepting decline or pretending its policies will reverse it. We accept the first half and deny that it is a failure. If people who are able to have the children they want choose, on average, fewer than two, that is a legitimate collective outcome, and societies can plan for it with pensions, labor markets and immigration policy. We make no promise that meeting people’s wishes will return fertility to 2.1; we say it is the only goal a government can pursue without treating its citizens as instruments. And we note that the pronatalist camp itself justifies its policies by the same gap between desired and actual family size — which means that where the gap closes, its mandate ends.

Camp: Adapt to fewer births
Adherents: IIASA, the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis, whose demographers Guillaume Marois and Wolfgang Lutz argued in Nature Human Behaviour in March 2026 that persistent low fertility is likely and may be economically advantageous, and that instead of pushing birth rates back to an arbitrary target governments should adapt social security systems and invest in education and productivity — https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1118228 ; the Niskanen Center, where economist Ed Dolan argues that welfare states built on the premise of perpetual population growth should be reformed for a low-fertility future through targeted immigration, pension and retirement reform and higher labor-force participation rather than pronatal intervention — https://www.niskanencenter.org/social-policy-for-a-low-fertility-future/ ; also the UN Population Division, which advises already-peaked countries to use automation, lifelong learning and multigenerational workforces, and notes that return migration could slow decline faster than fertility policy.
Variation inside the camp: Members differ on how much weight to put on immigration. Some treat managed immigration as the main tool; others, especially in Europe and East Asia, emphasize later retirement, higher employment of women and older workers, and investment in education and automation, with immigration as one input among several.
The case in the camp’s own voice: Low fertility is the normal condition of rich, educated societies, and every serious projection says it will persist. The more a country develops, the lower its fertility tends to be, and even the Nordic countries, long held up as proof that good family policy keeps fertility near replacement, have seen unexpectedly steep falls. Governments can nudge birth rates, but decades of evidence show the effects are modest and costly, and the 2.1 benchmark is an artificial construct that says nothing about whether people are prosperous. What matters for living standards is not how many people there are but how many are working and how productive they are. On that measure rich countries have large reserves: older people who are healthier than ever and can work longer, women still underemployed in much of Europe and East Asia, and technology that is raising output per worker. Smaller cohorts also mean more investment per child. The sensible response is to reform pensions so they do not depend on endless growth, link retirement to life expectancy, invest in education and productivity, admit the migrants an economy needs, and stop spending fortunes chasing a birth-rate target that was never the right measure of a society’s health.
Best evidence: The UN puts the probability that any of 24 ultra-low-fertility, already-shrinking countries returns to 2.1 within thirty years at 0.1 percent, and notes that the pool of women of childbearing age in those countries is shrinking so fast that pronatal policy will have diminishing effects on population size. The OECD finds that over the past decade rising life expectancy has contributed as much as falling fertility to population aging on average, and that immigration has significantly lowered the old-age ratio in Canada and Iceland. Hungary, Poland and Korea spent heavily and remain far below replacement. The dependency arithmetic also favors adaptation in the medium term: a baby born now is a dependent for twenty years, so higher fertility would worsen the ratio of workers to dependents through the 2040s.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that adaptation only manages decline: a population that shrinks by a third or more each generation eventually runs out of adjustments, and no country has shown it can raise productivity fast enough to carry a Korean-style age structure. We do not claim adaptation is painless, and we agree that extremely low fertility such as Korea’s, below one child per woman, is a serious problem. But the question is what policy can realistically change, and at what price. Raising fertility from 1.4 to 1.6 would be counted a triumph by any pronatal program and would barely alter the dependency ratio for two decades, while raising the employment rate of people aged 60 to 70 or adding a point to productivity growth changes it now. Productivity growth has not stopped, and new technologies, including AI, are more likely to accelerate it than slow it. If fertility recovers on its own, adaptation costs nothing; if it does not, adaptation is the only plan that works.

Camp: Welcome smaller populations
Adherents: Population Matters, the UK charity, which argues that people have fewer children when living conditions allow them to choose, that falling birth rates are a positive result of greater reproductive choice, and that aging should be met through productivity, labor-force participation, lifelong learning and pension reform rather than pronatalism — https://populationmatters.org/news/2023/05/pronatalism-in-the-uk/ ; Population Connection, the US organization, which says ending population growth is essential on a finite planet, calls for embracing fertility decline, describes the economic case for pronatalism as resting on the assumption of endless growth, and opposes the Trump administration’s pronatalist measures as a threat to reproductive rights — https://populationconnection.org/learn/pronatalism-in-the-us/ .
The case in the camp’s own voice: Falling birth rates are one of the great successes of the last century, not a crisis. They happened because women gained education, rights and control over their own fertility, and because children stopped dying young. A world of eight billion people consuming at today’s rates is already breaching the planet’s limits on climate, biodiversity and resources, and every country that stabilizes and gently declines eases that pressure while its people become richer, better educated and longer-lived. The pronatalist argument rests on an economic model that needs ever more workers to support ever more retirees, which is a pyramid scheme by construction; the answer is to fix the model, not to demand more babies to feed it. Aging is real and manageable with the tools every aging country is already using, and smaller populations can mean less crowding, cheaper housing, higher wages for scarce workers and more investment in each child. What we oppose most strongly is the drift of pronatalism toward pressure on women, restrictions on contraception and abortion, and stigma for people who choose small families or none.
Best evidence: Fertility has fallen wherever women’s education, legal rights and access to contraception have risen, which supports reading the decline as the outcome of choice. UNFPA’s survey found that unintended pregnancy remains common, reported by nearly one in three respondents, and that fertility aspirations go unmet in both directions, so a policy fixation on too few births ignores half the problem. Countries such as Japan and Italy that have lived with low fertility for decades remain among the richest and longest-lived societies in the world. IIASA demographers find that better-educated smaller populations can sustain high living standards, and the UN notes that even with continued decline the world’s population will keep growing to about 10.3 billion in the 2080s.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that a gentle decline is not what the numbers show: at Italy’s fertility each generation is little more than half the size of the one before, and at Korea’s well under half, and nobody has shown how a society with more pensioners than workers keeps its hospitals staffed and its promises kept. We agree that extremely rapid aging brings real strain and must be planned for, and we support the same adaptations the economists propose: longer working lives, pension reform, investment in care and technology. We also recognize the scope of this debate is national, not planetary, and that a country can be too old even if the world is too large. But the fact that a transition is hard is not a reason to reverse it, least of all by leaning on women to have children they do not want. The difficulties of an aging society are finite and solvable; the difficulties of a growing population on a planet already past its limits are not.

## Crux

The disagreement under the others is whether sustained below-replacement fertility is a problem serious enough to justify governments trying to change how many children their citizens have.

What evidence could settle: Two empirical questions do most of the work. The first is how much lasting harm low fertility does once a country has adapted — raised retirement ages, drawn more women and older people into work, invested in productivity and admitted migrants — and at what fertility level those adjustments stop being enough; the next twenty years in Japan, Korea, Italy and China will provide far better evidence than exists now. The second is what policy can actually buy: how many additional births, for how long and at what cost per birth, and how much of today’s low fertility reflects barriers that stop people having the children they want rather than changed preferences. Korea’s rebound, as the post-2030 cohort decline arrives, will be the most important single test. If adaptation proves cheap and pronatal policy weak, the case for targets collapses; if adaptation fails at very low fertility and well-designed policy proves able to close most of the gap between desired and actual family size, the case for acting becomes strong for all but those who welcome decline.

What is a value choice: No data can settle whether a nation’s size and continuity is a collective good that the state may pursue by shaping private decisions about children, or whether the number of children is purely each person’s business and the state’s role ends at making their choices possible. Nor can data settle how much weight to give future generations, the survival of particular languages and cultures, fairness between parents and the childless, the environmental benefits of fewer people, or the risk that a birth-rate target turns into pressure on women. People who agree on every projection can still divide on these.
