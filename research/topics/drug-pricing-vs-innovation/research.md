# Drug Pricing — research

Slug: drug-pricing-vs-innovation
Scope: Medicine prices against the profits that fund new-drug research.
Researched: 2026-11-23

## Overview

This topic is about what new medicines should cost and who should pay for them, set against the fact that most drug development is paid for by companies that expect to earn it back through high prices during a period of patent protection and regulatory exclusivity. It covers how prices for patented brand-name drugs are set: by company list prices and confidential rebates in the United States, by Medicare negotiation under the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act, by references to prices in other countries (“most favored nation” pricing), by health technology assessment and cost-effectiveness thresholds in countries such as the United Kingdom, and by trade pressure on other governments. It covers the evidence on what drug development costs, how strongly the number of new drugs responds to expected revenue, and the alternatives that would fund research some other way, such as public funding, prizes and nonprofit developers. It deliberately leaves aside the broader question of who should insure health care (the universal-healthcare topic), the specific debate over weight-loss drugs (obesity-drugs-medicalization), antibiotic incentives (antimicrobial-resistance) and antitrust in general (antitrust-market-power), touching each only where it bears on how drug prices relate to innovation. The debate is global, because one country’s prices fund research whose results reach every country, but its center of gravity is the United States, which pays the highest prices and supplies most of the industry’s revenue.

The why-now event: on September 30, 2026 the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services finalized the GLOBE model, the first mandatory Medicare program that ties what drugmakers owe to prices in other wealthy countries. It applies to certain Part B drugs (those given in doctors’ offices and clinics) in randomly selected areas covering about a quarter of people in Original Medicare, starts January 1, 2027, and runs to March 31, 2032 (CMS press release, https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/cms-finalizes-new-mandatory-drug-payment-model-deliver-lower-drug-prices-beneficiaries-original). The final rule was much narrower than the December 2025 proposal: CMS now projects savings of about $440 million over seven years against the $11.9 billion it projected for the proposal (Skadden, October 2026, https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/10/cms-finalizes-the-globe-model), after exclusions for orphan-only drugs, plasma-derived products and some cell and gene therapies, and a waiver for companies taking part in the administration’s Medicaid pricing model; by CMS’s own estimate only four manufacturers of in-scope drugs remain in it (Gibson Dunn, October 14, 2026, https://www.gibsondunn.com/the-globe-final-rule-a-narrow-model-that-may-be-a-bellwether-for-most-favored-nation-pricing/). The drug industry’s trade group, PhRMA, sued to block it on October 7, 2026 (AJMC, October 8, 2026, https://www.ajmc.com/view/phrma-sues-to-block-cms-most-favored-nation-model-for-part-b). A companion Part D model, GUARD, was still under review at the Office of Management and Budget in mid-October. GLOBE sits inside a larger push. A May 12, 2025 executive order directed agencies to bring US prices to the lowest level paid in comparable rich countries and to act against foreign “freeloading” (White House, https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/05/delivering-most-favored-nation-prescription-drug-pricing-to-american-patients/). A proclamation of April 2, 2026 put 100 percent tariffs on imported patented medicines, effective July 31, 2026 for 17 large companies and September 29 for the rest, with a zero rate for companies that had both a US manufacturing plan and a most-favored-nation pricing agreement (Arnold & Porter, April 2026, https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/04/trade-winds-shift-for-pharma). By September 18, 2026 the White House said 26 drugmakers covering 90 percent of the branded US market had signed such agreements, whose terms are largely confidential (White House, https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/09/president-trump-extends-most-favored-nation-pricing-to-every-medicaid-program-in-america/). Two older tracks continue. The first Medicare-negotiated prices, for 10 drugs, took effect January 1, 2026; the second set, for 15 drugs including Ozempic and Wegovy, takes effect in 2027; and CMS must publish the third set, for 15 drugs including the first from Part B, by November 30, 2026 (CMS, https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/medicare-prescription-drug-affordability/overview/medicare-drug-price-negotiation-program/selected-drugs-negotiated-prices). On May 18, 2026 the Supreme Court declined to hear six drugmakers’ challenges to the negotiation program (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/supreme-court-rejects-appeals-from-drug-manufacturers-over-medicare-price-negotiations). On November 3, 2026 Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in midterms where health costs were a leading issue (OPB/NPR, https://www.opb.org/article/2026/11/04/2026-midterms-democrats-win-the-senate/), and at a Senate Judiciary hearing on November 17 senators of both parties said drug-price legislation would fall to the new Congress (Capital News Service, November 19, 2026, https://cnsmaryland.org/2026/11/19/lawmakers-urge-new-congress-to-take-action-against-rising-drug-prices).

The debate is live because prices are being pushed down from several directions at once, and because the effect on future medicines cannot be observed until years after the decisions are made. The Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index for prescription drugs was 4.5 percent lower in September 2026 than a year earlier, while drugmakers still raised list prices on hundreds of brand-name drugs in January 2026, and early studies disagree on whether the Inflation Reduction Act has already shifted investment away from small-molecule pills. The argument runs between patient and senior groups and most Democrats, who want governments to negotiate prices down and see industry warnings as overstated; drugmakers, biotech investors and free-market economists, who say price controls of any kind will cost future cures and that the real affordability problem lies with insurers and middlemen; the Trump administration and some conservative and trade-policy economists, who say Americans pay for the world’s drug research and other rich countries must pay more; health economists and assessment bodies such as ICER and the UK’s NICE, who want prices tied to measured health benefit; and access-to-medicines advocates who want research paid for publicly so that drugs can be sold near the cost of making them.

- Note: US manufacturer prices for brand-name drugs were 422 percent of prices in 33 other OECD countries in 2022, and 308 percent after an estimated allowance for US rebates.
- Context: RAND compared prices for the same drugs, weighted by the US mix of drugs, using 2022 IQVIA sales data. Those who defend US pricing point out that comparisons of this kind leave out how much faster and more widely new drugs are available in the United States (stat 9) and that generics are cheaper in the US.

### 4. Estimated cost to develop a new drug
- Label: Estimated research and development cost per new drug, including failures and cost of capital
- Value: 708
- Unit: millions of US dollars, median per approved drug (RAND 2025)
- Comparison rows (different studies, bases differ, each including the cost of failed candidates and the cost of capital): RAND, Mulcahy et al., 38 drugs approved in 2019, median $708 million, mean $1.31 billion (JAMA Network Open, 2025); Wouters, McKee and Luyten, 63 drugs approved 2009–2018 with public cost data, median $1,141.7 million, mean $1,559.1 million, in 2018 dollars (JAMA, 2020); DiMasi, Grabowski and Hansen (Tufts Center for the Study of Drug Development), confidential company data, mean $2,558 million before approval in 2013 dollars, or $2,870 million including post-approval research (Journal of Health Economics, 2016)
- Data year: drugs approved 2019 (RAND); 2009–2018 (Wouters); published 2016, 2013 dollars (DiMasi)
- Source label: Mulcahy et al., JAMA Network Open, January 2025 (RAND), via EurekAlert release; Wouters et al., JAMA, March 2020; DiMasi et al., Journal of Health Economics, 2016
- Source URL: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1069759 (Wouters: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2762311; DiMasi: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jhecon/v47y2016icp20-33.html)
- Note: Published estimates of what it costs to bring one new drug to market, counting failures and the cost of capital, range from a median of about $0.7 billion to an average of about $2.6 billion.
- Context: The estimates differ in method and sample, and each side of the debate tends to cite one end. The RAND study found direct spending per new drug, before counting failures and capital costs, at a median of $150 million and a mean of $369 million; a few very expensive drugs pull the mean up, and dropping two of the 38 cut the mean from $1.3 billion to $950 million. Lead author Andrew Mulcahy said, “Our work suggests that it may not be as costly to develop the typical new drug as has been previously estimated.” Wouters and colleagues relied on public filings, which over-represent smaller companies and some therapeutic areas; their median ranged from $765.9 million for nervous-system drugs to $2,771.6 million for cancer and immune drugs. The Tufts estimate, the one industry most often cites, rests on confidential company data that outside researchers cannot check, a point critics such as Public Citizen and Knowledge Ecology International have pressed for years. All three count the cost of capital, the return investors forgo while money is tied up for a decade or more, which is close to half of the Tufts figure.

- Source label: CMS, Negotiated Prices fact sheets for initial price applicability years 2026 and 2027

- Note: CMS estimates the second round of Medicare negotiated prices would have cut net spending on the 15 drugs by 44 percent, or about $12 billion, had they applied in 2024.
- Context: Negotiated prices are compared with spending net of the confidential rebates Medicare plans already received, so the cut from list price is larger: semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) went from a $959 monthly list price to $274, a 71 percent discount, and the cancer drug Xtandi from $13,480 to $7,004, 48 percent. The industry argues the program’s value is lost in reduced future research (stat 6); supporters point to these savings and to the program’s survival in court.

Neither side’s figure can yet be checked against outcomes, because the drugs in question would be launched in the 2030s and 2040s.

## Questions

### 1. How many valuable new medicines would lower prices actually cost?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: very few lost / many cures lost
- Why contested: Everyone agrees research follows expected revenue; the size of the response and which drugs would be lost cannot be observed until years after policies change, so forecasts rest on models whose assumptions each side disputes. The Congressional Budget Office projects about 13 fewer drugs over 30 years from Medicare negotiation, about 1 percent; University of Chicago economists project 135 fewer through 2039 from the same law, and 500 fewer approvals and new uses over a decade from most-favored-nation pricing applied to Medicare and Medicaid.
- Very few lost: Those who expect small losses argue that most drug revenue comes from the years before Medicare negotiation begins, that the industry’s margins leave room to absorb lower prices without cutting research, and that published estimates of the market-size response (Dubois and colleagues, 2015) are well below one, meaning a 10 percent cut in revenue would trim new drugs by a few percent at most. They add that the drugs at the margin, the ones that would no longer be worth developing, are disproportionately close copies of existing medicines, and that venture investment in biotech did not collapse after the Inflation Reduction Act passed, a point economist Rena Conti of Boston University has made.
- Many cures lost: Those who expect large losses argue that drug development is a long chain of risky bets financed by the expectation of a few large successes, so a cut in the payoff for success hits the earliest and riskiest research hardest. They point to early evidence after the Inflation Reduction Act: a difference-in-differences study in Health Affairs Scholar found industry-funded oncology trials after first approval fell 45.3 percent a month for small molecules against 32.5 percent for biologics; analyses by Vital Transformation found venture funding for small-molecule startups fell about 68 to 70 percent; and PhRMA cites the 70 percent figure. They add that losses are invisible: no one sees the cure that was never developed, so a policy that costs many future lives can look costless for a decade.

### 2. Do Americans pay for the world’s drug research, and should other rich countries pay more?
- Tag: value
- Poles: others must pay more / Americans overpay
- Why contested: The facts are fairly clear: the US supplies most of the industry’s revenue and profit (RAND, ASPE, Schaeffer Center). What they mean is not. One reading is that other rich countries free-ride on American patients; the other is that American patients are overcharged by an industry that charges what each market will bear.
- Others must pay more: Those who favor burden-sharing argue that drug research is a global public good whose results reach every country, so its cost should be shared roughly by ability to pay, and that other rich countries use their power as single buyers to hold prices below what would be needed to fund the research they benefit from. The 2025 executive order says that “the inflated prices in the United States fuel global innovation while foreign health systems get a free ride.” They point to the UK’s 2025 decision to raise its threshold under US pressure as proof that the burden can be shifted, and argue that if Americans simply paid European prices without Europe paying more, the world’s research budget would shrink.
- Americans overpay: Those who reject the free-riding frame argue that companies set prices market by market to maximize revenue, so a higher price abroad would not reduce US prices but simply add to profits. They note that other countries pay through their public research budgets too, that an NBER working paper (w31272) found the rest of the world’s contributions to drug-research returns are below America’s but far from zero, and that the United States has chosen not to bargain as a single buyer. The fix, on this view, is for the United States to use the same bargaining power other countries do, not to ask them to pay more.

### 3. Should a drug’s price track the health it delivers?
- Tag: value
- Poles: price by value / let patients decide
- Why contested: Most rich countries other than the United States use cost-effectiveness analysis, which compares a drug’s price with the years of healthy life it adds, to decide what to pay. The Inflation Reduction Act bars Medicare from using cost per quality-adjusted life year in ways that treat extending the lives of elderly, disabled or terminally ill people as of lower value, reflecting objections from disability advocates.
- Price by value: Those who favor value-based pricing argue that it is the only principled answer to the question of how much a drug is worth: a payer with a fixed budget that pays more for a drug than the health it delivers takes health away from other patients whose care is displaced. It rewards innovation in proportion to how much it helps, steering research toward real advances rather than marginal ones. ICER found that 23 of 26 drugs it had reviewed from 2022 to 2025 launched above the price that would match their health benefit. In ICER’s words, “Moving away from a system that allows prices to be out of line with value and access restrictions not based on objective notions of fair access requires urgent action.”
- Let patients decide: Those who oppose it argue that quality-adjusted life years put a lower value on extending the lives of people who are old, disabled or chronically ill, because their years count as lower quality; that assessments miss benefits that appear only over time, such as improvements on a first drug in a class; and that cost-effectiveness thresholds are, in practice, a tool for rationing and delaying access, as the lower availability of new drugs in Britain and elsewhere shows. On their view patients and doctors, not an agency with a formula, should decide what a treatment is worth.

### 4. Is the real affordability problem drug prices or how insurance passes them on?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: prices are the problem / middlemen are the problem
- Why contested: Patients feel drug costs through deductibles and coinsurance often calculated on list price, while manufacturers receive far less than list price after rebates; Drug Channels Institute put the gap at about $416 billion in 2025. Each side points at the other’s part of the chain.
- Prices are the problem: Those who see prices as the problem argue that rebates are a symptom of high launch prices, that even net US prices for brand-name drugs remain about three times those abroad (RAND), that launch prices are rising, with ICER putting the 2025 median net launch price at about $257,000 a year, and that fixing insurance design only shifts the same high cost onto premiums and taxpayers.
- Middlemen are the problem: Those who see the supply chain as the problem argue that net prices for brand-name drugs have been flat or falling (Drug Channels found net prices fell at seven of eight large manufacturers in 2025), that pharmacy benefit managers and insurers profit from high list prices and keep rebates rather than passing them to patients, and that capping patients’ out-of-pocket costs and requiring rebates to reach the pharmacy counter would solve most of what people experience as unaffordable drugs without touching the revenue that funds research. PhRMA urges policymakers to “Target true cost drivers: PBMs and insurers who drive up patient costs.”

### 5. Does the Inflation Reduction Act’s shorter clock for pills distort what gets developed?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: real distortion / industry talking point
- Why contested: The law makes small-molecule drugs (usually pills) eligible for negotiation 7 years after approval and biologics (usually injections) 11 years after, with prices effective two years later, so the industry calls it a “pill penalty.” Bills such as the EPIC Act would equalize the two at the biologic timeline; Axios reported in 2025 that a preliminary CBO score put the cost at about $10 billion over a decade.
- Real distortion: Those who see distortion argue that pills are cheaper to make, easier for patients to take and often the better medicine, so tilting the payoff toward biologics will cost patients over time. They cite the post-approval oncology trial study (larger drops for small molecules than for biologics), the fall in small-molecule venture funding reported by Vital Transformation, and a survey cited at the 2026 ISPOR conference in which 77 percent of investors said the shorter clock discourages small-molecule research.
- Industry talking point: Those who doubt it argue that the shift toward biologics began years before the law, that the longer clock for biologics reflects their longer route to effective competition from biosimilars, and that the early declines may reflect the broader biotech funding downturn after 2021 rather than the law. Public Citizen calls the pill penalty a pharmaceutical industry myth and argues that the fix would hand the industry billions by pulling high-spend drugs out of negotiation.

### 6. Should government set prices directly, or only bargain like a large buyer?
- Tag: value
- Poles: set prices / negotiate only
- Why contested: Medicare negotiation, most-favored-nation benchmarks, state upper payment limits and European reference pricing all involve government determining the price; supporters call this bargaining by the largest buyer, opponents call it price control. Where the line falls shapes legal challenges, such as PhRMA’s October 2026 suit arguing that GLOBE exceeds CMS’s authority, and the Supreme Court’s May 2026 refusal to hear challenges to Medicare negotiation.
- Set prices: Those who favor government price-setting argue that a patented drug is a government-granted monopoly, so there is no free market to protect; that every other rich country sets or negotiates prices with a single public payer; and that Medicare, paying for drugs with public money, has the same right as any large buyer to refuse to pay more than a fair price.
- Negotiate only: Those who reject price-setting argue that negotiation backed by heavy taxes on refusal and mandatory participation is not bargaining but a government-imposed price; that importing foreign price controls imports their delays and narrower access; and that competition among insurers and drugmakers, with the threat of generics at the end of exclusivity, is a better route to lower prices over time than a regulator’s formula. For this reason more than 50 conservative and free-market leaders signed a letter opposing making most-favored-nation pricing permanent in law.

### 7. Could public funding or prizes replace high prices as the reward for new drugs?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: yes, delink / no, prices work
- Why contested: Proposals to “delink” research rewards from prices, by funding trials publicly or paying prizes for new drugs that then go generic, have never been tried at the scale of the whole industry, so their costs and results can only be estimated from partial examples.
- Yes, delink: Those who favor delinkage argue that taxpayers already fund much of the science (NIH-funded research contributed to 354 of 356 drugs approved from 2010 to 2019) and that paying again through monopoly prices is wasteful, because the price a monopoly charges keeps some patients from using drugs that cost little to make. They point to nonprofit developers such as the Drugs for Neglected Diseases initiative, to publicly funded drugs of the past such as AZT and Taxol, and to prize models for antibiotics, and argue that paying for research directly would cost less than the monopoly profits now paid for it. Economist Dean Baker has argued that with publicly financed research and no patent monopolies, most drugs would sell for generic prices.
- No, prices work: Those who doubt it argue that governments are poor at picking which of thousands of projects to fund, that prizes require setting a value for drugs not yet invented, and that public budgets are at the mercy of politics, as the administration’s proposed cuts to NIH funding in 2025 showed. The private system, for all its costs, decentralizes the bets across many investors and has produced most of the medicines approved in recent decades; replacing it would risk a sharp fall in development before any public system could take its place.

### 8. Are high US prices the price of faster access to new drugs?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: yes, faster access / no, separable
- Why contested: New drugs reach American patients sooner and more widely than patients in most other rich countries: 74 percent of 287 new drugs launched in 2018 to 2022 were sold in the US by late 2022, against 52 percent in Germany and 43 percent in the UK (ASPE). Whether this follows from US prices or from other features of the US system is disputed.
- Yes, faster access: Those who say yes argue that companies launch first and most widely where they can earn the most and where regulators do not hold out for low prices, so tying US prices to foreign prices would give companies reason to delay foreign launches, or to delay US launches until foreign prices are set. They point to warnings from the Swiss drug industry in November 2026 that US most-favored-nation pricing would delay launches abroad.
- No, separable: Those who say no argue that most of the lag abroad comes from slower assessment and reimbursement systems in other countries, not from the price the United States pays, and that a large market like the United States would still be launched first under any plausible price. They note that some drugs, especially in Japan, are available abroad and not in the US, and that Switzerland’s federal price overseer disputed the industry’s warning about launch delays.

## Positions

### 1. Let government negotiate lower prices
- Adherents: Patients For Affordable Drugs (institution; national patient advocacy group), https://www.patientsforaffordabledrugs.org/strategy/inflation-reduction-act/ ; Senator Bernie Sanders (ranking member, Senate HELP Committee; sponsor of the Prescription Drug Price Relief Act), https://www.help.senate.gov/dem/newsroom/press/news-sanders-report-finds-nearly-700-prescription-drugs-have-increased-in-price-under-trump . Also AARP, Public Citizen and most congressional Democrats.
- Case in the camp’s voice: A patented drug is a monopoly the government grants, and a monopoly charges what the market will bear. Americans pay three to four times what people in other rich countries pay for the same brand-name medicines, millions skip doses or leave prescriptions unfilled, and Medicare, spending public money, was barred until 2022 from bargaining at all. Medicare negotiation is working: the second round cut net spending on 15 drugs by an estimated 44 percent, and the courts have upheld it. It should be expanded to more drugs, sooner after launch, and to people with private insurance, and US prices should be anchored to what other rich countries pay; Patients For Affordable Drugs holds that “a well-designed MFN plan could align U.S. prices with the lower prices abroad.” Lower prices do not have to mean fewer cures. The industry is among the most profitable in the economy, spends heavily on marketing and share buybacks, extends monopolies with thickets of secondary patents, and builds on research taxpayers already funded through the NIH. A modest reduction in revenue, CBO estimates, would cost about 1 percent of new drugs over 30 years, mostly drugs that add little over what exists.
- Variation inside the camp: instruments differ. Some put negotiation first (expanding the Inflation Reduction Act), some international reference pricing (Sanders’s Prescription Drug Price Relief Act would ensure Americans pay no more than people in other major countries; the bipartisan Hawley–Welch bill would bar charging more than the international average), some state prescription drug affordability boards with upper payment limits, and some patent reform to speed generic and biosimilar entry (a theme Patients For Affordable Drugs CEO Merith Basey stressed in Senate testimony in November 2026). Many in the camp welcomed the administration’s most-favored-nation push in principle while criticizing its confidential deals as too weak; Public Citizen published records in September 2026 that it says show the deals favor companies over patients.
- Best evidence: RAND’s finding that US brand-name prices are 422 percent of other OECD countries’ (308 percent after estimated rebates); CMS’s estimates of 22 and 44 percent savings from the first two negotiation rounds; CBO’s estimate of about 13 fewer drugs out of 1,300 over 30 years; the Ledley study of profit margins (13.8 percent against 7.7 percent for other large companies); NIH funding behind 354 of 356 new drugs; and KFF polling showing 72 percent of adults, including 68 percent of Republicans, want more regulation of prices.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that lower prices will cost future cures): The industry has predicted the end of innovation before every reform, and novel approvals have held near their ten-year average since the Inflation Reduction Act passed. Revenue matters, but the estimated response of research to revenue is modest, and the drugs lost at the margin are mostly ones with little added benefit. If society wants more research in particular areas, it can pay for that directly through the NIH or targeted incentives, at far lower cost than paying monopoly prices on every drug.

### 2. Keep prices market-set and cut what patients pay at the counter
- Adherents: PhRMA, the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (institution; trade group of research drug companies), https://phrma.org/policy-issues/government-price-setting ; Tomas Philipson (University of Chicago economist; former acting chair of the White House Council of Economic Advisers), https://ecchc.economics.uchicago.edu/2025/09/29/drugs-are-cheaper-here-than-abroad-and-other-countries-should-adopt-our-model . Also BIO, the Biotechnology Innovation Organization, and many free-market groups.
- Case in the camp’s voice: The United States is where most of the world’s new medicines are discovered, launched first and made widely available, and that is not an accident: it is the market where a successful drug can earn back the cost of the many that fail. Government price-setting, whether called negotiation or most-favored-nation pricing, cuts the payoff for success and so cuts the research that produces the next generation of cures; the cost is real but invisible, because no one sees the treatments that are never developed. Early evidence already shows the damage: PhRMA says the Inflation Reduction Act’s “pill penalty” “has contributed to a 70% drop in investment in small molecule drug development,” and that “MFN takes away billions of dollars that could otherwise be used to expand manufacturing in America.” Meanwhile what patients actually pay is driven by insurance design: deductibles and coinsurance tied to list prices, and pharmacy benefit managers that pocket rebates rather than passing them on. Fix that, cap patients’ out-of-pocket costs, fix the 340B discount program, and patients get relief without sacrificing future medicines. And the system already delivers low prices in the end: 90 percent of US prescriptions are filled with generics that are cheaper than abroad.
- Variation inside the camp: Some in the camp accept that patent thickets and delayed generic entry are real abuses and want them fixed to keep the bargain honest. Biotech investor Peter Kolchinsky (RA Capital), through his idea of a biotech social contract, argues that high prices during exclusivity are justified only if drugs reliably go generic afterward and that insurance should shield patients from list prices; others in the camp, including much of the industry, resist patent reforms. The camp also wants other rich countries to pay their “fair share,” in PhRMA’s words, through trade negotiations, a goal shared with the third camp, though it opposes tying US prices to foreign ones.
- Best evidence: US availability of new drugs (212 of 287 new drugs sold in the US by late 2022, against 149 in Germany and 122 in the UK); the Health Affairs Scholar study showing larger post-approval trial drops for small molecules after the law; the University of Chicago estimates of large losses in new drugs; Drug Channels’ finding that net prices fell at most large manufacturers in 2025 while list prices rose; and the generic share of prescriptions.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that Americans pay three to four times what others pay): Comparisons of list prices across countries overstate the gap, ignore that US generics are cheaper, and leave out what the US gets for its money: earlier access to more new drugs. Lower prices in Europe have come with delays and denials of access that most Americans would not accept. The answer to foreign underpayment is to press other countries to pay more, not to import their price controls.

### 3. Make other rich countries pay their share
- Adherents: The Trump White House (institution; Executive Order 14297, May 12, 2025), https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/05/delivering-most-favored-nation-prescription-drug-pricing-to-american-patients/ ; Mercatus Center at George Mason University (institution; Alden Abbott and Satya Marar, working paper, October 14, 2026), https://www.mercatus.org/research/working-papers/fostering-higher-foreign-contributions-pharma-rd-through-trade-deals . Also USC Schaeffer Center economists Dana Goldman and Darius Lakdawalla, https://schaeffer.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/01.2018_Global20Burden20of20Medical20Innovation.pdf .
- Case in the camp’s voice: The research that produces new medicines is a global good, but Americans pay for most of it. The United States supplies about 80 percent of OECD revenue from new innovative drugs while making up a fraction of the population, and other rich governments use their monopoly buying power to hold prices below what they would pay if they shared the cost fairly. As the executive order puts it, “The inflated prices in the United States fuel global innovation while foreign health systems get a free ride.” The answer is neither to keep overcharging Americans nor to cut the world’s research budget by forcing US prices down to foreign levels while leaving foreign prices where they are. It is to rebalance the bill: use trade negotiations, tariffs and the leverage of the American market to make other wealthy countries pay more, while bringing US prices down toward theirs. It has begun to work: in December 2025 the UK agreed to raise its cost-effectiveness threshold by 25 percent and spend more on new medicines in exchange for zero US tariffs.
- Variation inside the camp: The administration combines burden-sharing with direct limits on US prices: most-favored-nation agreements with 26 companies, the GLOBE model for Medicare Part B and the proposed GUARD model for Part D, and tariffs waived for companies that sign. Mercatus and other free-market economists in the camp reject tying US prices to foreign ones, arguing, “Importing foreign caps on patented drug prices will likely slow US generic entry,” and want the burden shifted through trade and regulatory deals alone; Mercatus writes, “The current per-capita contribution levels of non-US countries fall significantly below that of the US.” The drug industry endorses the foreign-pressure half of this agenda but not the price limits.
- Best evidence: ASPE’s 2026 estimate that the US supplies 79.87 percent of OECD revenue from innovative drugs launched 2020 to 2025 (71.20 percent after rebates); the Schaeffer Center’s estimate that the US accounts for 64 to 78 percent of worldwide pharmaceutical profits; RAND’s finding that the US makes up 62.4 percent of OECD sales but 23.8 percent of volume; and the UK’s threshold increase as evidence that other countries can be moved.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that companies will simply pocket higher foreign prices rather than lower US ones): That is why the US half of the policy matters: most-favored-nation terms require companies to offer Americans the lowest price paid in comparable countries, so higher foreign prices become the only way for companies to protect their revenue. Raising foreign prices without that link would indeed just add to profits; tying US prices to foreign prices without pressure abroad would cut the world’s research budget. The two together protect both American patients and future cures.

### 4. Pay for drugs by the health they deliver
- Adherents: Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, ICER (institution; independent drug-assessment nonprofit), https://icer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/ICER_LPAR_2026_One-Pager_ForPublication.pdf ; Karl Claxton (professor of economics, University of York, Centre for Health Economics), quoted at https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/nice-cost-effectiveness-thresholds-increased-as-uk-strikes-trade-deal-with-united-states . The approach is the basis of NICE in England and of similar assessment bodies in Canada, Australia and much of Europe.
- Case in the camp’s voice: The question is not whether drug prices are high or low but whether they are in line with what a drug does for patients. Every health system has a budget, so paying more for a drug than the health it delivers takes health away from other patients whose care is displaced. Pricing by value, measured as the years and quality of life a drug adds compared with existing care, rewards companies most for the biggest advances and least for marginal ones, which is the right signal for research. By that test the US system is badly off: ICER found 23 of 26 drugs it reviewed from 2022 to 2025 launched above the price that would match their benefit, and the median new drug now launches at about $257,000 a year net. In ICER’s words, “Moving away from a system that allows prices to be out of line with value and access restrictions not based on objective notions of fair access requires urgent action.”
- Variation inside the camp: Members disagree about the threshold. ICER’s benchmarks use $100,000 per quality-adjusted life year and $150,000 per equal value life year gained; Claxton and colleagues at York argue that the right threshold is the health a system actually produces with marginal spending, which they estimate is below NICE’s old range, so they opposed the 2025 increase and argued, in Claxton’s words, “We urgently need to see an impact assessment, which takes account of the full weight of robust research evidence.” Others accept higher thresholds for rare diseases or for severe conditions. ICER also measures benefit with an “equal value life year” to answer the objection that quality-adjusted life years undervalue people with disabilities.
- Best evidence: ICER’s launch-price findings; the experience of NICE and similar bodies, which have paid for most new medicines while declining those with poor value for money; the 2025 UK threshold change, which health economists warned would raise spending without commensurate health gains; and the logic of opportunity cost in any fixed budget.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that cost-effectiveness rationing undervalues the old, disabled and seriously ill, and delays access): Measures can and do adjust for severity and disability, as the equal value life year does, and the alternative is not no rationing but hidden rationing through deductibles, coverage denials and prices patients cannot pay. Delays in Europe reflect slow processes and low thresholds, which can be fixed, not the principle of paying for value.

### 5. Fund research publicly and sell drugs near cost
- Adherents: Knowledge Ecology International, KEI (institution; nonprofit on knowledge governance and access to medicines), https://www.keionline.org/book/support-for-the-delinkage-of-the-costs-of-r-d-and-the-price-of-medicines ; Dean Baker (economist, co-founder of the Center for Economic and Policy Research), https://cepr.net/publications/why-arent-democrats-talking-about-ending-patent-financed-drug-research/ . Also the Drugs for Neglected Diseases initiative as a working nonprofit model, and economist Mariana Mazzucato’s Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose at University College London.
- Case in the camp’s voice: The question as usually posed, how high prices must be to pay for innovation, assumes the only way to pay for research is through monopoly prices. That is a choice, and a costly one. Monopoly pricing means a drug that costs a few dollars to make is priced so that many patients cannot get it, and the whole bill for research is paid through the sickest people’s prescriptions. Taxpayers already fund much of the science: NIH-funded research contributed to 354 of the 356 drugs approved from 2010 to 2019, at a cost of $187 billion. Paying for the rest of the research directly, through publicly funded clinical trials, prizes for new drugs and open licensing, would let every new medicine be sold at roughly the cost of making it, as generics are now, and would likely cost the public less than the monopoly profits it pays today. The reward for innovation would be “delinked” from the price, defined by KEI as a model in which “the price of a medical technology is not linked to the cost of R&D.”
- Variation inside the camp: Some want full replacement of patents with public funding and prizes; others want a gradual transition, starting with publicly funded clinical trials for important drugs or with areas the market neglects, such as antibiotics and tropical diseases. Some focus on public manufacturing of off-patent drugs, such as California’s CalRx insulin program and the nonprofit generic maker Civica, which address prices after exclusivity rather than the research reward.
- Best evidence: The NIH contribution to new drugs; examples of publicly or philanthropically developed medicines delivered at low cost by nonprofits such as DNDi; the price gap between generic and brand-name versions of the same drug; and estimates (RAND 2025) that the typical drug costs less to develop than the industry’s figures suggest.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that governments cannot pick winners and public budgets are unreliable): Governments already pick and fund most of the basic science; private investors would still compete for prizes and contracts, so the bets would remain decentralized. The real risk is in the current system, which funds research only where monopoly profits are large and neglects diseases of the poor and antibiotics, where the private model has plainly failed.

## Crux

The question under the others: how much would cutting the revenue drugmakers expect from a successful medicine reduce the number of medicines that truly improve health, and how should a society weigh lower prices for patients now against medicines for patients later?

The part evidence can settle is the size and shape of the response. Economists agree that research follows expected revenue, but published estimates of how strongly differ several-fold (Dubois and colleagues 2015; Acemoglu and Linn 2004), and forecasts of the drugs lost to the same law range from about 13 over 30 years (CBO) to more than 100 over 15 to 20 years (University of Chicago). Over the next decade, studies of the Inflation Reduction Act’s effects on trials, investment and approvals, and of whether lost projects were important advances or close copies, can narrow that range. Related empirical questions feed into it: how much of the revenue gap between the United States and other rich countries would companies make up abroad if pressed, and how much of the faster US access to new drugs depends on US prices.

The part no data can close is the weighing. Even with a precise estimate, people disagree about how much present affordability to give up for future medicines; whether the people now paying high prices, many of them sick, older or uninsured for drugs, should carry the cost of research that benefits everyone; whether foreign governments, taxpayers or patients should pay; and whether it is fair to price a drug by the health it delivers when that judgment can disadvantage people whose lives are counted as lower in quality. A person who expects small losses might still oppose price limits if they weigh future patients heavily, and a person who expects large losses might still favor them if they weigh access for today’s patients more.
