# Universal Healthcare — research

## Overview

Every rich country has to decide how people pay for medical care and who is guaranteed it. At one end, a single public insurer pays for nearly everyone’s care out of taxes (Canada’s provincial plans, Taiwan) or the state runs the service itself (the UK’s NHS). In the middle, everyone is required or automatically enrolled to hold coverage from competing, tightly regulated insurers, public and private (Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland). At the other end, coverage comes mainly through employers and individual purchase, with public programs for particular groups and prices set largely by negotiation (the United States). This topic compares these approaches on cost, access and quality, and asks what each would mean for the United States. It is not about how drugs are priced and developed (see drug-pricing-vs-innovation), organ markets, or inequality in general, except where they bear directly on how health coverage is organized.

Why now: on 15 September 2026 the US Census Bureau reported that 26.7 million people, 7.9 percent of the population, had no health insurance at any point in 2025 (https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2026/demo/p60-291.html). [Correction 2026-10-05: the Census figure counts people uninsured for all of 2025, not at any point in the year: 7.9 percent of people in the United States were uninsured for the entire year (US Census Bureau, https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2026/demo/p60-291.html).] That was the last full year before two policy changes took effect. The enhanced ACA premium tax credits expired on 31 December 2025; the House passed a three-year extension 230–196 on 8 January 2026 (https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2026-01-09-house-passes-bill-extending-enhanced-premium-tax-credits), but the Senate did not follow. Medicaid work requirements under the July 2025 reconciliation law must be in place by 1 January 2027 (https://www.chcs.org/resource/a-summary-of-national-medicaid-work-requirements/). The Congressional Budget Office projects that the law will leave 10 million more people uninsured in 2034 (https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61570).

The debate is live because the United States spends far more per person than any other country without matching their results on life expectancy or avoidable deaths, and yet the fixes pull in opposite directions. Single-payer supporters, led by Senator Bernie Sanders and Physicians for a National Health Program, reintroduced the Medicare for All Act in April 2025. Public-option supporters such as Senators Michael Bennet and Tim Kaine want to add a government plan alongside private insurance, and the American Medical Association backs universal coverage through a mix of public and private plans. Market-oriented groups such as the Paragon Health Institute and the Cato Institute blame government subsidies and regulation for high costs. A smaller hybrid school proposes guaranteed catastrophic coverage for everyone, and a price-focused group led by Arnold Ventures argues that capping what hospitals charge matters more than who does the insuring. Abroad, the same argument runs the other way: in single-payer countries such as Canada and the UK, the debate is about long waits and whether to allow more private care.

- Comparison (deaths per 100,000): Switzerland 114; Japan 135; Australia 146; Netherlands 149; France 162; Canada 184; Germany 195; OECD average 222; United Kingdom 227; United States 312

- Value: 34.2
- Unit: percent of national health expenditure
- Comparison (percent of spending, 2017): United States 34.2; Canada 17.0. Per person: United States $2,497; Canada $551
- Data year: 2017
- Source label: Himmelstein, Campbell & Woolhandler, Annals of Internal Medicine, 2020
- Source URL: https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M19-2818
- Note: One study estimated US administrative costs at 34.2 percent of health spending in 2017, twice Canada’s 17.0 percent.

### 12. Estimated cost of a US single-payer system
- Value: from a $0.7 trillion decrease to a $0.3 trillion increase in national health spending; federal subsidies up $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion
- Unit: change in US dollars in 2030, compared with current law
- Comparison: see context for other estimates, which use different bases
- Data year: projection for 2030, published December 2020
- Source label: Congressional Budget Office, How CBO Analyzes the Costs of Proposals for Single-Payer Health Care Systems That Are Based on Medicare’s Fee-for-Service Program (Working Paper 2020-08)
- Source URL: https://www.cbo.gov/publication/56811
- Note: CBO estimated that single-payer designs would change total US health spending in 2030 by between minus $0.7 trillion and plus $0.3 trillion, while raising federal spending by $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion.
- Context: The split is the whole argument in miniature. Nearly every estimate agrees that single payer would move most health spending onto the federal budget, raising taxes and lowering premiums and out-of-pocket costs. They disagree over whether total spending, public and private together, would fall. CBO’s five designs vary by provider payment rates, cost sharing and whether long-term care is covered, and CBO projected near-universal coverage under all of them. On other bases: Charles Blahous of the Mercatus Center estimated in 2018 that Medicare for All would add about $32.6 trillion to federal spending over 2022–2031, and that total national spending would dip slightly (by roughly $2 trillion over the decade), but only if providers accepted payment cuts of roughly 40 percent, which he doubted (https://www.mercatus.org/economic-insights/expert-commentary/medicare-all-32-trillion-new-costs-or-2-trillion-savings). Galvani and colleagues, writing in The Lancet in 2020, estimated a 13 percent saving in national health spending, more than $450 billion a year in 2017 dollars, and more than 68,000 lives saved a year (abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32061298/). CBO’s estimate uses 2030 dollars in a single year; Mercatus uses a ten-year total; Galvani uses annual savings at 2017 levels.

### 20. People worldwide without full coverage of essential health services
- Value: 4.6 billion
- Unit: people not fully covered by essential health services
- Comparison: 2.1 billion people faced financial hardship from out-of-pocket health spending in 2022 (26 percent of the world’s population); the UHC service coverage index rose from 54 in 2000 to 71 in 2023 (out of 100)
- Data year: 2023 (coverage); 2022 (financial hardship)
- Source label: World Health Organization, Universal health coverage fact sheet (5 December 2025), drawing on the WHO–World Bank 2025 Global Monitoring Report
- Source URL: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/universal-health-coverage-(uhc)
- Note: About 4.6 billion people were not fully covered by essential health services in 2023.
- Context: Universal health coverage is UN Sustainable Development Goal target 3.8, due by 2030. WHO says progress has slowed sharply since 2015 and projects the service coverage index will reach only 74 by 2030. The WHO definition is about outcomes (everyone gets needed services without financial hardship) and does not require any particular financing model, so single-payer, multi-payer and mixed systems can all meet it. Of those facing financial hardship, WHO counts 1.6 billion living in poverty or pushed deeper into it by health costs.

## Questions

### 1. Would a single public payer lower total US health spending?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Total spending falls” / “Total spending rises”
- Why contested: Everyone agrees that single payer would move health spending onto the government budget; the dispute is whether the total bill, public and private together, would shrink. The answer depends on assumptions no one can test in advance: how far provider payment rates would fall, how much administrative cost would disappear, and how much more care people would use once it is free at the point of use.
- Case for “Total spending falls”: The United States pays higher prices than other countries for the same services (the OECD estimates about 52 percent above average), and private insurers pay hospitals more than twice what Medicare does. One public buyer with national bargaining power could set prices, as Canada’s provinces and Medicare already do, and a single set of rules would strip out much of the billing apparatus that multiple payers require. CBO’s own range includes a $0.7 trillion saving in 2030, and independent estimates such as Galvani and colleagues project double-digit percentage savings.
- Case for “Total spending rises”: Savings estimates assume providers accept rates 40 percent below what private insurers pay today, which would squeeze hospitals that already say Medicare underpays them, or else rates rise under political pressure and savings evaporate. Eliminating deductibles and copays would increase use of care, and CBO expects congestion as demand outruns supply. CBO’s range runs up to $0.3 trillion in extra spending, and every estimate requires $1.5 trillion or more a year in new federal revenue.

### 2. Is health care something government must guarantee to everyone, or a good best left mainly to individuals and markets with a safety net?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “Guaranteed to all” / “Individual choice, safety net”
- Why contested: This is a question about what people owe one another and what government is for. Gallup finds 64 percent of Americans say government should ensure everyone is covered, but only 46 percent want a government-run system, and the split falls sharply along party lines.
- Case for “Guaranteed to all”: Illness is largely unchosen and unpredictable, and without coverage people delay care, fall into medical debt or go without. Every other wealthy democracy treats access to care as a condition of equal citizenship, and markets cannot supply coverage to people whose expected costs exceed what they can pay. A guarantee also frees people to change jobs or start businesses without losing coverage.
- Case for “Individual choice, safety net”: People differ in what care they value and what trade-offs they would make between cost, access and risk, and a single guaranteed package imposes one answer on everyone. Government guarantees crowd out charity, family and private arrangements and make the guarantee hostage to budget politics. A targeted safety net for the poor and the sick protects the vulnerable without taking the whole sector under state control or taxing everyone to pay for it.

### 3. Does expanding insurance coverage substantially improve health and survival?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Coverage saves lives” / “Modest health gains”
- Why contested: Randomized evidence is scarce, and the best studies point in somewhat different directions. The answer decides whether universal coverage is mainly a health program or mainly a financial-protection program.
- Case for “Coverage saves lives”: Miller, Johnson and Wherry (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2021) found a 9.4 percent reduction in annual mortality among low-income adults aged 55 to 64 in states that expanded Medicaid under the ACA, implying about 15,600 excess deaths a year in states that did not expand. A randomized IRS outreach experiment also found coverage reduced mortality. Countries with universal coverage have lower treatable mortality, and the US avoidable mortality rate is well above the OECD average.
- Case for “Modest health gains”: The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment, the only lottery-based randomized study of Medicaid, found no significant effect on blood pressure, cholesterol or blood sugar control within two years, though it reduced depression and nearly eliminated catastrophic out-of-pocket spending (New England Journal of Medicine, 2013). Much of the US life expectancy gap comes from overdoses, obesity, violence and road deaths, which insurance does little to change. On this view, coverage’s main benefit is financial security, which cheaper designs could provide.

### 4. Are long waits an inherent cost of universal public systems, or a fixable result of underfunding?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Inherent to the model” / “Fixable with funding”
- Why contested: Canada’s median wait from referral to treatment was 28.6 weeks in 2025, and NHS England had 7.3 million pathways waiting in July 2026, but several universal systems (the Netherlands, Germany, Switzerland) report short waits. Whether waits follow from the financing model or from how much a country chooses to spend is the question.
- Case for “Inherent to the model”: When care is free at the point of use and the budget is fixed, demand exceeds supply and queues do the rationing that prices do elsewhere. Single-payer systems control costs precisely by limiting capacity, so waiting is the mechanism, not a flaw. Canada spends more of its GDP than the OECD average (11.3 against 9.3 percent) and still reports a median wait of 28.6 weeks, and NHS England starts treatment within 18 weeks for only 65.4 percent of waiting pathways, against a 92 percent standard.
- Case for “Fixable with funding”: Waits vary widely across universal systems and within them (19.2 weeks in Ontario against 60.9 in New Brunswick), which points to management, workforce and funding, not the financing model. The NHS list grew through years of tight budgets and the pandemic and has fallen from its peak of about 7.8 million pathways in September 2023 (https://www.channel4.com/news/factcheck/factcheck-nhs-elective-waiting-list-in-england-falls-to-7-3-million). Americans also wait, for appointments, prior authorizations and care they cannot afford; the United States ranked last on access in the Commonwealth Fund’s 2024 comparison.

### 5. Why does the United States spend so much: high prices, or more care and sicker patients?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Prices and paperwork” / “Volume and demand”
- Why contested: The diagnosis determines the cure. If prices and administration drive the gap, then who sets prices matters most. If Americans use more care, richer technology and face higher disease burdens, then cutting prices alone would ration care.
- Case for “Prices and paperwork”: Anderson and colleagues’ “It’s the prices, stupid” work and its updates show US spending is high mainly because each service costs more, not because Americans see doctors or stay in hospital more often; the United States has fewer physicians per 1,000 people (2.7) than the OECD average (3.9). Private insurers pay hospitals 254 percent of Medicare rates, and administrative costs are estimated at a quarter to a third of spending.
- Case for “Volume and demand”: The OECD estimates US volume of care per person at about 50 percent above average, alongside prices about 52 percent above, so both matter. Americans get more imaging, more specialty care, faster access to new drugs and more intensive end-of-life care, and US obesity and diabetes rates are among the highest in the OECD. Higher incomes also drive health spending everywhere, and the United States is richer than most comparison countries.

### 6. Should private insurance for core medical care be replaced, or kept alongside public coverage?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “Replace private insurance” / “Keep a choice of plans”
- Why contested: This is the dividing line between Medicare for All and every other route to universal coverage. About half of Americans have employer coverage, and many say they like it, while the Medicare for All Act would end duplicative private coverage.
- Case for “Replace private insurance”: A single payer gets its savings from being single: one set of rules, one price schedule, no networks, no marketing and no profit margin. Keeping private insurers alongside a public plan keeps most of the administrative overhead and lets insurers select healthier customers. Employer coverage ties people’s care to their jobs, and “choice” of insurer means little when networks and costs change every year.
- Case for “Keep a choice of plans”: Many high-performing systems (the Netherlands, Switzerland, Germany) achieve universal coverage with competing insurers. Forcing 150 million people out of plans they have chosen would be a huge disruption and a political dead end. Competition among plans rewards service and innovation, and a public plan can compete on price without a monopoly that leaves patients no exit if it underperforms.

### 7. Would universal public financing slow medical innovation?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: “Slows innovation” / “Little effect”
- Why contested: The United States accounts for a large share of global spending on new drugs and devices, and its high prices are widely thought to fund part of global research. What would happen if US prices fell to other countries’ levels cannot be observed directly.
- Case for “Slows innovation”: The United States pays more, and so it is where new treatments launch first and where investors expect returns. A single buyer setting prices at European levels would cut the expected revenue that funds high-risk research, and the rest of the world would lose the market that subsidizes it. US patients also get faster access to new cancer drugs and devices than patients in most single-payer systems.
- Case for “Little effect”: Much basic research is publicly funded, and a large share of US health spending goes to hospital and administrative prices that have no link to innovation. Payment systems can reward valuable new treatments deliberately rather than paying high prices across the board, and other rich countries with universal systems also produce major medical advances. The effect of lower drug prices on innovation is real but modest in most estimates (see drug-pricing-vs-innovation).

### 8. How much should patients pay at the point of care?
- Tag: value
- Poles: “Free at the point of use” / “Cost sharing for most”
- Why contested: Cost sharing both controls spending and deters care. Where to set it is a choice between financial protection, equal access and the discipline of prices, and it separates Medicare for All (no copays or deductibles) from almost every other design, including many universal systems in Europe.
- Case for “Free at the point of use”: Deductibles and copays deter needed care as much as unneeded care, especially for low-income and chronically ill patients, and they create medical debt (20 million US adults owe more than $250). Care free at the point of use is the most popular principle of the NHS (89 percent support). Costs are better controlled by setting prices and budgets than by charging the sick.
- Case for “Cost sharing for most”: When care is free at the point of use, people use more of it, including care of little value, and the system must ration by queue or budget instead. Income-related deductibles, as in universal catastrophic coverage proposals, protect the poor and the very sick fully while keeping everyone else price-conscious. Switzerland and the Netherlands combine universal coverage with substantial deductibles and rank well on outcomes.

## Positions

### 1. Cover everyone through a mix of public and private insurers
- Adherents: American Medical Association (institution), which states that it “has long advocated for health insurance coverage for all Americans, as well as pluralism, freedom of choice, freedom of practice and universal access for patients,” and opposes single payer — https://www.ama-assn.org/health-care-advocacy/access-care/ama-vision-health-care-reform ; Senators Michael Bennet and Tim Kaine, whose Medicare-X Choice Act (reintroduced 4 December 2025) would put a public plan in every county alongside private plans, with the stated aim to “achieve universal coverage” — https://www.bennet.senate.gov/2025/12/04/bennet-kaine-colleagues-reintroduce-medicare-x-choice-act-to-achieve-universal-health-care/ . Related: the Urban Institute’s Healthy America proposal (Blumberg, Holahan & Zuckerman, 2018) — https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/98432/2001826_2018.05.11_healthy_america_final_1.pdf ; Rep. Angie Craig’s House Medicare-X bill (July 2026).
- Variation inside the camp: some favor a public option (Medicare-X, a state public option); some favor auto-enrollment or an individual mandate with regulated private insurers on the Dutch or Swiss model; some focus on closing specific gaps (restoring enhanced tax credits, expanding Medicaid in the 10 states that have not). What unites them is universal coverage without abolishing private insurance.
- The case: The goal is that everyone is covered and no one is ruined by illness, and the fastest, surest route there builds on what already works. The ACA brought the uninsured rate to record lows. The gaps are specific and fixable: people who cannot afford marketplace premiums, adults in states that have not expanded Medicaid, workers whose employer offer is “affordable” on paper but not in practice. A public plan in every market adds a low-cost, stable choice and competitive pressure on private insurers without forcing anyone out of coverage they like. This is how Germany, the Netherlands and Switzerland reach universal coverage: competing insurers under strict rules, with subsidies for everyone who needs them. It is also what most Americans will accept: a majority want government to ensure coverage, but not a majority want to give up private plans.
- Best evidence: The Commonwealth Fund’s two top-ranked systems in 2024 include the Netherlands (competing regulated private insurers). Switzerland and Germany, both multi-payer, have among the lowest avoidable mortality rates in the OECD (114 and 195 per 100,000). The AMA’s 2024 plan to cover the uninsured records that nonelderly uninsured fell from 46.5 million when the ACA was enacted in 2010 to 25.6 million in 2022 (https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2024-ama-plan-to-cover-uninsured.pdf), and marketplace enrollment more than doubled while the enhanced subsidies were in place (10.4 million in February 2019 to 22.1 million in February 2025, effectuated).
- Answer to the strongest objection (from single-payer supporters: multiple payers keep the waste and the high prices): Administrative waste comes mostly from nonstandard billing rules and prior authorization, which can be standardized without a single payer; Cutler estimates about $50 billion a year in savings from coordination alone. Prices can be regulated across all payers (Maryland does this for hospitals, and a public option paying near-Medicare rates pulls prices down). Multi-payer systems abroad have costs well below US levels, which shows the problem is regulation, not the number of insurers.

### 2. Cover everyone through a single public insurer
- Adherents: Physicians for a National Health Program (institution), which defines single payer as “a single public or quasi-public agency” organizing financing while delivery remains largely private, and projects “over $500 billion in administrative savings” — https://pnhp.org/what-is-single-payer/ ; Senator Bernie Sanders and Representatives Pramila Jayapal and Debbie Dingell, who reintroduced the Medicare for All Act (S. 1506, H.R. 3069) on 29 April 2025 — https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-jayapal-dingell-hundreds-of-health-care-workers-introduce-medicare-for-all/ . Related: National Nurses United.
- Variation inside the camp: most US proposals follow the Canadian model (public insurance, private doctors and hospitals). A smaller group favors a national health service with publicly employed clinicians (the UK’s Beveridge model). Some would allow a supplementary private market; the Medicare for All Act prohibits private coverage that duplicates the public plan.
- The case: Health care is a human right, and only a system that covers everyone automatically, for everything medically necessary, with no premiums, deductibles or networks, actually delivers it. Every piecemeal fix leaves people falling through the gaps, as the lapse of the enhanced subsidies and the coming Medicaid work requirements show, because coverage that depends on income checks, employer offers and annual enrollment will always shed people. One public payer is also the only design that controls costs. It sets prices with national bargaining power, as Canada’s provinces and Medicare do, and removes the hundreds of billions spent on billing, marketing, claim denials and insurer profits. The savings pay for covering the uninsured and eliminating out-of-pocket costs.
- Best evidence: US administrative costs estimated at 34.2 percent of spending against 17.0 percent in Canada (2017). Private insurers paying hospitals 254 percent of Medicare rates. CBO’s range for single payer includes lower total national spending ($0.7 trillion lower in 2030) with near-universal coverage. Medical debt affects 20 million US adults; it barely exists in single-payer countries. Medicaid expansion reduced mortality by 9.4 percent among near-elderly low-income adults.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from market and multi-payer supporters: single payer means long waits and huge tax increases): The taxes replace premiums, deductibles and copays that families and employers already pay; CBO and even Mercatus found total spending about flat or lower, so most households would pay less overall. Canada’s waits reflect Canada’s spending choices (a system of about 11 percent of GDP); a US single payer would start from 17 to 18 percent, far above any other country. Americans already wait, and 26.7 million have no coverage to wait with.

### 3. Lower costs through competition and consumer choice
- Adherents: Paragon Health Institute (institution); its president Brian Blase testified in March 2023 that “counterproductive, ill-advised government policies have significantly contributed to high and rising health care prices, costs, and spending” and that “expanding inefficient health care subsidies makes health care less affordable” — https://paragoninstitute.org/public-health/why-healthcare-unaffordable/ ; Michael F. Cannon of the Cato Institute, whose Recovery: A Guide to Reforming the U.S. Health Sector (2023) argues that government already controls a larger share of US health spending than Canada’s or the UK’s and that making care “more universal” requires removing government barriers — https://www.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/ebookfiles/michael-f-cannon-recovery.pdf . Related: the Heritage Foundation; the Trump administration’s HHS, whose June 2026 ASPE brief attributes much of the ACA enrollment decline to removing improper enrollment.
- Variation inside the camp: some want to scale back federal subsidies and mandates entirely (Cato); others accept targeted subsidies but want them flowing to individuals through health savings accounts, individual coverage HRAs and cheaper, less regulated plans (Paragon). Nearly all favor price transparency, site-neutral payment and removing certificate-of-need and scope-of-practice limits that restrict supply.
- The case: US health care is expensive because it is not a market. Tax breaks for employer coverage and open-ended subsidies hide the price of care from patients, so neither they nor insurers bargain hard; mandates make every plan cover everything; and licensing, certificate-of-need laws and payment rules shield hospitals from competition and encourage consolidation. Government-financed demand without price discipline pushes prices up. Where patients do spend their own money (cosmetic surgery, LASIK, cash-price clinics), prices are posted and fall over time. Give people control of their health dollars through HSAs and portable coverage, let insurers offer plans people actually want, remove supply restrictions, and costs fall. Target public help to the poor and the sick, rather than putting everyone into a government plan that rations by queue.
- Best evidence: The Oregon experiment found no significant two-year effect of Medicaid on measured physical health. Canada’s median wait is 28.6 weeks and NHS England has 7.3 million pathways waiting. The United States ranks second on care process in the Commonwealth Fund comparison, and 75 percent of Americans are satisfied with the availability of quality care in their area, above the OECD average. ASPE estimates 2.9 million improper enrollments were removed from ACA marketplaces between 2025 and 2026, which Paragon and the administration cite as evidence that zero-premium subsidies invited fraud.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from universal-coverage supporters: markets leave the sick and poor without coverage and the United States already has the worst outcomes among rich countries): Poor outcomes reflect a system dominated by government payment rules and third-party payment, not a free market, and much of the life expectancy gap comes from causes outside medicine. The sick and poor are better served by direct, targeted help, such as funded high-risk pools and subsidies to individuals, than by putting everyone into government coverage. The US system already delivers fast access to advanced care for those in it.

### 4. Guarantee catastrophic protection for everyone and leave routine care to markets
- Adherents: Niskanen Center (institution), which in 2019 proposed Universal Catastrophic Coverage, “a universal, catastrophic tier of health insurance” with cost sharing tied to income, as a basis for bipartisan reform (Ed Dolan) — https://www.niskanencenter.org/universal-catastrophic-coverage/ ; economists Katherine Baicker, Amitabh Chandra and Mark Shepard, who argue for a publicly financed “basic bundle” for everyone with private coverage available on top (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2023) — https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.37.2.99 .
- Variation inside the camp: designs differ on how generous the floor is (catastrophic only versus a basic bundle including primary care), whether deductibles scale with income, and whether coverage is automatic. Singapore’s combination of mandatory savings accounts and public catastrophic insurance is often cited as a working example.
- The case: The left is right that everyone should be protected from financial ruin and automatically covered, and the right is right that prices, choice and personal responsibility keep costs in check. Universal catastrophic coverage does both. Everyone is automatically enrolled in a public floor that pays fully for the poor and the very sick, while people with means pay income-scaled deductibles for routine care and can buy more coverage if they want it. No one has to apply, re-enroll or prove eligibility, so no one falls through the gaps. The design targets public money where insurance matters most, at large and unpredictable costs, and leaves ordinary spending to consumer choice.
- Best evidence: The Oregon experiment found coverage’s clearest benefit was eliminating catastrophic out-of-pocket spending, which is exactly what a catastrophic floor provides. Switzerland and the Netherlands combine universal coverage with meaningful deductibles and perform well. Writing for Niskanen in June 2018, Ed Dolan reported a RAND analysis by Jodi Liu estimating that a basic universal catastrophic plan would reduce total national health spending by $211 billion, about 8.7 percent, compared with current law, and would save the federal budget a net $40 billion (https://www.niskanencenter.org/could-we-afford-universal-catastrophic-health-coverage/); the same summary says Sanders-style first-dollar coverage would raise federal health spending by 60 percent. Automatic enrollment addresses the churn seen when enhanced subsidies lapsed.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from single-payer supporters: high deductibles deter needed care; from market supporters: it is still a large new entitlement): Deductibles scale with income, so the poor pay nothing and no one faces costs that are large relative to what they earn. The floor replaces much of today’s tangle of Medicaid, ACA subsidies and the employer tax break rather than adding to it, so its net cost depends on design and could be close to what government already spends.

### 5. Cap health care prices across all payers, whoever does the insuring
- Adherents: Arnold Ventures (institution), which argues that “the U.S. pays top dollar for almost every service when compared with other countries,” blames provider consolidation, and backs site-neutral payment and limits on prices charged by providers with market power — https://www.arnoldventures.org/work/commercial-sector-prices ; Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (institution), which proposed capping commercial hospital prices at 200 percent of Medicare and estimated that this would cut national health spending by just over $1 trillion over 2021–2030 — https://www.crfb.org/papers/capping-hospital-prices . Related: Gerard Anderson of Johns Hopkins, co-author of “It’s the prices, stupid” (2003) and its 2019 update; the Center for American Progress’s April 2026 “Patients’ Bill of Rights,” which proposes capping hospital prices at 300 percent of Medicare in concentrated markets without a public option (https://www.americanprogress.org/article/a-patients-bill-of-rights-to-lower-health-care-costs/).
- Variation inside the camp: proposals range from caps on out-of-network or outpatient prices, through a cap on all commercial hospital prices (200 to 300 percent of Medicare), to full all-payer rate setting as in Maryland’s hospital system. Some pair caps with antitrust enforcement and bans on hospital ownership of physician practices.
- The case: The single-payer-versus-market debate asks the wrong question. The United States spends more than other countries mainly because each service costs more, and prices are high because consolidated hospital systems have market power over private insurers. Changing who insures people does not by itself change that, and neither does adding a public plan or handing patients health savings accounts. Capping what providers can charge, or setting prices across all payers as Maryland does for its hospitals, would lower premiums whatever the coverage model. It could be done now, without the upheaval of replacing employer coverage or the delay of building a new system. Once prices are under control, extending coverage becomes affordable under any model.
- Best evidence: Private insurers paid hospitals 254 percent of Medicare prices in 2022, with variation driven mostly by market power (RAND). The OECD finds US health prices about 52 percent above the OECD average. CRFB’s 200 percent cap would save just over $1 trillion over ten years, including $889 billion in lower premiums. Maryland has set hospital rates for all payers since the 1970s, and under its federal All-Payer Model, begun 1 January 2014, it moved hospitals to global budgets and agreed to hold all-payer per capita hospital spending growth to 3.58 percent a year (https://www.cms.gov/priorities/innovation/innovation-models/maryland-all-payer-model); since 2019 the Maryland Total Cost of Care Model has extended the arrangement.
- Answer to the strongest objection (from market supporters: price caps cause shortages and hospital closures; from universal-coverage supporters: price caps leave 26.7 million people uninsured): Caps set well above Medicare (200 to 300 percent) bind only on the highest-priced, usually most consolidated hospitals, not rural or safety-net ones, and Maryland’s hospitals have operated under rate setting for decades. On coverage, lower prices make every coverage expansion cheaper; the camp sees price control as the step that makes universal coverage affordable, not as a substitute for it.

## Crux

The question underneath the others: how much of the gap between the United States and other wealthy countries in cost, access and outcomes comes from the way coverage is organized, and would a different design close it, and at what cost to what else?

What evidence could settle: How far the US spending gap is driven by prices and administration rather than by volume of care, demographics and income, and how much of each a single payer, a regulated multi-payer system, price caps or more competition would actually remove in the US setting. Whether coverage expansions reduce deaths and illness or mainly protect finances, and by how much. Whether waiting times follow from the financing model or from funding and capacity choices. How a sudden cut in provider prices would affect hospital finances, supply and innovation. Studies, natural experiments (Medicaid expansion, Maryland’s rate setting, the lapse of the enhanced subsidies) and cross-country comparisons can narrow these, though not all the way.

What is a value choice: Even with the facts settled, people weigh the goods differently. How much should equal access for everyone count against individual choice of plan and provider? Is waiting in a queue a fairer way to ration care than rationing by price and ability to pay? Should the healthy and well-off pay, through taxes, for the care of the sick and poor, and how much disruption to people’s existing coverage is worth a more universal system? Is health care a right the state guarantees or a service people arrange for themselves, with help for those who cannot? No data can answer these.
