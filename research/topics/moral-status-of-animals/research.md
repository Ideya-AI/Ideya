# Moral Status of Animals — research

## Overview

The moral status of animals is the question of whether animals matter morally for their own sake, and if they do, how much and in what way. It covers three linked disputes: which animals have the kind of inner life that could ground moral standing at all (mammals and birds, fish, octopuses, crabs, insects); how much weight an animal’s interests carry compared with similar human interests; and what form that standing takes — protection from needless suffering while use for human purposes continues, equal consideration of interests, rights that rule out being owned and used, or duties that depend on the species and on the relationship. It takes in the philosophical positions, the science of animal sentience that feeds them, and the places where law has had to decide the status question directly: whether animals are things or property, whether any animal can be a legal person, and which animals a sentience statute protects. It deliberately excludes the policy fights that follow from the answer. Farming practices, diet, meat and climate belong to other topics, as does the suffering of wild animals and whether to intervene in nature, and whether artificial systems could be conscious. Farm, fish and laboratory numbers appear here only to show what rides on the status question, not to argue about how those industries should run.

Why now: on 17 July 2026 the Michigan Supreme Court declined to hear the Nonhuman Rights Project’s appeal seeking a writ of habeas corpus for seven chimpanzees kept at the DeYoung Family Zoo in Wallace, leaving in place an October 2025 Court of Appeals ruling that chimpanzees are not persons and cannot seek habeas relief (Michigan Supreme Court order, SC 169351, https://www.courts.michigan.gov/4a0b3f/siteassets/case-documents/uploads/sct/public/orders/169351_97_01.pdf). Justice Elizabeth Welch dissented, writing that the court should have granted leave “to explore whether the common law understanding of habeas relief should evolve in light of our more advanced understanding of the animal kingdom.” It was the latest in a run of top-court refusals in the United States — New York’s Court of Appeals on Happy the elephant, 5 to 2, in June 2022, and the Colorado Supreme Court on five elephants at the Cheyenne Mountain Zoo in January 2025 — set against the Constitutional Court of Ecuador’s 2022 holding, 7 to 2, that a wild woolly monkey named Estrellita was a subject of rights with intrinsic value. In the same year the science side institutionalized: the London School of Economics opened the Jeremy Coller Centre for Animal Sentience on 30 September 2025, led by Jonathan Birch, whose 2021 evidence review brought octopuses, crabs and lobsters inside the United Kingdom’s Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022.

The debate is live because the science has moved faster than the categories. The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, now signed by more than 600 researchers, holds that there is strong support for conscious experience in mammals and birds and at least a realistic possibility in all vertebrates and many invertebrates, including insects — which, if taken seriously, stretches moral standing to creatures numbered in the trillions. At the same time law in most countries still treats animals as property, most people eat them, and the mainstream view in public polling is that animals deserve protection but may be used for human benefit. The participants are moral philosophers, who have argued this since Peter Singer’s Animal Liberation in 1975 and Tom Regan’s The Case for Animal Rights in 1983; comparative neuroscientists and animal welfare scientists; veterinary and biomedical research bodies; religious traditions; animal rights and animal welfare organizations; legislators writing sentience into statute; and courts asked to decide whether any animal is a “person.”

Critics from one side say the proxy method overcounts simple animals; critics from the other say capacity for suffering need not track brain complexity. The estimates are used mainly by charities deciding where animal-welfare spending does most good, which is also why they are contested.

It is the clearest case of a sentience review directly changing which animals the law counts.

## Questions

Question: Is the capacity to feel enough to matter morally, or does moral status require more?
Kind: value
Poles: Feeling is enough / Reason or personhood needed
Why contested: Every theory of moral status has to say what property makes a being count, and the candidates sort animals very differently. If sentience is the ground, a pig and a toddler are in the same moral category and the question becomes one of degree. If what matters is rational agency, the ability to make and keep moral claims, or membership in a kind whose normal members have those abilities, humans stand apart and duties to animals take a different form. Nothing observable settles which property is the right one.
Case for feeling being enough: What makes it wrong to hurt someone is that it hurts them. Pain is bad for the one who feels it whether or not they can reason about it, and every attempt to name a higher threshold — language, rationality, moral agency, self-awareness — excludes some human beings, such as newborn infants and people with severe cognitive disabilities, whom nobody thinks it acceptable to use as we use animals. Jeremy Bentham put it in 1789: “the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?” Peter Singer’s equal consideration of interests, Tom Regan’s subject-of-a-life criterion and the sentience clauses written into EU and UK law all start here. Once the grounds are named, the argument from marginal cases makes any capacity-based line that keeps all humans in and all animals out look like species membership in disguise.
Case for reason or personhood being needed: Moral status is not only a matter of being affected; it is a matter of standing in a community of beings who can recognize claims, owe duties and be held responsible. Rights and obligations come as a package among agents who can reason about them, which is why animals are not held to account for killing one another. Kant grounded duties about animals in what cruelty does to human character, and contractualists such as Peter Carruthers argue that moral status belongs to those who can take part in the agreements that constitute morality. On the “kind” version defended by Carl Cohen and others, a human infant counts fully because it belongs to a rational kind, which answers the marginal-cases argument without denying that cruelty to animals is wrong. On this view animals deserve kindness, not the rights of persons.

Question: Which animals can actually feel pain or pleasure?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Mostly vertebrates / Many invertebrates too
Why contested: Consciousness cannot be observed directly in any creature other than oneself, so every attribution rests on inference from behavior, physiology and evolutionary relatedness. For mammals and birds the inferences are strong and widely accepted. For fish, and more so for crabs, octopuses and insects, the same behaviors can be read as signs of feeling or as unconscious reflexes and learning, and the answer turns partly on which theory of consciousness is true — itself unsettled.
Case for mostly vertebrates: Nociception — detecting and reacting to damage — is found almost everywhere in animal life and does not require any feeling at all; plants and single cells respond to harm. The human neuroscience of pain points to the cerebral cortex, which fish and invertebrates lack, and neuroscientists such as Brian Key argue that without comparable structures there is no basis for inferring felt pain. A 2023 review by Benjamin Diggles, Robert Arlinghaus, Howard Browman and colleagues in Reviews in Fisheries Science & Aquaculture, “Reasons to Be Skeptical about Sentience and Pain in Fishes and Aquatic Invertebrates,” argued that many fish and invertebrate pain studies are poorly replicated and interpret flexible behavior too generously. Behavioral tests that look like evidence of feeling — trading off shocks against shelter, rubbing an injured spot — can be produced by simple learning systems. Declaring whole phyla sentient on this evidence risks turning a scientific question into a policy slogan.
Case for many invertebrates too: The evidence has accumulated on exactly the behaviors that pain is supposed to explain. Octopuses tend and guard wounds and prefer places where they received painkillers; hermit crabs given small electric shocks are more likely to abandon their shells, and less likely to abandon a shell of a kind they prefer; rainbow trout injected with acid stop feeding and rub their lips, and morphine reduces it; bumblebees in a 2022 study rolled balls with no reward, which the authors read as play. The LSE review of more than 300 studies found very strong evidence of sentience in octopuses and strong evidence in true crabs, and the 2024 New York Declaration, now signed by more than 600 researchers, holds that there is at least a realistic possibility of consciousness in all vertebrates and in insects. Different brain structures can do the same job, as the bird brain shows: birds lack a layered cortex and are nonetheless accepted as conscious.

Question: Can being human by itself justify giving human interests more weight?
Kind: value
Poles: Species is irrelevant / Humans come first
Why contested: Almost everyone treats a human life as worth more than an animal life, and almost everyone rejects discrimination by race or sex. Whether preferring one’s own species is like those wrongs or like the permissible partiality of a parent for a child is a question about what counts as a morally relevant difference, and both sides think the other’s answer is obviously wrong.
Case for species being irrelevant: A difference in species is a biological fact, like a difference in skin color, and neither tells you anything about how much a being can suffer or how much its life matters to it. Singer coined “speciesism” for this reason: giving weight to an interest because of whose it is, rather than how strong it is, is the structure of racism and sexism. Where humans do deserve more weight, it is because of capacities many humans have — planning a future, deep relationships, richer experience — and the same principle then gives a chimpanzee more weight than a human with no such capacities. The fact that the preference is universal is not an argument for it; so, once, were many prejudices now rejected.
Case for humans coming first: Morality begins in the bonds of a shared life, and giving priority to members of one’s own community — family, then fellow citizens, then fellow humans — is not prejudice but the structure of every real ethics. Bernard Williams called the preference for humans “the human prejudice” and defended it: it is not a bias that corrupts our moral judgment but a condition of having one. Humans are also the only beings who can recognize and honor the claims of others, including animals, which gives the species a moral standing that its weakest members share because they belong to it. The conclusion that a healthy chimpanzee may matter more than a cognitively disabled child is, for this camp, a reductio of the premise that led there.

Question: Do animals feel as intensely as humans do?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Comparable intensity / Far dimmer
Why contested: Even those who agree that a chicken can suffer disagree about how much. The answer determines whether an animal’s pain counts almost as much as a human’s or a tiny fraction of it, and multiplied across tens of billions of animals the difference is decisive. There is no instrument that measures the intensity of anyone else’s experience, human or not.
Case for comparable intensity: Pain is an ancient warning system, and there is no evolutionary reason for it to hurt less in an animal that needs to avoid injury as much as we do. Some features may even make animal suffering worse: an animal in pain cannot understand that it will end, cannot distract itself with thoughts of recovery and has no concept of why it is happening. The brain systems for basic affect — fear, pain, distress — sit in structures humans share with other mammals and in many cases with birds and fish. On Rethink Priorities’ estimates, a pig’s capacity for welfare is about half a human’s and a chicken’s a third; the upper end of their ranges reaches or exceeds the human level for several species.
Case for far dimmer: Much of what makes human suffering so bad is cognitive — dread, memory, humiliation, the knowledge of what one is losing — and animals with simpler minds lack most of it. Neuron counts differ by orders of magnitude: a human has about 86 billion, a chicken about 220 million, a honey bee under a million. If the intensity or richness of experience scales at all with the machinery producing it, simple animals feel far less. The lower bounds of Rethink Priorities’ own estimates run close to zero for every species it assessed, and the authors call their numbers placeholders. Treating a shrimp’s pain as a meaningful fraction of a human’s on present evidence is a wager, not a finding.

Question: Does killing an animal painlessly wrong it?
Kind: value
Poles: Death harms them / Only suffering counts
Why contested: Most animal protection law and much public opinion is aimed at suffering, not death: humane slaughter is legal nearly everywhere, and a painless death is the standard for euthanizing pets. Whether death itself harms a being depends on whether it has a future that it has an interest in, and whether that requires being aware of oneself as having a future.
Case for death harming them: Death deprives an animal of all the good experiences it would otherwise have had, which is the same thing that makes death bad for a human, and this does not require the animal to know it has a future. Tom Regan’s rights view holds that any subject of a life — a being with beliefs, desires, perception, memory and an emotional life — has inherent value and a right not to be killed for others’ benefit, whether the killing hurts or not. Law and practice are inconsistent here: we would not accept that painlessly killing a dog for no reason is harmless. If animals matter only when they suffer, it becomes permissible to kill any number of them as long as it is done well, which most people on reflection reject.
Case for only suffering counting: A being that lives in the present, without plans, projects or a sense of itself as continuing through time, loses less in death than one that has all of these, and on some views loses nothing it could care about. Singer himself has long held that for animals that are not self-aware, painless killing followed by replacement with another animal with an equally good life may not be wrong, and much animal welfare science takes the quality of a life, not its length, as what matters. Veterinary ethics treats a painless death as a kindness in the right circumstances. Treating every animal death as a serious wrong would condemn not only slaughter but pest control, fishing and many conservation culls.

Question: Should the possibility that an animal feels be treated as if it does?
Kind: value
Poles: Act on precaution / Wait for evidence
Why contested: For many animals the honest answer to “can it suffer?” is “we do not know.” The moral question is what to do with that uncertainty. The stakes are asymmetric in both directions: wrongly ignoring sentience could mean vast unrecognized suffering, while wrongly granting it could impose large costs on food, research and livelihoods for creatures that feel nothing.
Case for precaution: When there is a realistic possibility that an animal is sentient, ignoring it is reckless in the same way ignoring a realistic risk to a human would be. Jonathan Birch’s framework, set out in The Edge of Sentience (2024), asks for proportionate responses — cheap, sensible protections where the evidence is suggestive, stronger ones as it firms up — rather than certainty that will never arrive. The UK applied this when it extended its sentience law to octopuses and crabs in 2022, and 91 percent of UK respondents in a 2021 survey agreed that if experts see some evidence an animal could feel pain, we should be careful about harming it. Waiting for proof means that, for the animals most people have never considered, the answer is always no.
Case for waiting for evidence: A precautionary principle with no clear threshold proves too much. If insects, whose numbers are commonly estimated in the quintillions [unverified], get standing on the basis of a realistic possibility, almost every human activity — farming, building, driving — becomes a moral catastrophe, and the concept stops guiding anything. Law and policy work best on established findings; the scientists skeptical of fish and invertebrate pain argue that the evidence base is thin, often unreplicated, and prone to being read generously by those who already favor protection. Treating a possibility as a fact also risks diverting attention and resources from animals whose suffering is not in doubt.

Question: Should any animal be a legal person?
Kind: value
Poles: Legal persons with rights / Protected property
Why contested: Legal personhood is a technical status — corporations, ships and rivers have held versions of it — but for animals it would mean the ability to have courts enforce rights on their behalf, most immediately the right not to be confined. The highest courts of New York, Colorado and Michigan have refused it, while Ecuador’s Constitutional Court and courts in Argentina, India and Pakistan have used the language of rights or personhood for particular animals.
Case for legal persons: So long as animals are property, their interests are weighed only against their owners’ and almost always lose, which is why cruelty statutes exempt the uses that cause the most suffering. Personhood is not a claim that an elephant is human; it is a recognition that some beings have interests the law must protect for their own sake, as it already does for corporations and, in some countries, for rivers. The Nonhuman Rights Project argues that cognitively complex, autonomous animals such as elephants, great apes and cetaceans have a common-law right to bodily liberty, and that habeas corpus has expanded before to include people once treated as property. Two New York judges, and one Michigan justice in 2026, agreed the question deserved a full answer.
Case for protected property: Personhood in the law is tied to the capacity to bear duties and responsibilities, and extending it to animals would either empty the concept or invite endless litigation over which animals qualify. Animal protection is better achieved through welfare legislation, which can be as strict as legislators choose, than through judges reinventing ancient writs. The New York, Colorado and Michigan courts all said that a change this large belongs to legislatures. Human exceptionalists add that a personhood claim for animals, however well meant, weakens the idea that human beings have a dignity and set of rights that do not depend on their capacities.

Question: Do our duties to an animal depend on our relationship to it?
Kind: value
Poles: Same duties to all / Relationships shape duties
Why contested: Most people treat dogs, pigs and wild deer very differently, and most theories of animal status say that is inconsistent, since the three may have similar capacities. Relational views say the difference is not a mistake: we bred domesticated animals into dependence, share cities with some wild species and have no relationship with most, and those facts generate different obligations.
Case for the same duties to all: If a being matters because of what it can experience, then a pig matters as much as a dog of similar capacities, and the difference in how they are treated reflects habit, not morality. Relational accounts risk building the arbitrariness of current practice into theory, and they can be used to justify neglecting animals who happen to have no human champion. The consistent view — the one most philosophers who work on animals hold — is that capacities set the baseline and relationships can add duties, but never subtract them.
Case for relationships shaping duties: Justice is always relational. Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka argue in Zoopolis (2011) that domesticated animals, whom we bred into dependence, are best thought of as members of our political community with claims to care and representation; wild animals as sovereign communities we should mostly leave alone; and urban wildlife as residents with a thinner set of claims. Clare Palmer argues that we owe assistance to animals whose vulnerability we caused and generally not to wild animals whose suffering we did not. Martha Nussbaum argues that what each animal is owed depends on the form of life characteristic of its species. Ecuador’s Constitutional Court read animal rights species by species for a similar reason. These views explain the pet-versus-pig intuition without endorsing everything done to pigs.

Question: Does raising the status of animals lower the status of humans?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Widens the circle / Erodes human dignity
Why contested: The history of moral progress is often told as a widening circle, from tribe to nation to all humans and then beyond. Critics fear that the arguments used to widen it to animals work by denying that humans have a special dignity, which could weaken protections for the humans least able to defend themselves. Whether expanding animal protection in fact goes with more or less respect for vulnerable humans can be studied, but the evidence is thin and mostly correlational.
Case for widening the circle: Concern for animals and concern for vulnerable humans tend to go together, not against each other; the same societies and individuals that expanded human rights have expanded animal protection, and research on the link between animal cruelty and violence against people is one reason cruelty is now a felony in every US state. Recognizing animal sentience in law, as the EU and UK have, has not been accompanied by any reduction in human rights. Most people who argue for animals’ moral status do not deny that humans have it; they deny only that being human is what gives it.
Case for eroding human dignity: The arguments most used for animal status rest on capacities, and if moral worth tracks capacities then human beings with fewer of them — infants, people with severe dementia or cognitive disability — are worth less. Wesley J. Smith and the Discovery Institute’s Center on Human Exceptionalism point to Singer’s own conclusions about infanticide and severely disabled infants as where that logic leads. Equating animal farming with human atrocities, as some campaigns have, trivializes the atrocities. On this view the belief that every human being has equal and inviolable worth is a hard-won achievement, and a theory that makes it depend on capacities puts it at risk.

## Positions

Camp: Keep human interests first while preventing needless animal suffering
Adherents: American Veterinary Medical Association, Animal Welfare Principles — https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/avma-policies/avma-animal-welfare-principles (loads in a browser; blocks automated fetching) ; Catholic Church, Catechism of the Catholic Church paragraphs 2415-2418 — https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8B.HTM ; Discovery Institute’s Center on Human Exceptionalism, chaired by Wesley J. Smith — https://humanexceptionalism.center/about/ ; also the World Organisation for Animal Health, whose Global Animal Welfare Strategy all its members adopted in 2017 (https://www.woah.org/en/what-we-do/animal-health-and-welfare/animal-welfare/), the Foundation for Biomedical Research, the philosopher Carl Cohen, and the late Roger Scruton. Variation inside the camp: welfare scientists and veterinary bodies hold that animal suffering matters for the animal’s own sake but weighs less than human interests; Kantians, contractualists such as Peter Carruthers, and human exceptionalists hold that only humans have moral rights and that duties about animals rest on human virtue, stewardship or dignity. [Correction 2026-10-05: Peter Carruthers is removed from this camp’s proponents on the page; his higher-order view denies that most animals have conscious pain and grants only indirect duties, so he does not fit a camp that opens “Animals feel pain and deserve protection.”] They agree on the practical line: humane use is legitimate, cruelty is not.
The case in the camp’s own voice: Animals are not things, and nobody serious says they are. They can feel pain and fear, and a decent person and a decent society protect them from suffering they need not endure — which is why cruelty is a felony in every American state and why veterinarians swear an oath to animal welfare. But a human being is not one more animal with a different set of capacities. Humans are the only creatures who can reason about right and wrong, make promises, hold themselves accountable and recognize the claims of others, including the claims of animals; that is what it means to be a moral being, and it is why the idea of human dignity has done so much good. It follows that human interests come first when they genuinely conflict with animal interests. Using animals for food, clothing, companionship, work and medical research is legitimate, as it has been in every human society, provided it is done humanely and without needless suffering. The catechism says it plainly: animals are owed kindness, and it is contrary to human dignity to make them suffer or die needlessly. What we reject is the step from “animals can suffer” to “animals are our moral equals.” That step, taken seriously, would forbid the research that produced vaccines and transplant surgery, would make the family dinner an atrocity, and — worse — would make human worth depend on capacities that infants and people with severe disabilities do not have.
Best evidence: This is the settled view of the public and of the law. In Gallup’s 2015 poll, 62 percent of Americans chose “animals deserve some protection but can still be used for the benefit of humans,” against 32 percent for equal rights and 3 percent for little protection. Every legal system that has written animal sentience into law — the EU treaty since 2009, the UK Act of 2022, the civil codes that stopped calling animals things — still permits their humane use for food and research, and the highest courts of New York, Colorado and Michigan have refused to treat any animal as a legal person. Among professional philosophers, 48 percent accepted or leaned toward eating meat being permissible, more than any alternative. The European research statistics record 8 million animals used in science in 2023 under a regime built on replacing, reducing and refining their use, not ending it — the model this camp stands for.
Answer to the strongest objection — that preferring humans is speciesism, like racism: Race and sex are morally irrelevant because they tell you nothing about a person’s nature; being human tells you a great deal. It means belonging to the only kind of being that lives by moral reasons, and a newborn or a person with severe dementia shares in that nature even when the capacities are undeveloped or lost — which is why we protect them fully rather than weighing them by their abilities. The marginal-cases argument proves the opposite of what it intends: if moral worth tracks capacities, the vulnerable humans it invokes are the ones who lose. Giving priority to our own kind, while treating other creatures humanely, is not a prejudice but the structure of every morality people have actually lived by.

Camp: Give animals’ interests equal consideration with like human interests
Adherents: Peter Singer, Animal Liberation (1975) and Animal Liberation Now (2023), as set out by utilitarianism.net — https://utilitarianism.net/peter-singer-animal-liberation/ ; Animal Ethics, on speciesism — https://www.animal-ethics.org/speciesism/ (loads in a browser; blocks automated fetching) ; Rethink Priorities, whose Moral Weight Project assumes that equal amounts of welfare count equally regardless of species — https://rethinkpriorities.org/research-area/an-introduction-to-the-moral-weight-project/ ; also Oscar Horta, Animal Charity Evaluators, and much of the effective altruism movement’s animal welfare wing. Variation: most adherents are utilitarians, but the principle is shared by sentientists who are not, and adherents differ on how much weight to give different species and on whether painless killing is wrong.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The principle is simple and most people already accept it for humans: a pain is as bad as it is, no matter whose it is. We do not discount someone’s suffering because of their race or sex, and there is no better reason to discount it because of their species. Equal consideration does not mean equal treatment. A pig has no interest in voting, and a human’s interest in continuing a life full of plans may well outweigh a mouse’s. It means that where an animal and a human have a comparable interest — above all the interest in not suffering — that interest gets the same weight. Apply that honestly and the conclusion is uncomfortable but clear: tens of billions of sentient animals are made to suffer every year for purposes that come nowhere near justifying it, and this is one of the largest moral problems in the world. We do not claim to know exactly how much a chicken or a fish can suffer; that is an empirical question, and we take the science seriously, including the work that tries to estimate it. But uncertainty about the size of the weight is not a reason to set it at zero, and at this scale even a small weight is enormous.
Best evidence: The case rests on the scientific consensus that mammals and birds are conscious and the strong evidence for fish and some invertebrates: 89 percent of philosophers accept or lean toward cats being conscious and 65 percent toward fish, the Cambridge (2012) and New York (2024) declarations endorse animal consciousness, and more than 600 researchers have signed the second. The best available estimates of how much animals can suffer, Rethink Priorities’ welfare ranges, put a pig at about half a human’s capacity and a chicken at a third, with wide uncertainty. Multiplied by about 88 billion land animals and more than a trillion fish killed each year, even the low ends of those ranges imply suffering on a scale that dwarfs most human-caused harms. The argument from marginal cases — that no capacity separates all humans from all animals — has never been answered without appeal to species membership itself.
Answer to the strongest objection — that this lowers the worth of vulnerable humans: The principle raises animals; it does not lower anyone. Every human being’s suffering counts fully under equal consideration, and nothing in it licenses harming a disabled person. What it denies is that membership in a species, rather than the capacity to suffer, is what makes suffering matter. The objection assumes that if some humans and some animals have similar capacities, the humans must lose. The conclusion we draw is the opposite: that the animals have been denied the protection we rightly extend to all humans.

Camp: Recognize animals as rights-holders who may not be used as property
Adherents: PETA — https://www.peta.org/about-peta/why-peta/why-animal-rights/ ; Gary Francione, The Abolitionist Approach to Animal Rights — https://www.abolitionistapproach.com/about/the-six-principles-of-the-abolitionist-approach-to-animal-rights/ ; Nonhuman Rights Project — https://www.nonhumanrights.org/who-we-are/ ; also the late Tom Regan, author of The Case for Animal Rights (1983), Christine Korsgaard, whose Fellow Creatures (2018) gives a Kantian case that animals are ends in themselves, and Steven Wise. Variation: abolitionists such as Francione hold that every sentient animal has a right not to be property and reject welfare reforms as entrenching use; the Nonhuman Rights Project pursues legal personhood case by case, starting with cognitively complex animals such as great apes and elephants; PETA combines the rights claim with campaigns for incremental reform.
The case in the camp’s own voice: Animals are not ours to experiment on, eat, wear or use for entertainment. The reason is not that using them produces more suffering than benefit — a calculation like that could justify anything, including using humans. It is that each animal is someone, a subject of a life that matters to them, with a value of their own that does not depend on their usefulness to anyone else. Beings like that have rights, and the most basic is the right not to be treated as property. We recognize this for every human being, including those who cannot reason or speak for themselves, and the only thing that separates them from a pig or a chimpanzee in the law’s eyes is species. Welfare laws cannot fix this, because as long as an animal is property its interests will be weighed against its owner’s and will almost always lose — which is why the same laws that make cruelty a felony exempt the practices that cause the most suffering. The Nonhuman Rights Project’s cases ask the courts to do what they have done before when a class of beings was treated as things: to recognize that the category was wrong. Justices and judges in New York and Michigan have already written that the question deserves an answer.
Best evidence: Legal status tracks exactly what this camp says: even where animals are no longer called things, they can still be owned, sold and killed, and the US Animal Welfare Act excludes the rats, mice and birds bred for research. Courts have begun to say what the camp says. Ecuador’s Constitutional Court held 7 to 2 in 2022 that animals are subjects of rights with intrinsic value; New York’s highest court split 5 to 2 on Happy the elephant, with Judge Jenny Rivera writing that “a gilded cage is still a cage”; and in July 2026 Justice Elizabeth Welch of the Michigan Supreme Court wrote that the evidence of chimpanzees’ self-awareness, planning and grief is “considerable and seemingly undisputed.” In Gallup’s polling, the share of Americans saying animals deserve the same rights as people rose from 25 to 32 percent between 2008 and 2015.
Answer to the strongest objection — that rights require the capacity to bear duties: Infants and people with severe cognitive disabilities bear no duties and have full rights, so rights cannot depend on that capacity. A right is a protection of an interest, not a reward for moral agency. The practical objection — that recognizing animal rights would upend food, clothing and research — is a statement of the cost of ending an injustice, not an argument that it is not one. Every earlier extension of rights was opposed on the ground that the economy depended on the practice it ended.

Camp: Protect every animal that may be sentient, in proportion to the evidence
Adherents: Jeremy Coller Centre for Animal Sentience at the London School of Economics, led by Jonathan Birch — https://www.lse.ac.uk/sentience ; the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness and its signatories, including the NYU Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy directed by Jeff Sebo — https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration ; also the UK government in the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022, which extended protection to cephalopods and decapods on the basis of the LSE review, and Birch’s book The Edge of Sentience (2024). Variation: adherents range widely on the weight question — some are welfarists who accept humane use, some favor equal consideration — and share the claim about how to act under uncertainty. This camp partly rejects the question as posed: it holds that for many animals “do they matter?” cannot yet be answered yes or no, and that the right question is what a realistic possibility of sentience requires of us now.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The fight between those who say animals matter as much as us and those who say they matter much less has missed where the real uncertainty lies. For dogs, pigs and chickens the science is settled: they are sentient. For fish it is very strong; for octopuses and crabs it is strong; for bees and flies it is suggestive and growing. No theory of consciousness is secure enough to rule any of these animals in or out with confidence, and it would be irresponsible to wait for certainty that may never come. The answer is proportionate precaution. Where there is a realistic possibility that an animal can feel, we take it into account in decisions that affect it; the cost of the precaution should scale with the strength of the evidence and the size of the risk; and the judgment about what is proportionate should be made openly, informed by experts and by the public, not left to industry or activists. That is what the UK did in 2022: an independent review weighed more than 300 studies against clear criteria, found strong evidence for octopuses and crabs, and the law was changed. It did not ban anything; it made sure those animals are considered.
Best evidence: The method has been tested in law. The LSE review scored cephalopods and decapods against eight criteria and found very strong evidence for octopods (seven criteria met with high confidence) and strong evidence for true crabs (five), and Parliament extended the Sentience Act accordingly in 2022. Researchers have converged on the core claim: 611 have signed the New York Declaration that a realistic possibility of consciousness exists in all vertebrates and many invertebrates and that it is irresponsible to ignore it. The public agrees with the principle: 91 percent of UK adults in a 2021 survey said that if experts see some evidence an animal could feel pain, we should be careful about harming it. Expert opinion is genuinely divided at the margin — 35 percent of philosophers accepted or leaned toward flies being conscious and 38 percent rejected it — which is exactly the situation precaution is designed for.
Answer to the strongest objection — that precaution without proof proves too much, reaching every insect: Proportionality is the answer. A realistic possibility is a real threshold, assessed against evidence, not a bare conceivability, and it leaves out organisms such as plants and, on current evidence, many simple invertebrates. What it requires scales with the evidence and the cost: for insects it may mean humane slaughter standards in insect farming or research into pesticide suffering, not a ban on driving. The alternative — treating an animal as unfeeling until proven otherwise — has repeatedly been wrong, as the history of assumptions about fish, newborn infants and animal pain in surgery shows.

Camp: Give animals rights fitted to their species and their relationship to us
Adherents: Martha Nussbaum, Justice for Animals (2023) — https://www.law.uchicago.edu/news/greater-good-magazine-interviews-martha-nussbaum-about-justice-animals ; Constitutional Court of Ecuador, Judgment No. 253-20-JH/22 (Estrellita), which held that animals are subjects of rights to be read through an “interspecies principle” grounded in each species’ characteristics — https://www.nonhumanrights.org/wp-content/uploads/Final-Judgment-Estrellita-w-Translation-Certification.pdf ; also Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka, Zoopolis (2011), and Clare Palmer, Animal Ethics in Context (2010). Variation: Nussbaum’s capabilities approach gives each sentient creature a claim to flourish in the form of life characteristic of its kind; Donaldson and Kymlicka give domesticated animals a kind of citizenship, wild animals sovereignty and urban wildlife residency; Palmer grounds special duties in the vulnerabilities humans have created. This is the hybrid camp, and it rejects the single-scale framing of the question: it holds that “how much do animals matter?” has no one answer, because what is owed differs by species and by relationship.
The case in the camp’s own voice: Both the welfare tradition and the utilitarian tradition ask the wrong question. They put every creature on one scale — how much can it suffer? — and then argue about the weights. But a whale, a dog, a chicken and a hummingbird are not smaller or larger versions of a human being with more or less pain. Each is a distinctive form of life, with its own ways of moving, playing, bonding and exploring, and justice for an animal means that it has the real opportunity to live that life. Suffering matters, but so does a caged elephant’s inability to roam, even if she is fed and pain-free. And what we owe depends on how we are related. We bred dogs, pigs and chickens into dependence on us, so we owe them care and a voice in the decisions that govern their lives; wild animals are owed, mostly, to be left alone and to have their habitats respected. This is why the Constitutional Court of Ecuador, recognizing that Estrellita had rights with intrinsic value, read those rights through the needs of her species — a condor’s right to food is not a dolphin’s — and did not declare that all human use of animals is forbidden. Justice for animals, like justice for humans, has to fit the beings it serves.
Best evidence: The approach explains judgments the other camps treat as inconsistencies: the near-universal sense that we owe more to a dog in our home than to a rat in a sewer, and that a zoo animal can be wronged by confinement even when it is not in pain. It has been adopted in constitutional law: the Ecuadorian court, deciding 7 to 2, held that animals are subjects of rights “as individuals with intrinsic value” while holding that hunting, fishing and domestication can be legitimate forms of interaction. The courts that denied habeas petitions for elephants and chimpanzees did not dispute the evidence that these animals are autonomous, social and suited to a life that a zoo cannot provide, which is the kind of harm the capabilities approach names.
Answer to the strongest objection — that relational duties rationalize current practice: Nothing in this view permits what is done to farmed animals now. Domestication creates stronger duties, not weaker ones, and the capabilities approach condemns any life that denies an animal its characteristic activities, which rules out most intensive confinement. The view does not replace sentience as the ground of moral standing; it says sentience opens the door and the animal’s form of life and our relationship to it decide what we owe once it is open.

## Crux

The question under the others is whether what makes a being matter morally is something animals can have in full — the capacity to experience its own life going well or badly — or something bound up with being human: reason, moral agency, or membership in a kind whose members have them. If feeling is the ground, the argument moves to how much each animal can feel, and species stops mattering in itself. If being human is the ground, animals can deserve real protection, even strong protection, without ever standing where humans stand. Almost every other disagreement on this page — speciesism, killing, legal personhood, what precaution requires — is downstream of which answer a person gives here, and whether they think one scale can capture it at all.

What evidence could settle: which animals are sentient, and how rich and intense their experiences are. Comparative neuroscience, behavioral tests of pain, trade-offs and learning, work on the neural basis of consciousness in humans, and better methods for estimating welfare ranges could all move this part. It matters a great deal: whether fish and insects feel changes the number of beings at stake by orders of magnitude, and whether a chicken’s suffering is a third of a human’s or a thousandth changes what equal consideration demands. It would not by itself settle the rest, because the same facts about an animal’s mind are read differently by those who think capacities ground status and those who do not.

What is a value choice: whether equal experiences deserve equal weight regardless of whose they are, or whether belonging to the human community — a kind of being that lives by moral reasons — gives a claim that no animal has; and whether moral standing is one quantity to be measured or a set of relationships and forms of life that call for different kinds of justice. No discovery about octopus brains or chicken pain will tell a reader whether the moral community is defined by who can suffer, by who can reason and answer for themselves, or by who we have taken into our care. Each camp here gives an answer to that choice, and each can accept all of the same evidence.
