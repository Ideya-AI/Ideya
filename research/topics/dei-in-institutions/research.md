# DEI in Institutions — research

## Overview

Diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) is the umbrella name for formal programs that employers, colleges and schools run to change who gets hired, admitted, promoted and included, and how people from different groups experience the institution. In practice the label covers a wide spread of things: diversity offices and chief diversity officers; diversity and implicit-bias training; recruiting aimed at underrepresented groups; demographic goals, targets and the tracking of workforce data by race and sex; diverse-slate rules such as the “Rooney Rule” for interviews and board searches; mentoring, fellowship and internship programs, some open to everyone and some limited by race or sex; employee resource groups; supplier-diversity programs; diversity statements in academic hiring; and, in admissions, the consideration of race. This topic covers whether such programs are needed, whether they work, whether some of them are themselves discriminatory or coercive, and who should decide: institutions themselves, courts, legislatures or the executive branch. It is centered on the United States, where the fight is most intense, with comparisons to Europe where they help. It deliberately leaves neighboring debates to their own pages: whether merit-based selection is itself fair (meritocracy and its discontents), the broader argument over organizing politics around group identity (identity politics versus universalism), compensation for historical wrongs (reparations), and bias in automated decision systems (algorithmic fairness). Race-conscious college admissions, which the Supreme Court ended in 2023, appears here only as one of the practices the DEI debate is about, not as a full topic.

Why now: on 25 August 2026 the US Justice Department announced that Deloitte would pay $21.5 million to settle allegations that it violated the False Claims Act by certifying compliance with federal anti-discrimination requirements while using race and sex in hiring, promotion and staffing to meet internal demographic goals, tying some senior partners’ pay to those goals, and limiting two development programs by race and sex; Deloitte denied the allegations and admitted no liability (US Department of Justice, https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/deloitte-agrees-pay-215m-resolve-alleged-employment-discrimination-violations). It was the second such settlement under the department’s Civil Rights Fraud Initiative, after IBM agreed to pay about $17 million in April 2026, and the whistleblower who brought the Deloitte case, Edward Blum’s American Alliance for Equal Rights, receives $4.3 million of the recovery. The settlement landed amid a cluster of federal moves that turn opposition to DEI into enforceable rules: a 26 March 2026 executive order requiring a new clause in every federal contract barring “racially discriminatory DEI activities”; a 23 July 2026 Equal Employment Opportunity Commission proposal to abolish the EEO-1 report, the annual race-and-sex workforce count employers have filed since the 1960s; and 3 September 2026 Treasury and IRS proposed regulations that would deny tax-exempt status to private schools and colleges that consider race in admissions, scholarships or programs (Gibson Dunn DEI Task Force update, https://www.gibsondunn.com/dei-task-force-update-september-11-2026/).

The debate is live because both sides now have institutional power behind them. The Trump administration, Republican-led states and conservative legal groups treat many DEI practices as unlawful discrimination and are using executive orders, contract terms, funding conditions, investigations and state laws to end them; on 6 February 2026 the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated an injunction and let the administration enforce key provisions of its 2025 anti-DEI orders while litigation continues, without deciding what counts as unlawful DEI (Littler, https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/fourth-circuit-allows-implementation-dei-executive-orders-proceed). Civil rights organizations, Democratic state attorneys general and many employers and investors argue that DEI remedies discrimination that research still finds in hiring, and that most of it is lawful; shareholders at large companies gave anti-DEI proposals about 1 percent average support in the 2026 proxy season, continuing 2025’s near-unanimous rejections (Fenwick & West, https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/06/05/how-dei-shareholder-proposals-are-faring-in-2026/). Between those poles sit researchers and HR professionals who say the most common DEI tools, especially mandatory training, were never shown to work and should be replaced with practices open to everyone; free-speech groups who oppose both compelled diversity statements and state bans on teaching about race; and a strand on the left and center that wants to shift help from race and sex to economic disadvantage. Corporate practice is moving fast but ambiguously: most large companies have stripped “DEI” from their filings and dropped explicit diversity rules, while the measured diversity of their boards has barely changed, which leaves open whether the retreat is substantive or a change of vocabulary.

Viz: bar

Source label: Quillian, Pager, Hexel & Midtbøen, “Meta-analysis of field experiments shows no change in racial discrimination in hiring over time,” PNAS (2017)
Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5642692/
Note: Across US hiring experiments since 1989, white applicants received on average 36 percent more callbacks than equally qualified Black applicants, with no decline over 25 years. [Correction 2026-10-05: the experiments ran from 1989 to 2015, not to the present; the page says “from 1989 to 2015” with “no decline over the period” (Quillian and colleagues, PNAS 2017, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5642692/).]
Context: Field experiments, also called audit or correspondence studies, send matched fictitious applications that differ only in a name or other signal of race, so a gap in callbacks measures discrimination directly rather than inferring it from outcomes. This meta-analysis pooled every available US study of hiring discrimination against African Americans or Latinos (28 in all); the 24 conducted since 1989 involved 54,318 applications for 25,517 positions. White applicants received 36 percent more callbacks than African Americans and 24 percent more than Latinos, and the authors found no change in discrimination against African Americans over the period and modest evidence of a decline for Latinos. It is the single most cited piece of evidence for the view that discrimination in hiring is ongoing rather than historical. The study measures callbacks at the first stage of hiring, not job offers or pay, and covers studies published up to 2015.

Viz: bar

Source label: Kline, Rose & Walters, “Systemic Discrimination Among Large U.S. Employers,” NBER Working Paper 29053 (published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2022)
Source URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w29053
Note: In more than 83,000 fictitious applications to 108 of the largest US employers, distinctively Black names lowered the chance of hearing back by 2.1 percentage points, and a fifth of the firms accounted for nearly half of the lost contacts.
Context: This is the largest recent hiring experiment in the United States, sent to entry-level openings at Fortune 500 companies. [Correction 2026-10-05: “largest recent” is unsupported; the page calls the 2019 to 2021 experiment “one of the largest US hiring experiments” (Kline, Rose and Walters, https://www.nber.org/papers/w29053).] Its finding cuts in two directions that both sides of the DEI debate use. The average gap is modest in absolute terms, but it is concentrated: firms in the most discriminatory fifth accounted for nearly half of all lost contacts to Black applicants, and the authors identified 23 individual companies that discriminated against Black applicants with high confidence. A follow-up “report card” from the same authors, summarized by the University of Chicago’s Becker Friedman Institute (2024, https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/A-Discrimination-Report-Card-1.pdf), found the worst-graded firms 24 percent less likely to contact a Black applicant than a white one and the best-graded 3 percent less likely. The original paper also reported that racial gaps were persistent within each company and smaller at firms with more centralized recruiting, at more profitable firms and at federal contractors, a finding cited by those who argue that structure and oversight, rather than training, reduce bias. Gender gaps averaged close to zero but varied widely by firm, with some favoring men and others women.

Data year: about three decades of workforce data from more than 800 US firms
Source label: Frank Dobbin & Alexandra Kalev, “Why Diversity Programs Fail,” Harvard Business Review (July–August 2016)
Source URL: https://hbr.org/2016/07/why-diversity-programs-fail
Note: In companies that made diversity training mandatory for managers, the share of Black women in management was on average 9 percent lower five years later, while mentoring programs and diversity task forces were followed by gains of roughly 9 to 30 percent.
Context: Dobbin, a Harvard sociologist, and Kalev, of Tel Aviv University, matched the timing of companies’ diversity programs to the companies’ own EEO-1 workforce reports over three decades, which makes their work the main body of evidence on which DEI tools are followed by more diverse management. These are relative changes in a group’s share of managers, not percentage points. Their headline finding is that the most common tools, mandatory training and rules that constrain managers’ discretion, were followed by no gains or by losses, which they attribute to managers resisting being told what to do, while programs that engage managers voluntarily (mentoring, task forces, targeted recruitment, self-managed teams) and that assign someone responsibility were followed by gains. They reported that nearly all of the Fortune 500 used mandatory training, while only about 10 percent of firms had mentoring programs. The design is observational, so it shows what followed each program rather than proving causation, and it predates the post-2020 expansion of DEI. Their later book, Getting to Diversity (2022), and a 2025 Harvard Business Review article argue for reaching the same goals through management practices open to all employees.

Context: Annual reports filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission are the most standardized record of what large companies say about themselves, so the collapse of DEI language is the clearest measure of the corporate retreat. It followed the January 2025 executive orders, the administration’s warnings that “illegal DEI” could expose federal contractors to False Claims Act liability, and earlier legal pressure after the 2023 Supreme Court admissions ruling. A Conference Board study of 2025 filings found the same direction one year in: use of the acronym “DEI” in S&P 500 filings fell 68 percent from 2024, “racial” 58 percent and “diversity” 33 percent (https://www.conference-board.org/press/corporate-diversity-disclosure-2025). What the numbers cannot show is how much of the change is vocabulary. Companies have renamed programs around “inclusion,” “belonging,” “opportunity” or “human capital,” and the Conference Board described the 2025 pattern as companies “selectively reframing commitments, reducing public exposure” rather than abandoning the work.

Note: The share of S&P 500 boards with a rule to consider diverse candidates for director seats fell from 58 percent to 12 percent in one year, while the share of directors who are women or from underrepresented minorities barely moved.
Context: The Rooney Rule, named for the National Football League’s 2003 requirement to interview minority candidates for head coaching jobs, commits a board to include women or minority candidates in every search without dictating who is chosen. Its near-disappearance is one of the sharpest single changes in corporate practice since 2024; a separate ESGAUGE analysis for Bloomberg News, reported by Fortune in August 2026, found that 61 of the S&P 100 companies had removed explicit diversity criteria for new directors since 2023. Yet Spencer Stuart, the executive search firm that publishes the annual Board Index, reports that overall board diversity fell by only 0.3 percentage points, from 49.6 to 49.3 percent. The pairing is central to the “retreat or rebrand” question: board composition changes slowly because directors serve for years, so the effect of dropped policies, if any, would show up first in new appointments.

Label: Effect of diversity training on participants, by outcome
Value: 0.38
Unit: overall effect size (Hedges g) of diversity training across 260 studies
Viz: bar
Data year: studies spanning more than 40 years, meta-analysis published 2016
Source label: Bezrukova, Spell, Perry & Jehn, “A meta-analytical integration of over 40 years of research on diversity training evaluation,” Psychological Bulletin (2016)
Source URL: https://ecommons.cornell.edu/handle/1813/71443
Note: Across 260 studies, diversity training had a moderate overall effect (0.38 standard deviations), largest for participants’ reactions and knowledge, smaller for behavior and attitudes, and effects on attitudes faded over time.
Context: This is the most comprehensive review of whether diversity training changes the people who take it. An effect size of 0.38 means the average trained group scored about four-tenths of a standard deviation better than an untrained one on the measured outcome, which is moderate by social-science conventions, but most of that comes from how participants rated the training and what they learned. Effects on attitudes decayed over time while knowledge held up, and training worked better when it was part of a broader diversity effort, built skills as well as awareness, and ran over a long period. Two later reviews are less encouraging about one popular variety, implicit-bias training. A 2019 meta-analysis of 492 studies with more than 87,000 participants by Patrick Forscher, Calvin Lai and colleagues in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology found that procedures could shift implicit-bias measures but that, in the 63 studies that tested it, those shifts did not carry through to behavior (https://source.washu.edu/2019/08/change-the-bias-change-the-behavior-maybe-not/). A 2021 Annual Review of Psychology survey of 418 prejudice-reduction experiments by Elizabeth Levy Paluck and colleagues found signs of publication bias and limited effects in landmark studies (https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-071620-030619).

## Questions

Question: Should institutions aim to treat every individual the same, or to make their membership reflect the groups they serve?
Tag: value
Poles: Equal treatment / Representative outcomes
Why contested: This is the moral fork under the whole debate. Both sides say they oppose discrimination, but they mean different things by it. One side holds that fairness is a property of each decision, so that any use of race or sex is wrong whatever the purpose; the other holds that fairness is also a property of results, so that persistent gaps between groups are a sign that decisions are not neutral and a reason to act on them. No study can settle which standard an institution should hold itself to.
Case for equal treatment: The promise of the Civil Rights Act was that people would be judged as individuals, not as members of a race or sex. A program that counts people by group, sets targets for each group and rewards managers for hitting them treats individuals as stand-ins for categories, which is the very wrong civil rights law set out to end. Groups differ in age, education, field of study, location and preferences, so equal treatment will not produce identical shares, and demanding identical shares pushes institutions toward preferences. Fair procedures, open competition and vigorous enforcement against actual discrimination are the remedy; the outcome is whatever fair procedures produce. The Supreme Court’s 2023 admissions ruling and the unanimous 2025 Ames decision, which held that majority-group plaintiffs face no extra burden under Title VII, both rest on this principle.
Case for representative outcomes: Decisions that look neutral one at a time can add up to exclusion, through referral networks, unexamined criteria, biased evaluations and the accumulated effects of past discrimination in schooling, housing and wealth. Hiring experiments still find white applicants called back far more often than identical Black applicants, so procedures that are formally equal are not equal in practice. Watching outcomes by group is how an institution finds out whether its procedures are actually fair, and acting when gaps persist is how it fixes them. An institution that serves a diverse public, teaches diverse students or relies on talent from every group has its own reasons to want its people to reflect them.

Question: How much of the gap in who gets hired, promoted and admitted is caused by discrimination today?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Mostly discrimination / Mostly other causes
Why contested: The case for DEI depends on discrimination being a present-day, institution-level cause of the disparities it targets, and the case against depends on it being small or already illegal and policed. The evidence is real on both sides: field experiments measure discrimination directly but only at the first step of hiring, while raw representation gaps shrink, sometimes sharply, once education, experience and occupation are taken into account.
Case for mostly discrimination: The most direct evidence available, randomized field experiments, finds substantial and unchanging discrimination. A meta-analysis of every US hiring experiment since 1989 found white applicants received 36 percent more callbacks than equally qualified Black applicants, with no decline in 25 years, and a 2019 to 2021 experiment at more than 100 of the largest US employers found significant gaps concentrated in identifiable firms. [Correction 2026-10-05: the experiments ran from 1989 to 2015, not to the present; the page says “from 1989 to 2015” with “no decline over the period” (Quillian and colleagues, PNAS 2017, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5642692/).] Discrimination at the callback stage compounds through every later stage, from interviews to promotions to pay, and earlier discrimination in schools and neighborhoods shapes the very credentials that “other causes” explanations treat as neutral. Gaps that persist across decades, industries and levels are what systemic barriers look like.
Case for mostly other causes: Callback gaps in résumé experiments are real but small in absolute terms (2.1 percentage points in the largest recent study) and concentrated in a minority of firms, which argues for targeted enforcement against those firms rather than programs that reshape every institution. [Correction 2026-10-05: “largest recent” is unsupported; the page calls the 2019 to 2021 experiment “one of the largest US hiring experiments” (Kline, Rose and Walters, https://www.nber.org/papers/w29053).] Most of the difference in representation in management, professions and selective colleges tracks differences in educational attainment, field of study, age and work patterns that arise long before anyone applies for a job. Treating every gap as evidence of discrimination ignores that some groups, such as Asian Americans, are overrepresented in many elite settings, and it leads institutions to address symptoms at the hiring stage instead of causes in early education and family circumstances.

Question: Do DEI programs, as actually practiced, reduce bias and widen opportunity?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Measurably work / Little or backfire
Why contested: DEI is not one intervention but dozens, and the evidence differs sharply among them. The best-studied tool, training, changes knowledge more than behavior, and longitudinal company data link mandatory training with no gains or losses, while mentoring, task forces and accountability structures are linked with gains. Most programs were never rigorously evaluated, and the strongest studies are observational.
Case for measurably work: The question is which DEI works, not whether it does. Three decades of data from more than 800 companies show that mentoring programs, diversity task forces, targeted recruiting and assigning responsibility to a manager were followed by 9 to 30 percent increases in the representation of underrepresented groups in management. Structured interviews, clear promotion criteria, pay audits and centralized recruiting reduce the room for bias, and the largest hiring experiment found smaller racial gaps at firms with centralized recruiting. [Correction 2026-10-05: “largest recent” is unsupported; the page calls the 2019 to 2021 experiment “one of the largest US hiring experiments” (Kline, Rose and Walters, https://www.nber.org/papers/w29053).] Diversity training has a moderate overall effect in a meta-analysis of 260 studies, and works best when it is part of a broader effort. Dropping the effective tools along with the weak ones would throw away real gains.
Case for little or backfire: The tools that made up most of the DEI boom, mandatory training, implicit-bias workshops and diversity statements, are the ones with the weakest evidence. Mandatory training was followed by fewer Black women in management, not more, and changing implicit bias does not change behavior. A field that grew into a large industry on the strength of correlational business-case studies, with little rigorous evaluation of its core products, has not earned the presumption that it works. Programs that sort people by identity and teach them to see colleagues as oppressors or oppressed can also breed resentment and division, and the rise in workers calling DEI a bad thing suggests that cost is real.

Question: Should government ban DEI programs in the institutions it funds, or leave the choice to the institutions?
Tag: value
Poles: Ban by law / Institutions decide
Why contested: Federal executive orders, contract clauses, funding conditions, False Claims Act settlements and laws in more than a dozen states now restrict DEI in public universities, schools, agencies and contractors. Those who back the restrictions see them as enforcing civil rights law against institutions that ignored it; opponents, including some who are critical of DEI, see government dictating the internal life and even the teaching of colleges, schools and private firms, with no clear definition of what is banned.
Case for ban by law: Public universities and agencies are arms of the state and spend taxpayers’ money, so the public has every right to decide what offices they fund and what principles they apply. Institutions had years to reform themselves and instead expanded DEI bureaucracies, mandatory training and diversity statements that function as political tests. Private contractors and grantees take federal money on the condition that they do not discriminate; enforcing that condition is ordinary law enforcement, not censorship. Without enforcement, institutions repackage the same practices under new names, so clear prohibitions and real penalties are the only reliable remedy.
Case for institutions decide: A federal court found that the Education Department’s 2025 anti-DEI directive gave no workable definition of “DEI” and no way to tell lawful from unlawful practice, and the same vagueness runs through many bans, so institutions cancel lawful mentoring, outreach and even courses out of fear. Employers, colleges and schools know their own workforces and students, and existing anti-discrimination law already forbids genuine preferences. Government bans on what may be taught or discussed about race and sex violate academic freedom and free speech, which is why free-speech groups that oppose compelled diversity statements also oppose the bans. Using funding threats and fraud liability to force uniform policy concentrates power in whoever holds the executive branch, a precedent both sides could come to regret.

Question: Is it fair to reserve some opportunities, such as fellowships, mentoring slots or supplier programs, for underrepresented groups?
Tag: value
Poles: Fair remedy / Unfair preference
Why contested: Group-restricted programs are the sharpest edge of DEI and the main target of lawsuits and federal settlements. Their defenders see a modest, targeted corrective for groups shut out of networks others inherit; their critics see the plain exclusion of individuals by race or sex. Courts have increasingly sided with the critics since 2023, but the moral question of whether such programs should exist is separate from whether they are currently lawful.
Case for fair remedy: Opportunity is not distributed by résumé alone. Internships, mentors, introductions and capital flow through networks that are themselves the product of past exclusion, so a program that brings people from underrepresented groups into those networks levels a field that is not level. These programs are small, voluntary and additive: they create opportunities rather than taking positions away from anyone. Many cover groups whose underrepresentation is extreme and well documented, and they were created in the open, often with government encouragement, for decades.
Case for unfair preference: An opportunity closed to an applicant because of race or sex is discrimination against that applicant, whatever its motive, and the individual turned away does not experience it as additive. Race and sex are crude proxies for disadvantage: they include the children of affluent professionals and exclude poor applicants from every group. The Civil Rights Act protects every individual, as the Supreme Court unanimously confirmed in 2025, and programs that would be plainly illegal if they favored white men cannot be legal because they favor someone else. Opening the same programs to anyone who faces disadvantage serves the goal without the exclusion.

Question: Should help be targeted by race and sex, or by economic disadvantage?
Tag: value
Poles: Race and sex / Economic background
Why contested: After the Supreme Court ended race-conscious admissions, and as federal pressure narrows group-based programs, many institutions are turning to income, first-generation status, neighborhood and adversity. Advocates of the shift on the left and the right say it is fairer and more popular; many civil rights advocates say class proxies miss the specific barriers of racial and sex discrimination and do not reproduce racial diversity.
Case for race and sex: Racial discrimination is not reducible to income. Black families at every income level have far less wealth than white families with the same income, live in more segregated neighborhoods and face discrimination in hiring that experiments show is independent of qualifications. Women face barriers in promotion and pay that have little to do with their parents’ class. Class-based programs help, but evidence from selective colleges shows they recover only part of the racial diversity that race-conscious policies achieved, and the steep fall in Black enrollment at some selective colleges after 2023 shows what is lost.
Case for economic background: Disadvantage in America runs most deeply along lines of class, and race-based programs disproportionately benefit the most advantaged members of minority groups while ignoring poor white, Asian and rural applicants. Economic criteria are lawful, broadly popular and direct help to the people with the fewest advantages of any race, and because poverty is concentrated among Black and Hispanic Americans they also produce substantial racial diversity. Richard Kahlenberg, who testified as an expert witness for the challengers in the Harvard case, argues that a politics of class unites working people that race-based preferences divide, and Walter Benn Michaels argues that a focus on diversity let elite institutions celebrate representation while ignoring inequality.

Question: Does diversity in leadership make organizations perform better?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Performance gain / No proven link
Why contested: The “business case” for diversity is the argument companies relied on most, because it frames DEI as profit-seeking rather than politics, and much of it rests on consultancy studies whose data are not public. Academic studies find mixed results that depend on the setting, the kind of diversity and the measure of performance.
Case for performance gain: McKinsey’s four studies across a decade and more than 1,200 companies find that firms in the top quarter for executive-team gender or ethnic diversity are 39 percent more likely to outperform the bottom quarter. Diverse teams bring a wider range of knowledge and perspectives, guard against groupthink, and help companies understand diverse customers and recruit from the whole talent pool. A firm that hires from a narrow slice of the population is leaving talent unused, and majorities of Americans agree that diverse workforces make companies more innovative and more profitable.
Case for no proven link: An independent attempt to reproduce McKinsey’s result on S&P 500 companies found no significant relationship and argued that McKinsey’s timing could not show diversity causing performance. Academic research on team diversity finds effects that are small, inconsistent and sometimes negative depending on the task and how teams are managed. Profitable firms may simply have more room to invest in diversity, which would produce the correlation in reverse. And if the case for DEI rests on profits, it fails the moment the numbers do; the honest case is one of fairness, which should be argued as such.

Question: Is the corporate retreat from DEI a real change in practice or mostly a change of vocabulary?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real retreat / Mostly rebranding
Why contested: The visible evidence of retreat is overwhelming: “DEI” has nearly vanished from annual reports, board diversity rules have collapsed, and pay incentives tied to diversity have halved. But the measurable composition of boards has barely moved, board oversight disclosures have risen, and many companies say they have renamed rather than ended programs. Which reading is right determines whether the policy fight is over substance or symbols, and both camps make claims about it.
Case for real retreat: Companies have ended the practices with teeth: representation goals, diverse-slate rules, pay incentives, supplier targets and programs limited to particular groups, and many have cut DEI staff. Disclosure of workforce data, the main way outsiders could hold firms accountable, is disappearing, and the federal government proposes to stop collecting it at all. Policy changes take years to show up in the composition of boards and management, so the absence of change in 2026 says little about 2030. Those who back the rollback count it as a success; those who regret it count it as a loss, but both see substance.
Case for mostly rebranding: Many firms moved programs under new names, such as inclusion, belonging, opportunity or talent, opened group-restricted programs to all employees, and moved oversight to the board, which is where legal defensibility lies. Board diversity is essentially unchanged and record numbers of women lead Fortune 500 companies. Shareholders reject anti-DEI proposals by overwhelming margins, which suggests investors expect the underlying commitments to continue. The administration’s critics of DEI themselves complain that institutions are disguising rather than ending it, which is why enforcement has shifted to investigations and fraud liability.
## Positions

Order note: the first two camps are the main poles and are held most widely: the first by civil rights organizations, Democratic officials and many employers and investors, the second by the current US administration, Republican-led states and conservative legal and policy groups. The third is the hybrid: it accepts the goals of the first camp and many of the criticisms of the second, and it is where much of corporate and HR practice has moved since 2024. The fourth rejects the question as posed for the parts of the debate that concern speech, teaching and academic hiring, holding that the issue is coercion from either direction rather than DEI itself. The fifth rejects the question’s axis, arguing that help should be targeted by class rather than by race or sex. A narrower legal strand, that DEI is lawful when it is open to all and unlawful when it allocates opportunities by race or sex, is shared by the first and third camps and is noted inside them rather than treated as a camp.

Camp: Keep DEI programs to counter discrimination
Adherents: NAACP Legal Defense Fund, https://www.naacpldf.org/press-release/ldf-condemns-trump-administration-executive-order-mandating-the-elimination-of-deia-in-federal-contracts/ (27 March 2026, condemning the executive order on federal contractors; its director Demetria McCain said diversity, equity, inclusion and accessibility programs “advance merit by removing barriers to employment for talent” and that the administration “should focus on expanding these opportunities, not stripping them away”); Illinois Attorney General Kwame Raoul, with Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Campbell and 14 other state attorneys general, https://www.illinoisattorneygeneral.gov/news/story/attorney-general-raoul-issues-multistate-guidance-for-businesses-on-diversity-equity-inclusion-and-accessibility-employment-initiatives-in-the-workplace (13 February 2025 multistate guidance stating that DEIA policies “do not amount to impermissible discrimination,” are not illegal, and help prevent unlawful discrimination). Also: the National Urban League and its president Marc Morial, plaintiffs in National Urban League v. Trump; Kimberlé Crenshaw and the African American Policy Forum; the American Federation of Teachers, which won the vacatur of the Education Department’s 2025 anti-DEI directive; and the shareholders who have rejected anti-DEI proposals at large companies.
Case: Discrimination in American workplaces and schools is not a memory. Matched-résumé experiments show employers calling back white applicants far more often than identical Black applicants, with no improvement in a generation, and women and people of color remain sharply underrepresented as managers, executives and professionals. DEI is how institutions find and fix that: by recruiting from the whole talent pool, using structured hiring and promotion criteria, auditing pay, tracking outcomes so problems can be seen, mentoring people who lack inherited networks, and building workplaces and campuses where everyone can do their best work. Almost all of it is lawful and has been for decades; preferences and quotas were already illegal, and the campaign against “DEI” deliberately blurs lawful inclusion with unlawful preference to scare institutions out of both. Merit is not served when talent is filtered out by bias, and diverse institutions serve a diverse country better. Abandoning this work under political pressure will not make institutions neutral; it will let old patterns return quietly.
Best evidence: A meta-analysis of every US hiring experiment since 1989 found white applicants received 36 percent more callbacks than equally qualified African Americans, with no change over 25 years (Quillian and colleagues, PNAS 2017). [Correction 2026-10-05: the experiments ran from 1989 to 2015, not to the present; the page says “from 1989 to 2015” with “no decline over the period” (Quillian and colleagues, PNAS 2017, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5642692/).] Black workers are 12.7 percent of the employed but 8.8 percent of managers and 5.6 percent of chief executives (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025). Mentoring programs, diversity task forces and assigned accountability were followed by 9 to 30 percent gains in management diversity across more than 800 firms (Dobbin and Kalev). After race-conscious admissions ended, the Black share of MIT’s entering class fell from about 13 percent to 5 percent. And 69 percent of Americans say it is important for businesses to promote DEI (Gallup, 2025).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that DEI itself discriminates, by sorting people by race and sex and handing out opportunities accordingly. The camp’s answer is that this describes a small fraction of what DEI is, and that those practices, where they exist, were already unlawful and can be fixed by opening programs to all; the remedy for a flawed program is reform, not abolition of the whole effort. Measuring outcomes by group is not discrimination but the only way to detect it, which is why the government required it of large employers for six decades. The camp also notes that the enforcement campaign has produced settlements without findings of liability, and a federal court found that the government could not define what “illegal DEI” is.
Variation inside the camp: Civil rights litigators focus on legality and enforcement, defending DEI as lawful under existing civil rights law and suing to block executive orders and funding conditions. Corporate defenders emphasize the business case of talent, innovation and markets. A more ambitious wing, associated with “equity” and antiracist frameworks, holds that institutions should actively work to close outcome gaps and treats race-conscious measures as necessary; others in the camp accept that programs should be open to all and move toward the third camp. In education, the camp includes defenders of race-conscious admissions, now unlawful, and of teaching about systemic racism in schools.

Camp: End DEI programs and treat everyone without regard to race or sex
Adherents: The White House, Executive Order 14173, “Ending Illegal Discrimination and Restoring Merit-Based Opportunity” (21 January 2025), https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-14173-ending-illegal-discrimination-and-restoring-merit-based-opportunity (describing DEI as “dangerous, demeaning, and immoral race- and sex-based preferences,” revoking the 1965 affirmative action order for federal contractors, and requiring contractors to certify they run no unlawful DEI programs); Christopher Rufo and Ilya Shapiro of the Manhattan Institute, with Matt Beienburg of the Goldwater Institute, “Abolish DEI Bureaucracies and Restore Colorblind Equality in Public Universities” (January 2023), https://media4.manhattan-institute.org/sites/default/files/model_dei_legislation013023.pdf (model state legislation to abolish DEI offices, end mandatory diversity training, curtail “political coercion” and end identity-based preferences). Also: Edward Blum’s American Alliance for Equal Rights, which brought the Deloitte case and has sued law firms, scholarship programs and venture funds over race-restricted programs; Equal Employment Opportunity Commission Chair Andrea Lucas; the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Fraud Initiative; and the legislatures of the states that have banned DEI offices at public colleges, including Texas, Florida, Utah, Iowa and Ohio.
Case: The civil rights revolution established a simple principle: people must be treated as individuals, not sorted by race or sex. DEI abandoned that principle. Behind a vocabulary of inclusion, it built bureaucracies that classify every employee and student by identity, set demographic targets, tie executives’ pay to hitting them, reserve fellowships and programs for some races and exclude others, require faculty to profess an ideology in diversity statements, and train workers to see one another as oppressors and oppressed. That is discrimination, and it is illegal under the Civil Rights Act no matter which group it favors, as the Supreme Court confirmed in 2023 and again unanimously in 2025. It also failed on its own terms: the core tools did not work, the business case did not replicate, and the effort bred division and resentment. The remedy is to restore merit and equal treatment: abolish the offices, end preferences and compelled speech, enforce anti-discrimination law equally for everyone, and stop the government itself from demanding racial bookkeeping.
Best evidence: Under Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023) and Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services (2025), the Supreme Court held that civil rights law protects every individual equally, without extra hurdles for majority-group plaintiffs. Mandatory diversity training was followed by fewer Black women in management, not more (Dobbin and Kalev), and changing implicit bias does not change behavior (Forscher and colleagues, 2019). McKinsey’s business case did not replicate on S&P 500 firms (Green and Hand, 2024). Half of Americans disapproved of colleges considering race in admissions, against a third who approved (Pew, 2023). The Justice Department alleged that Deloitte tracked units’ demographic progress with color-coded reports and tied senior partners’ pay to it.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that discrimination persists and that ending DEI removes the tools to fight it. The camp’s answer is that the law already forbids discrimination and that the right response is to enforce it, case by case and for everyone, not to impose group preferences that are themselves discriminatory. Hiring experiments show discrimination concentrated in particular firms, which calls for targeted enforcement against them, not training and quotas for everyone else. Programs open to all, such as mentoring, skills training and outreach to poor communities, remain lawful and welcome. And where gaps reflect differences in education and opportunity long before hiring, the place to address them is schooling and family stability, not the hiring desk.
Variation inside the camp: The camp divides on means. Some want institutions to end DEI voluntarily and the law to be enforced through ordinary lawsuits; others back the full use of state power, including executive orders, funding conditions for universities and schools, False Claims Act liability for contractors, bans on DEI offices in public colleges, and limits on what public colleges may teach. Some also oppose the collection of race and sex data altogether, which the EEOC has proposed to end. A libertarian strand opposes DEI but is uneasy with government dictating private employers’ policies, overlapping with the fourth camp.

Camp: Pursue DEI goals through programs open to everyone
Adherents: Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM), the largest US association of HR professionals, “SHRM’s BEAM Framework for Inclusion: Aligning Merit with Organizational Excellence” (31 January 2025), https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/inclusion-diversity/shrms-beam-framework-for-inclusion-aligning-merit-with-organizational-excellence (calling for “inclusion for all,” opportunities based on “job-related qualifications and requisite merit… without unlawful preferences or quotas,” and a shift “from optics to outcomes”; SHRM dropped “equity” from its own framework in July 2024); Frank Dobbin of Harvard and Alexandra Kalev of Tel Aviv University, “Achieve DEI Goals Without DEI Programs,” Harvard Business Review (July–August 2025), https://hbr.org/2025/07/achieve-dei-goals-without-dei-programs (arguing that recruitment, skills development, mentoring and performance-based retention open to all employees boost diversity “without inviting the backlash that formal DEI programs can incur”). Also: the many large employers that have renamed DEI around inclusion and belonging and opened group-restricted programs to all employees; the Conference Board’s description of companies “selectively reframing” rather than abandoning commitments; and the researcher and consultant Lily Zheng.
Case: The goals of DEI were right and the methods were often wrong. Workplaces and schools should be fair, should draw on talent from every group and should make everyone feel they belong, but the tools that dominated the DEI boom, mandatory training, implicit-bias workshops, identity-restricted programs and demographic targets, either did not work or provoked the backlash that now threatens the whole project. Decades of company data show what does work: structured and transparent hiring and promotion, mentoring and sponsorship, skills training, flexible scheduling, centralized recruiting, and accountability for managers. Those practices help everyone, are clearly lawful, and raise diversity as a by-product without dividing workers by identity. Inclusion for all is not a retreat but a more durable and more defensible way to reach the same ends.
Best evidence: Mentoring, task forces and targeted recruitment were followed by 9 to 30 percent gains in management diversity, while mandatory training was followed by losses (Dobbin and Kalev, more than 800 firms over three decades). Firms with centralized recruiting showed smaller racial callback gaps in the largest US hiring experiment (Kline, Rose and Walters). [Correction 2026-10-05: “largest recent” is unsupported; the page calls the 2019 to 2021 experiment “one of the largest US hiring experiments” (Kline, Rose and Walters, https://www.nber.org/papers/w29053).] Diversity training works best as part of a broader, sustained effort (Bezrukova and colleagues, 260 studies). Board diversity at S&P 500 companies barely moved even as diverse-slate policies fell from 58 to 12 percent of boards (Spencer Stuart, 2026), consistent with outcomes holding when methods change.
Answer to the strongest objection: From the first camp, the strongest objection is that “inclusion for all” is surrender dressed as reform: that without targets, data and programs aimed at specific groups, attention drifts and gaps return. The camp answers that it keeps measurement and accountability, the tools that work, and drops only the tools that did not, and that programs open to all can still be designed to reach the people most often excluded. From the second camp, the objection is that this is DEI under a new name. The camp answers that the test is conduct, not vocabulary: practices that allocate nothing by race or sex are lawful and fair, whatever they are called.
Variation inside the camp: HR practitioners and many companies emphasize rebranding and legal risk management, keeping existing programs while removing eligibility limits and changing language. Researchers emphasize evidence, arguing for evaluating every program and dropping those that fail. Some in the camp keep internal demographic goals and data while ending public targets; others move to measuring only process fairness, such as pay audits and promotion-rate checks.

Camp: Protect free inquiry from both DEI mandates and DEI bans
Adherents: Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression (FIRE), “DEI in higher ed: When it’s constitutional and when it’s not,” https://www.fire.org/research-learn/dei-higher-ed-when-its-constitutional-and-when-its-not (opposing DEI practices that restrict or compel speech or impose ideological litmus tests in hiring and promotion, while also opposing state bans on teaching about race and sex such as Florida’s Stop WOKE Act: “All ideas should be open to discussion and debate, and all topics should be freely taught”), and its model Intellectual Freedom Protection Act (February 2023), which bans political litmus tests “without regard to viewpoint or ideology” while preserving anti-discrimination enforcement and teaching about diversity, https://www.fire.org/news/new-fire-model-legislation-takes-dei-bureaucracys-chilling-effect-campus; Heterodox Academy, “Required DEI Statements,” https://heterodoxacademy.org/issues/required-dei-statements/ (opposing mandatory diversity statements as compelled speech that undermines open inquiry, and promoting alternatives such as service statements, viewpoint diversity and institutional neutrality). Also: the University of Chicago’s Kalven Report tradition of institutional neutrality and the universities that have adopted it; and FIRE’s president Greg Lukianoff.
Case: For universities and schools, the most damaging part of the DEI fight is not about who gets hired but about what may be said and taught. Required diversity statements made a contested set of political views a condition of academic employment, and campus speech codes and bias-reporting systems chilled debate. Now governments are doing the same thing in reverse, banning concepts from classrooms, defunding courses and threatening institutions over what they teach. Both are attacks on the same principle: that institutions devoted to inquiry must not impose orthodoxy, whether the orthodoxy is progressive or conservative. The right answer is not to pick a winner but to protect speech and teaching from both directions: ban loyalty oaths and litmus tests of any viewpoint, keep universities institutionally neutral on contested public questions, and let diversity and inclusion be discussed, taught and argued about freely.
Best evidence: A federal court vacated the Education Department’s February 2025 anti-DEI directive in part on First Amendment grounds, finding it caused educators to fear that lawful and even beneficial speech could be punished (American Federation of Teachers v. Department of Education, August 2025). MIT dropped required diversity statements in faculty hiring in May 2024, its president Sally Kornbluth saying that “compelled statements impinge on freedom of expression, and they don’t work” (WBUR, https://www.wbur.org/news/2024/05/07/massachusetts-institute-technology-diversity-statements), and Harvard’s Faculty of Arts and Sciences replaced them with service statements in June 2024 (Harvard Crimson, https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2024/6/4/dei-faculty-hiring-stopped/). Heterodox Academy tracks a wave of institutional neutrality policies adopted across US universities since 2023 (https://heterodoxacademy.org/announcements/heterodox-academy-releases-report-tracking-institutional-statement-neutrality-policy-wave-across-the-academy/).
Answer to the strongest objection: From the second camp, the objection is that neutrality lets entrenched DEI bureaucracies survive, and that only legislation can dislodge them. The camp answers that legislation can and should ban compelled speech and identity-based preferences in a viewpoint-neutral way, as its model law does, but that banning ideas from classrooms hands the next government the same power to ban other ideas. From the first camp, the objection is that “free speech” has been used to shield harassment and to end inclusion work. The camp answers that anti-discrimination and harassment law still apply in full and that inclusion programs that do not compel belief are untouched.
Variation inside the camp: Some members focus narrowly on higher education and speech, taking no position on workplace DEI or admissions; others oppose DEI bureaucracies generally on the ground that they drift into policing speech. The camp also divides over the Trump administration’s pressure on universities, with most opposing funding threats and compacts as government coercion and a minority seeing them as a necessary corrective.

Camp: Target help by economic disadvantage instead of race and sex
Adherents: Richard Kahlenberg, director of the American Identity Project at the Progressive Policy Institute and author of Class Matters (2025), https://www.progressivepolicy.org/kahlenberg-in-the-new-york-times-affirmative-action-is-gone-can-class-based-admissions-replace-it/ (24 March 2025, arguing that a “race-based system of affirmative action pits the white working class against Black people” and calling for “a liberal politics of class rather than race”); Walter Benn Michaels, author of The Trouble with Diversity (2006) and, with Adolph Reed Jr., No Politics but Class Politics (2023), https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/diversity-or-equality-talk-walter-benn-michaels/ (arguing that the focus on diversity distracted from economic inequality and that elite institutions celebrate representation while doing little for the poor). Also: the many selective colleges that expanded recruitment and aid by income and first-generation status after 2023, among them MIT; and conservatives who endorse economic rather than racial criteria, which the Treasury’s 2026 proposed rules for private schools expressly permit.
Case: The deepest divide in American opportunity is class, and DEI mostly ignored it. Programs organized around race and sex disproportionately reward the most advantaged members of each group, the children of professionals who attend the same schools as everyone else at the top, while poor and working-class Americans of every race are left out. Using race as a proxy for disadvantage is both over- and under-inclusive, divides working people against one another and has proven politically unsustainable. Targeting help by family income, wealth, neighborhood, school quality and first-generation status reaches the people who actually face the steepest barriers, is clearly lawful, enjoys broad public support, and, because poverty is concentrated among Black and Hispanic Americans, still produces substantial racial diversity. Diversity without equality is a hollow achievement; the goal should be opportunity for those who have the least of it.
Best evidence: Half of Americans disapproved of colleges considering race in admissions in 2023, a view shared by majorities of white and Asian adults (Pew). Kahlenberg points to preliminary admissions results after the 2023 ruling, analyzed in Class Matters, as showing that colleges using economic preferences and recruitment can sustain racial and economic diversity without racial preferences. Economic criteria such as family income, first-generation status and hardship are expressly permitted even under the most restrictive current federal proposals.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from the first camp, is that class-based programs do not reproduce racial diversity, because there are more poor white Americans than poor Black Americans in absolute numbers and because racial discrimination operates at every income level. The camp answers that well-designed class preferences that count wealth and neighborhood, not just income, recover much of the racial diversity, that ending legacy and donor preferences would recover more, and that a policy which reaches the poor of every race is more just and more durable than one that a majority of the public and now the courts reject. From the second camp, the objection is that any preference is unfair to the individual displaced. The camp answers that weighing hardship is weighing merit: a high score earned against long odds says more about ability than the same score earned with every advantage.
Variation inside the camp: A left wing, including Michaels and Reed, sees DEI as a corporate substitute for unions, wages and public goods and wants a politics of economic redistribution rather than better admissions formulas. A center-left wing, represented by Kahlenberg, focuses on class-based affirmative action and on ending legacy, donor and early-decision preferences that favor the affluent. Conservatives who adopt the idea tend to favor it as a lawful alternative for institutions that want diversity, without endorsing preferences of any kind as an obligation.
## Crux

The question under the others: when an institution finds that some groups are persistently underrepresented, is the fair response to make its procedures neutral and stop there, or to take steps aimed at the groups themselves, and which of the specific steps it might take actually reduce unfairness rather than adding new unfairness of their own?

What evidence could settle: How much of today’s gap in hiring, promotion, pay and admissions comes from discrimination inside institutions, and how much from differences that arise earlier, in schooling, family resources, neighborhoods and choices; field experiments, longitudinal company records and studies that follow people across careers can narrow this. Which specific programs change outcomes: whether mentoring, structured hiring, centralized recruiting, accountability and transparent pay raise the representation and advancement of underrepresented groups, whether training and diversity statements do anything durable, and whether group-restricted programs work better than the same programs opened to all. Whether DEI programs provoke backlash that worsens relations among groups, or improve the sense of belonging they aim at. Whether class-based measures can reach the same people that race- and sex-based ones did. And whether the corporate retreat changes who gets hired and promoted, which the next few years of workforce data could show, if that data continues to be collected. Progress on these questions would move people: evidence that discrimination is large and that open-to-all practices close gaps strengthens the hybrid camp against both poles; evidence that gaps arise mostly before hiring strengthens those who would redirect effort to schools and class; evidence that the retreat reopens gaps strengthens those who want DEI restored.

What no data can close: Whether fairness is a property of each individual decision or also of the pattern of results across groups. Someone who holds that every use of race or sex wrongs the individual affected will oppose group-conscious measures even if they work; someone who holds that a fair society must actively undo the effects of past and present exclusion will support them even at some cost to strict individual equality. Related choices sit alongside it: how much weight to give the interests of an applicant displaced by a preference against those of a group that has been shut out; whether race, sex or economic disadvantage is the injustice that most demands a remedy; and how far government should go in dictating what employers, colleges and schools may do, measure and teach. Evidence can show what each approach does; it cannot tell a society which of these commitments to put first.
