# Algorithmic Fairness — research

## Overview

Software now makes or shapes many of the decisions that decide who gets a loan and at what price, whose résumé a recruiter ever sees, which neighborhoods police patrol, whose face a police search returns, and whether a defendant waits for trial at home or in jail. This topic covers the argument over whether those automated decisions are fair to the groups that anti-discrimination law protects, chiefly by race, sex, age and disability, and what fairness should even mean for them: whether a system is fair when it treats every applicant by the same rule, when its errors fall equally on every group, or when its outcomes do; whether it should be judged against a perfect standard or against the human judgment it replaces; who answers for a biased result, the vendor that built the tool or the lender, employer or police force that used it; and whether the answer is audits and disclosure, the existing civil rights laws, or a ban on some uses altogether. It covers three fields named in the scope: lending (credit scoring, underwriting and pricing, including mortgages), hiring (résumé screening, assessments, video interviews and ranking platforms) and policing (place-based and person-based crime prediction, police face recognition, and the pretrial and sentencing risk scores that follow an arrest). It deliberately excludes medical and insurance algorithms, welfare and benefit fraud detection, social media ranking and content moderation, and bias in what chatbots say, except where a study of them bears directly on these three fields. Mass surveillance and the privacy cost of police technology belong to Privacy vs. Surveillance; diversity programs in general belong to DEI in Institutions; bail and sentencing policy as such belong to Crime, Punishment & Reform.

Why now: in 2026 the United States government moved to withdraw the legal doctrine that most algorithmic fairness enforcement has rested on. Disparate-impact liability lets a person challenge a neutral practice, such as a credit model or a hiring test, because its results fall disproportionately on a protected group, without proving anyone meant to discriminate. On April 22, 2026 the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau published a final rule, effective July 21, 2026, deleting the “effects test” from the regulation that implements the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and taking the position that the act does not authorize disparate-impact liability (CFPB, https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/final-rules/equal-credit-opportunity-act-regulation-b/; Husch Blackwell, https://www.huschblackwell.com/newsandinsights/cfpb-finalizes-major-regulation-b-overhaul-disparate-impact-out-discouragement-narrowed-and-spcps-restricted). On June 9, 2026 the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel told the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission that its long-standing reading of disparate impact under Title VII, including the 1978 Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, is unconstitutional (https://www.justice.gov/olc/media/1444871/dl?inline), and the commission’s regulatory agenda projected a final rule rescinding the interpretive parts of those guidelines in November 2026 (Ogletree, July 7, 2026, https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/the-eeocs-regulatory-agenda-10-signs-of-intent/). The states split. Colorado, whose 2024 law was the first broad state law against “algorithmic discrimination,” repealed and replaced it on May 14, 2026, dropping the duty to prevent algorithmic discrimination and the impact assessments, after Elon Musk’s xAI sued and the Justice Department intervened to argue the law violates equal protection (Crowell, https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/colorado-hits-reset-on-ai-regulation-sb-26-189-repeals-and-reenacts-the-colorado-ai-act; Justice Department, April 24, 2026, https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-intervenes-xai-lawsuit-challenging-colorados-algorithmic-discrimination). On September 17, 2026, the attorneys general of fourteen states issued joint guidance that disparate-impact liability remains enforceable and urged lenders and landlords to test algorithmic tools for discriminatory outcomes (https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/httpswwwmassgovdocmultistate-guidance-antidiscrimination-protections-9172026download.pdf). In the courts, the largest algorithmic hiring case, Mobley v. Workday, reached its next turn on November 10, 2026, when Workday asked the judge to decertify a nationwide age-discrimination collective of more than 15,000 applicants (MLex, https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2548823). Abroad, the European Union pushed back the date its AI Act’s rules for high-risk systems in hiring, credit scoring and law enforcement apply, to December 2, 2027 (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published July 24, 2026; Cuatrecasas, https://www.cuatrecasas.com/en/spain/intellectual-property/art/digital-omnibus-ai-has-been-published), and on November 4, 2026 Britain announced a specific law for police facial recognition under which police technologies must be “rigorously and independently tested against bias or discrimination of age, gender or race” (GOV.UK, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/stronger-laws-for-police-using-facial-recognition-technology).

The debate is live because the evidence cuts more than one way and the definitions themselves are in dispute. Audits have found real disparities: a risk score whose false alarms fell twice as often on Black defendants, face analysis that failed darker-skinned women far more often than lighter-skinned men, lending models and résumé screeners that penalized proxies for race, sex or age. Yet other studies find that well-built models can approve more applicants, price credit more evenly and release more defendants than the humans they replace, and a proof from computer science shows that the most popular fairness measures cannot all be met at once when groups differ in their underlying rates. The participants include civil rights and consumer groups such as the ACLU, the Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights, the National Consumer Law Center and the Algorithmic Justice League; the Trump administration, the Manhattan Institute and xAI, who argue that outcome-based rules push companies toward racial balancing; business groups such as the US Chamber of Commerce, who want existing law and a single national standard rather than state AI statutes; abolitionist groups such as the Stop LAPD Spying Coalition and Amnesty International, who want predictive policing banned; economists and lenders such as Sendhil Mullainathan and Upstart, who argue algorithms can be made fairer than people; and the vendors, employers, banks, police forces, courts, state legislatures, attorneys general and European regulators who must decide what to use and on what terms.

The two findings are both true of the same data, and the clash between them launched the modern fairness debate.

Label: Face recognition false positive differences across demographic groups
Value: 10 to beyond 100
Unit: times higher false positive rate for the most affected groups than the least
Viz: bar
Data year: 2019 (189 algorithms from 99 developers)
Source label: National Institute of Standards and Technology, Face Recognition Vendor Test Part 3: Demographic Effects, NISTIR 8280 (December 2019)
Source URL: https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8280.pdf
Note: Across 189 mostly commercial algorithms, NIST found that false positive rates often varied by factors of 10 to beyond 100 times across demographic groups.
Context: NIST processed 18.27 million images of 8.49 million people. With high-quality application photos, false positives were highest for West and East African and East Asian people and lowest for Eastern Europeans, though some algorithms developed in China reversed the East Asian result; with US mugshots, false positives were highest for American Indians, with elevated rates for African American and Asian people. False positives were higher for women than men and for the elderly and children. NIST stressed the spread between vendors: the most accurate algorithms produce many fewer errors, and some highly accurate one-to-many search algorithms had undetectable false positive differences. It concluded that it is “incumbent upon the system owner to know their algorithm,” which is why both those who would regulate police use and those who would ban it cite the report.

Value: 14

Viz: bar

Source label: ACLU, press release on the wrongful jailing of a Maryland woman (April 14, 2026)

Note: The ACLU counts 14 people publicly known to have been wrongfully arrested by US police because of reliance on face recognition, as of April 2026.
Context: The count is the ACLU’s, an organization that campaigns against police face recognition, and it covers only publicly documented cases; police often do not disclose that a face search led to a suspect, so the true number is unknown, and other trackers give somewhat different totals. The first widely reported case was Robert Williams, arrested in Detroit in 2020; in a June 2024 settlement, Detroit police agreed not to arrest people based solely on face recognition results or on photo lineups that directly follow a face search (ACLU, https://aclu.org/press-releases/civil-rights-advocates-achieve-the-nations-strongest-police-department-policy-on-facial-recognition-technology). Police departments and vendors respond that a match is a lead, not probable cause, and that the wrongful arrests followed failures of investigation, such as skipping corroboration, rather than of the software alone. Both readings point to the same policy question: what a match should and should not be allowed to justify.

Data year: New York City arrests, 2008–2013
Source label: Jon Kleinberg, Himabindu Lakkaraju, Jure Leskovec, Jens Ludwig and Sendhil Mullainathan, Human Decisions and Machine Predictions, NBER Working Paper 23180 (published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2018)
Source URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w23180
Note: In a policy simulation, a machine-learning release rule could have cut crime by up to 24.8 percent with no change in jailing rates, or cut the jail population by 42.0 percent with no increase in crime, while reducing the share of Black and Hispanic defendants in jail.
Context: The authors trained a model on 758,027 defendants arrested in New York City between 2008 and 2013 and compared its predictions with judges’ actual release decisions: judges released 48.5 percent of the defendants the model placed in the riskiest 1 percent, whose predicted crime risk was 62.6 percent. It is the main empirical anchor for the view that algorithms can be fairer and more accurate than people.

## Questions

Question: Do automated decisions treat protected groups better or worse than the human decisions they replace?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Fairer than people / Bias at scale
Why contested: Most audits measure an algorithm against an ideal of no disparity, while most defenses measure it against the loan officer, recruiter or judge it replaced, and the two comparisons give different answers. Good comparisons are rare because the human decisions being replaced were seldom recorded in a form anyone could audit, and because a tool’s effect depends on how people use it.
Case for fairer than people: Human judgment is the baseline, and it is badly biased and invisible. In mortgage pricing, Bartlett, Morse, Stanton and Wallace found that algorithmic lenders discriminated 40 percent less than face-to-face lenders and did not discriminate in approvals at all (NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w25943). In New York City pretrial cases, Kleinberg and colleagues found that judges released nearly half of the defendants a model rated riskiest, and that a model-based rule could have cut the jail population by about 42 percent with no rise in crime while reducing the share of Black and Hispanic defendants jailed (NBER, https://www.nber.org/papers/w23180). Upstart reported to the CFPB that its model approved 27 percent more applicants at 16 percent lower average APRs, with gains for near-prime, young and lower-income borrowers (CFPB, 2019, https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/update-credit-access-and-no-action-letter/). An algorithm’s bias, unlike a person’s, can be measured on every decision, retested after a fix and corrected; a biased person mostly cannot. Even the public senses this: in a Pew survey, 47 percent of Americans said AI would do better than humans at evaluating all job applicants in the same way, and 15 percent worse (https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/04/20/ai-in-hiring-and-evaluating-workers-what-americans-think/).
Case for bias at scale: Algorithms learn from the records of past human decisions, so they inherit the bias in those records and then apply it to millions of people at once, with an aura of objectivity that makes it harder to challenge. Amazon abandoned an experimental résumé-rating tool after, trained on résumés submitted mostly by men, it began to penalize résumés containing the word “women” (BBC, October 10, 2018, https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-45809919). AI text models used to rank résumés favored white-associated names in 85.1 percent of comparisons in one 2024 study (Wilson and Caliskan, https://arxiv.org/html/2407.20371v1). The simulated gains from pretrial algorithms have not appeared in practice: when Kentucky made risk assessment mandatory in 2011, release rates rose only slightly and the change eroded within a couple of years as judges returned to old habits (Stevenson, Minnesota Law Review, https://www.courts.wa.gov/subsite/mjc/docs/2019/Assessing%20Risk%20Assessment_Stevenson.pdf). And COMPAS, used to inform decisions about real defendants, proved no more accurate than untrained people recruited online (Dressel and Farid, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777393/). One biased loan officer harms the applicants at one branch; one biased vendor model, like the Workday platform through which about 1.1 billion applications were rejected, reaches everyone who applies through it.

Question: Should a decision be unlawful because its results fall unequally on a protected group, even when no one meant to discriminate?
Tag: value
Poles: Outcomes count / Intent required
Why contested: This is the doctrine of disparate impact, and it is the legal fork of the moment. Since Griggs v. Duke Power in 1971, federal law has let people challenge neutral practices whose effects fall disproportionately on a protected group unless the user shows the practice is justified and no less discriminatory alternative would serve; in 2025 and 2026 the federal executive branch moved to abandon it, while fourteen states said they would keep enforcing it (CRS, October 20, 2026, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R49490/R49490.1.pdf). Algorithms sharpen the question because a model rarely “intends” anything.
Case for outcomes count: An algorithm almost never discriminates on purpose; it discriminates because its training data, its target or its inputs carry the imprint of past discrimination, through proxies such as zip code, school, gaps in employment or a college’s loan default rate. If the law reaches only intentional discrimination, most algorithmic discrimination becomes legal by definition, since there is no one whose intent to prove and the model is too complex to read. The Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights argues that disparate impact is therefore the doctrine best suited to AI, and that “disparate impact liability remains the law” despite the federal retreat (January 2026, https://civilrights.org/resource/disparate-impact/). The test is not a quota: a lender or employer whose model produces a gap can defend it as necessary, and loses only if a less discriminatory alternative would serve as well. The Massachusetts attorney general’s 2025 settlement with the student lender Earnest, over a model input that allegedly penalized Black and Hispanic applicants, shows the kind of case that would vanish without it (Consumer Finance Monitor, https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2025/07/18/massachusetts-ag-reaches-settlement-with-student-loan-company-earnest-operations-llc/).
Case for intent required: Equal protection means treating each person the same regardless of race or sex, not producing equal results across groups. When liability turns on group outcomes alone, a lender or employer can avoid it only by watching the racial results of its decisions and adjusting them, which is itself race-based decision-making. The Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel concluded in June 2026 that the EEOC’s approach “functions as a qualified racial-proportionality mandate and spurs employers to engage in race-based decisionmaking to avoid liability,” and that disparate impact should reach only practices that suggest a strong likelihood of intentional discrimination (https://www.justice.gov/olc/media/1444871/dl?inline). Applied to AI, the same logic means a law forcing models to avoid disparate impact would require them to produce less accurate predictions to hit demographic targets; the White House said in December 2025 that Colorado’s law “may even force AI models to produce false results” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/). Those who hold this view argue that the remaining real problem, a model using a proxy to stand in for race, can be targeted directly without a general outcomes test (Manhattan Institute, October 2026, https://manhattan.institute/article/the-future-of-disparate-impact).

Question: When fairness measures conflict, should a risk score have equal error rates across groups or mean the same risk for every group?
Tag: value
Poles: Equal error rates / Same score, same risk
Why contested: In 2016 ProPublica showed that COMPAS wrongly flagged Black defendants who did not reoffend as higher risk at nearly twice the rate of white defendants, while Northpointe showed that a given score meant about the same likelihood of reoffending for both groups (ProPublica, https://www.propublica.org/article/how-we-analyzed-the-compas-recidivism-algorithm; https://www.propublica.org/article/technical-response-to-northpointe). Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan then proved that “except in highly constrained special cases, there is no method that can satisfy these three conditions simultaneously,” meaning calibration and balanced error rates cannot all hold when groups differ in their underlying rates (arXiv 1609.05807, https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807). So the choice is not between a fair and an unfair tool, but between kinds of fairness.
Case for equal error rates: The harm that matters is to the person wrongly labeled: the Black defendant held in jail who would not have reoffended, or the creditworthy applicant wrongly denied. If those errors fall twice as often on one group, the group bears a burden the other does not, whatever the score means on average. Because the gap in base rates, such as rearrest rates, partly reflects where police patrol and whom they arrest, calibrating to those rates builds past discrimination into the score. A tool that cannot equalize its mistakes across groups should have its thresholds adjusted or not be used for decisions about liberty.
Case for same score, same risk: A score should mean the same thing whatever the defendant’s race; if a 7 meant a 60 percent risk for one group and 40 percent for another, judges would have to adjust scores by race, which is exactly what anti-discrimination law forbids. When groups truly differ in rates of the predicted outcome, any calibrated score will produce different error rates, so forcing equal error rates means applying different standards to individuals by group. ProPublica’s own analysis found COMPAS predicted correctly for Black and white defendants at roughly the same rates, 63 and 59 percent. The better remedy for unequal base rates is to address their causes, and to choose what is predicted carefully, not to bend the measurement.

Question: Should predictive tools be used in policing and pretrial decisions at all?
Tag: value
Poles: Use with safeguards / Ban them
Why contested: The stakes are liberty rather than a loan, the data are police records rather than repayment histories, and the evidence on accuracy is weak for some tools and strong for others. Governments have gone both ways: the European Union banned person-based crime prediction based solely on profiling (https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/), while Britain is expanding live facial recognition under a new law that requires bias testing (GOV.UK, November 4, 2026, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/stronger-laws-for-police-using-facial-recognition-technology).
Case for use with safeguards: Police and courts make predictions anyway, about who is dangerous, where crime will happen, and whether a face on a camera matches a suspect, and the alternative to a tested tool is an untested human hunch. Face recognition at a strict threshold made no false matches for any group in the Home Office’s 2025 test (NPL, https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/693002a4cdec734f4dff4149/1a_Cognitec_NPL_Equitability_Report_October_25.pdf), and NIST found some leading algorithms had undetectable differences across groups (https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8280.pdf). The answer to the wrongful arrests is rules such as Detroit’s, which bars an arrest based solely on a face match, not abandoning a tool that helps identify suspects in serious crimes. Pretrial risk tools let jurisdictions release more people without money bail, and simulations show large gains in both safety and freedom when judges’ inconsistent judgments are disciplined by data.
Case for ban them: Policing data record where police looked, not where crime happened, so prediction sends officers back to the same neighborhoods, generates more stops and arrests there, and confirms itself. Amnesty International UK found almost three-quarters of UK forces use crime-predicting technology and concluded that “these systems have been built with discriminatory data and serve only to supercharge racism” (February 20, 2025, https://www.amnesty.org.uk/latest/uk-police-forces-supercharging-racism-crime-predicting-tech-new-report/). Many tools also simply do not work: fewer than 100 of 23,631 Geolitica predictions in Plainfield matched a reported crime (The Markup, https://themarkup.org/prediction-bias/2023/10/02/predictive-policing-software-terrible-at-predicting-crimes). The Pretrial Justice Institute, once a leading promoter of risk tools, concluded in 2020 that they “perpetuate structural racism and institutional inequities” and “can no longer be part of the solution” (https://www.pretrial.org/research/updated-position-on-pretrial-risk-assessment-tools). Safeguards depend on police disclosing their use, which the ACLU’s count of 14 publicly known wrongful arrests suggests they often do not.

Question: Do bias audits and disclosure requirements actually reduce discrimination by automated systems?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Audits work / Paper compliance
Why contested: Audit mandates are the main policy instrument of the regulatory camp, and the evidence from the first one is discouraging, but the problem may lie in that law’s design rather than in audits as such. Little evidence yet links any audit regime to measurably fairer outcomes for applicants.
Case for audits work: Every documented case of algorithmic bias in this field was found by testing, from Gender Shades and COMPAS to the independent monitorship of Upstart, which found approval disparities for Black applicants that the lender’s own reports had not shown (Student Borrower Protection Center, March 27, 2024, https://protectborrowers.org/ldf-sbpc-and-upstart-announce-final-monitorship-report-on-ai-and-fair-lending/). Audits make problems visible, and visibility is what enables enforcement, as in the Earnest settlement, which requires annual fair lending testing. New York City’s weak results reflect a law that let employers decide for themselves whether they were covered and an agency that received only two complaints in two years and did not check whether its complaint line worked (New York State Comptroller, December 2025, https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf); a law with clear coverage, access to data and real penalties would work better. Even critics of disparate impact, such as the Manhattan Institute, propose that employers who audit their systems in advance get some liability protection.
Case for paper compliance: The first audit law produced almost no audits: 18 of 391 New York City employers posted one (Wright et al., FAccT 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01399v1), and the state comptroller found at least 17 instances of potential non-compliance where the city found one. An audit tests what the auditor chooses to measure on data the company supplies, usually a selection-rate ratio, and can bless a tool that is invalid or discriminatory in ways the ratio misses. Colorado, after two years of trying to write workable rules, dropped its impact assessments and risk management mandate entirely in 2026 in favor of notice, explanation and human review (Crowell, https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/colorado-hits-reset-on-ai-regulation-sb-26-189-repeals-and-reenacts-the-colorado-ai-act). Meanwhile, the people harmed often cannot get the data to prove harm: in Mobley v. Workday, the court refused to order the vendor to produce its customers’ applicant data (CRS, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R49490/R49490.1.pdf).

Question: Should models be allowed to use race, sex or other protected traits in order to correct for bias?
Tag: value
Poles: Use to correct / Keep models blind
Why contested: Anti-discrimination law has generally forbidden using protected traits in decisions, and lenders are largely barred from even collecting race for non-mortgage credit, yet computer scientists have shown that a model blind to race can still discriminate through proxies and that correcting it may require knowing who is in which group. After the Supreme Court’s 2023 admissions decision, race-conscious corrections face sharper constitutional scrutiny.
Case for use to correct: Removing race from the inputs does not remove race from the model, because zip codes, names, schools and purchase histories stand in for it; the only way to detect and fix that is to measure outcomes by group, and sometimes to adjust. Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan and Rambachan found in a college admissions example that “the inclusion of variables such as race can increase both equity and efficiency,” and argued that fairness goals should shape how predictions are used, not blind the predictor (American Economic Association Papers and Proceedings, 2018, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20181018). Even firms that want to test must now guess: Upstart does not collect demographic data and estimates race from surname and location (https://www.upstart.com/lenders/regulatory-compliance/access-to-credit-report).
Case for keep models blind: Using race to adjust a model’s outputs is treating applicants differently by race, which is what civil rights law was written to forbid, whatever the motive. The Justice Department challenged Colorado’s 2024 law in part because it exempted from “algorithmic discrimination” differential treatment meant to increase diversity or redress historic discrimination (Justice Department, April 24, 2026, https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-intervenes-xai-lawsuit-challenging-colorados-algorithmic-discrimination). The remedy for proxy discrimination is to remove or justify the proxy, as the Massachusetts settlement with Earnest did by ending the use of a school default-rate variable, not to add race back in. Those who hold this view also warn that collecting more demographic data creates new risks of misuse.

Question: Who should answer for a biased automated decision: the vendor that built the tool or the lender, employer or police force that used it?
Tag: value
Poles: Hold vendors liable / Hold users liable
Why contested: Civil rights law was written for employers, lenders and landlords, not for the software companies whose products now shape their decisions, and a handful of vendors serve thousands of users. The user usually cannot see inside the model; the vendor usually does not see the user’s applicants.
Case for hold vendors liable: The vendor designs the model, chooses its training data and sells it to thousands of employers or lenders, so it is the only party that can find and fix a bias at its source; suing each small employer is slow and lets the same flaw recur everywhere. The court in Mobley v. Workday held in 2024 that, on the facts alleged, Workday could be liable as the employers’ agent (CRS, citing 740 F. Supp. 3d 796, https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R49490/R49490.1.pdf). Colorado’s 2026 law voids contract clauses by which a party tries to shift liability for its own discriminatory use of automated decision tools to someone else (Crowell). The AI Civil Rights Act proposed by Senator Ed Markey and Representative Yvette Clarke would put evaluation duties on developers as well as deployers (ACLU, December 3, 2025, https://www.aclu.org/news/racial-justice/ai-is-infringing-on-your-civil-rights-heres-how-we-can-stop-that).
Case for hold users liable: The employer or lender makes the decision, chooses the tool, configures it, sets its thresholds and decides whether to follow its recommendations, so the existing law that already holds the decision-maker responsible is enough. Workday argues that its tools do not make hiring decisions, its customers do (Proskauer, https://www.proskauer.com/blog/ai-bias-lawsuit-against-workday-reaches-next-stage-as-court-grants-conditional-certification-of-adea-claim). Colorado’s lawmakers rewrote their law partly because companies worried about being held responsible for harmful outcomes of technology they did not build, in the words of Assistant House Majority Leader Jennifer Bacon (Denver7, https://www.denver7.com/news/local-news/colorado-lawmakers-approve-rewrite-of-landmark-ai-law-addressing-algorithmic-discrimination). Vendor liability for every customer’s use would push developers to withdraw products or to bury them in disclaimers.

Question: Should each state set its own rules for automated decisions, or should one national standard override them?
Tag: value
Poles: State rules / One national rule
Why contested: Congress has passed no general law on algorithmic discrimination, and the federal executive branch is withdrawing from disparate-impact enforcement, so the states are where new rules are made, and the administration and business groups are trying to stop them. In July 2025 the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip a moratorium on state AI laws from the budget bill (PBS, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/senate-pulls-ai-regulatory-ban-from-gop-bill-after-complaints-from-states); in December 2025 the president ordered a federal litigation task force to challenge state AI laws and named Colorado’s (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/).
Case for state rules: States have always enforced their own civil rights and consumer protection laws, and with federal agencies retreating, they are the only remaining public enforcers; fourteen attorneys general said in September 2026 that they would keep enforcing disparate-impact liability (https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/httpswwwmassgovdocmultistate-guidance-antidiscrimination-protections-9172026download.pdf). Preemption without a federal replacement would leave people with no protection at all. States can also test different designs, as Colorado, New York City, Illinois and California have, and learn which work.
Case for one national rule: Lenders, employers and AI developers operate across all fifty states, and a patchwork of conflicting definitions of “algorithmic discrimination,” audit formats and disclosure rules raises costs, favors the largest companies and slows adoption of tools that may outperform human judgment. The US Chamber of Commerce says Congress must stop states “from doing Congress’s job” and that policymakers should rely on existing laws and voluntary standards (2026 policy priorities, https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/20260303-CTEC-Policy-Priorities-2026-021226.pdf). A national standard would also prevent one state from effectively setting rules for products used everywhere.

Question: Does adding new kinds of data to lending models widen fair access to credit or create new proxies for race?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Widens access / New proxies
Why contested: Lenders and fintechs argue that bank account cash flows, rent and other new data can score people that traditional credit files miss, who are disproportionately young, low-income, Black and Hispanic; consumer advocates worry that the same data carry hidden correlations with race and invite surveillance. The size of the excluded group itself shrank on revision: the CFPB cut its 2010 estimate of adults with no credit record from 11.0 to 5.8 percent and estimated 2.7 percent in 2020 (https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_update-credit-invisibles-estimate_2025-06.pdf).
Case for widens access: Traditional credit files are thin or noisy for many low-income and minority borrowers, which makes scores less accurate for exactly those people, and better data fix that at the source. FinRegLab’s 2025 study, using loan-level data from six firms, found that a machine-learning model combining credit bureau and cash-flow data was the most predictive overall and across all subgroups and produced the highest approval rates (https://finreglab.org/research/advancing-the-credit-ecosystem-machine-learning-cash-flow-data-in-consumer-underwriting/). Upstart reports approving 52 percent more Black applicants than a traditional model (https://www.upstart.com/lenders/regulatory-compliance/access-to-credit-report).
Case for new proxies: The more data a model uses, the more ways it can reconstruct race without being told it. The Massachusetts attorney general alleged that Earnest’s use of a school’s student loan default rate penalized Black and Hispanic applicants (Consumer Finance Monitor). A study reviewed by FinRegLab found that more accurate machine-learning mortgage models raised approvals modestly for all groups, most for Black borrowers, but widened the spread of predicted risk in a way that increased pricing disparities between groups (Fuster and colleagues, summarized in FinRegLab, 2025, https://finreglab.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/FinRegLab_07-01-2025_Advancing-the-Credit-Ecosystem-Main.pdf). The National Consumer Law Center warns that weakened fair lending rules will bring “an uptick in digital redlining” in an economy “where black box AI models advertise, offer, approve, and price credit” (https://library.nclc.org/article/cfpb-guts-core-equal-credit-opportunity-act-protections-regulation-b).

## Positions

Note on shape: the debate has two axes. One is the standard: whether an automated decision is unfair when its outcomes or errors fall unequally on a group (disparate impact) or only when it treats people differently because of a protected trait, directly or through a deliberate proxy (disparate treatment). The other is the instrument: new AI-specific duties such as audits and impact assessments, the existing civil rights and consumer laws, or a ban on some uses. Five camps hold distinct combinations. The camp that wants algorithms, built to be fairer, to replace human judgment is the hybrid, combining enthusiasm for the technology with built-in fairness constraints. The camp that wants predictive tools out of policing partly rejects the question as posed: its adherents argue that asking how to make crime prediction fair misses that the data and often the tools themselves are unsound. Many people hold different camps for different fields; the ACLU, for example, seeks audits for hiring and lending tools and a ban on police face recognition. The order below puts the regulatory camp first because it carries forward the disparate-impact enforcement of the past half century and is the approach of the EU, the equal treatment camp second because it is now the position of the US federal government, and the existing-law camp third because it is the dominant position of business.

Camp: Require bias testing and accountability for automated decisions
Adherents:
- ACLU (institution), December 3, 2025, urging Congress to pass the AI Civil Rights Act, which would require developers, deployers and independent auditors to “conduct pre-deployment evaluations, impact assessments, and annual reviews” and make it “explicitly unlawful” to use algorithms that cause a disparate impact in areas such as housing and employment: https://www.aclu.org/news/racial-justice/ai-is-infringing-on-your-civil-rights-heres-how-we-can-stop-that
- Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights (institution), report by Chiraag Bains, When Machines Discriminate: The Critical Role of Disparate Impact in AI Accountability, January 2026: “Notwithstanding the federal government’s abandonment of the doctrine, disparate impact liability remains the law”: https://civilrights.org/resource/disparate-impact/
- Senator Edward J. Markey (Democrat of Massachusetts), reintroducing the AI Civil Rights Act with Representative Yvette Clarke on December 2, 2025: “We must address AI-powered bias and discrimination in the AI age”: https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/sen-markey-rep-clarke-reintroduce-ai-civil-rights-act-to-eliminate-ai-discrimination-and-enact-guardrails-on-use-of-algorithms-in-decisions-impacting-peoples-rights-civil-liberties-livelihoods
- Attorneys general of California, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, New Mexico, New York, Oregon, Vermont and Washington (institutions), joint guidance of September 17, 2026, recommending “analyses of machine learning and algorithm-based lending and tenant screening tools throughout their lifecycle” to “identify discriminatory outcomes”: https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/httpswwwmassgovdocmultistate-guidance-antidiscrimination-protections-9172026download.pdf
- Also in this camp: the European Union, whose AI Act classes hiring, creditworthiness and many law enforcement systems as high-risk, subject to risk management, data governance and human oversight duties from December 2, 2027 (https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/); the National Consumer Law Center; the Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law; the Algorithmic Justice League; the Center for Democracy & Technology; and the Electronic Frontier Foundation, which urged Colorado to strengthen rather than weaken its 2024 law (https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/11/strengthen-colorados-ai-act).
Case: Automated systems now decide, at a scale no human could, who is hired, who gets credit and on what terms, and whom police pursue, and they learn from records shaped by generations of discrimination. Without testing, nobody knows whether a model is reproducing that history, least of all the applicant it rejected, who never learns a machine was involved. Civil rights law already forbids discrimination, but it was built for human decision-makers whose motives could be questioned; a model has no motive, its reasoning is often opaque, and the evidence of its effects sits in data only the company holds. So the burden must shift: those who build and deploy high-stakes systems should test them for disparate impact before and after deployment, look for less discriminatory alternatives, tell people when a machine is deciding, explain adverse decisions and give them a human to appeal to. Disparate-impact liability is the legal backbone, because it reaches discrimination no one intended, which is the kind algorithms produce. With federal agencies abandoning it, states and private plaintiffs must carry it.
Best evidence: Every documented case of algorithmic bias in these fields came from testing someone did: ProPublica’s COMPAS analysis, which found Black defendants who did not reoffend were labeled higher risk at 44.85 percent against 23.45 percent for white defendants; Gender Shades; the Upstart monitorship, which found approval disparities for Black applicants; the Massachusetts attorney general’s case against Earnest over a model input that allegedly penalized Black and Hispanic applicants. Mortgage data show a Black-white denial gap of 7.8 percentage points remaining within the same lender after income, loan amount and debt ratios are accounted for (St. Louis Fed, 2026). Studies of AI résumé screening find large name-based preferences that a vendor’s aggregate accuracy figures would never show.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that outcome tests push companies toward racial balancing and that audit laws produce paperwork, as New York City’s did. Disparate impact is not a quota: a company whose model produces a gap can justify it as necessary and loses only if an equally effective, less discriminatory alternative exists, a test the Supreme Court upheld in 2015. Searching for such alternatives is ordinary model building, since there are usually many models of nearly equal accuracy and some are fairer than others. New York City’s law failed for specific reasons, self-determined coverage and complaint-only enforcement, that a better-drafted law can fix; the lesson of Colorado’s retreat is the power of industry and federal pressure, not that testing does not work.
Variation inside the camp: Members differ on whether testing should be required before deployment, as in the EU and the AI Civil Rights Act, or enforced afterward through existing law, as in the state attorneys general’s guidance; on whether vendors as well as users should be liable; and on policing, where some, like the ACLU on face recognition, go further and seek bans.

Camp: Judge automated decisions by equal treatment, not group outcomes
Adherents:
- US Department of Justice (institution). Its Office of Legal Counsel concluded on June 9, 2026 that the EEOC’s disparate-impact rules “contemplate liability based on disproportionately adverse effects alone, without regard to an employer’s likely intent,” which “functions as a qualified racial-proportionality mandate and spurs employers to engage in race-based decisionmaking”: https://www.justice.gov/olc/media/1444871/dl?inline. Intervening against Colorado’s law on April 24, 2026, Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon said, “Laws that require AI companies to infect their products with woke DEI ideology are illegal”: https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-intervenes-xai-lawsuit-challenging-colorados-algorithmic-discrimination
- Manhattan Institute (institution), report by senior fellow Robert VerBruggen, The Future of Disparate Impact, October 22, 2026, urging Congress to “pare back disparate impact along the lines that OLC suggests” while creating a targeted test for algorithms that turn job-related factors into proxies for protected traits: https://manhattan.institute/article/the-future-of-disparate-impact
- xAI (institution), complaint against Colorado filed April 9, 2026, alleging that “the State of Colorado now seeks to force xAI to abandon its disinterested pursuit of truth and instead promote the State’s ideological views on various matters, racial justice in particular”: https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cod.253513/gov.uscourts.cod.253513.1.0.pdf
- Also in this camp: the White House, whose April 23, 2025 executive order set the policy “to eliminate the use of disparate-impact liability in all contexts to the maximum degree possible” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/04/restoring-equality-of-opportunity-and-meritocracy/); the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, which removed the effects test from its fair lending rules in 2026; the EEOC under Chair Andrea Lucas; and the Reason Foundation, which criticized Colorado’s 2024 law for treating “statistical patterns of inequities” as discrimination (https://reason.org/commentary/colorados-attempt-to-regulate-ai-harms-innovation-wont-protect-consumers).
Case: Civil rights law promises each person equal treatment, not each group equal results. A credit model or hiring screen that applies the same job- or risk-related criteria to everyone is fair, even if outcomes differ, because groups differ in income, credit history, experience and many other things for reasons the lender or employer did not cause and cannot fix. When the law makes companies liable for any statistical gap they cannot defend to a regulator’s satisfaction, it gives them every reason to watch the racial results of their models and tune them until the numbers come out right, which is discrimination by race in the name of preventing it, and which the Constitution forbids. For AI the cost is sharper still: a model forced to equalize outcomes must be made less accurate, approving loans that will default or rejecting the best candidates, and denying the truthful predictions that make the technology valuable. The real wrongs, a model told to use race or built around a proxy chosen to stand in for it, are disparate treatment and remain illegal.
Best evidence: The Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan proof shows that when groups differ in base rates, a calibrated score, one that means the same risk for everyone, cannot also have equal error rates, so a rule demanding equal outcomes or errors demands a score that treats individuals differently by group. ProPublica’s own data showed COMPAS predicted reoffending correctly for Black and white defendants at about the same rates. Colorado’s 2024 law exempted from “algorithmic discrimination” differential treatment meant to increase diversity or redress historic discrimination, which the Justice Department argued shows that outcome-based regimes favor some groups over others by design. The Supreme Court in Ricci v. DeStefano (2009) held that an employer that threw out a test because of its racial results engaged in disparate treatment.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that without outcome tests, algorithmic discrimination becomes invisible and unpunishable, since no model has intent. That is why reform should target proxy discrimination directly: as the Manhattan Institute proposes, a challenger could show that an algorithm relies on factors with no reasonable relationship to the job, or that it warps job-related factors into proxies for protected characteristics, and employers who audit for that in advance would get a safe harbor. Intent-based law already reaches a developer who designs or selects a model for its discriminatory effect. What it rightly refuses to do is treat every unexplained gap as proof of wrongdoing.
Variation inside the camp: The administration acts through executive orders, agency rules and litigation, and frames state laws as forcing AI to produce “false results”; the Manhattan Institute wants Congress to write a narrower, explicit standard, including a test for algorithmic proxies, so the change does not depend on one administration. Some members would end disparate-impact liability entirely; others would keep it as evidence of hidden intent.

Camp: Rely on existing anti-discrimination law, not new AI rules
Adherents:
- US Chamber of Commerce (institution), 2026 technology policy priorities: “Congress should resist top-down, one-size-fits-all regulations that stifle innovation. Instead, policymakers should rely on existing laws and voluntary standards to address AI-related harms”: https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/20260303-CTEC-Policy-Priorities-2026-021226.pdf
- Adam Kovacevich, chief executive of Chamber of Progress, a center-left technology industry group (institution), March 19, 2025, opposing Virginia’s HB 2094: “Instead of singling out AI with a specific regulatory structure, Virginia should strengthen existing civil rights laws” “to ensure broad protections in different settings”: https://cardinalnews.org/2025/03/19/virginia-leads-the-country-in-tech-talent-one-ai-bill-on-youngkins-desk-could-throw-it-all-away/
- Governor Glenn Youngkin of Virginia, vetoing HB 2094 on March 24, 2025: “There are many laws currently in place that protect consumers and place responsibilities on companies,” and the bill’s “rigid framework fails to account for the rapidly evolving and fast-moving nature of the AI industry” (as quoted by IAPP): https://iapp.org/news/a/virginia-governor-vetoes-ai-bill
- Also in this camp: Colorado Governor Jared Polis, who in 2026 signed the replacement for Colorado’s 2024 law, keeping notice, explanation and human review but dropping the discrimination-prevention duties and impact assessments.
Case: Discrimination by an algorithm is already illegal. Title VII, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, the Fair Housing Act, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act and state civil rights and consumer laws apply to a decision whether a person or software makes it, and the cases brought so far, from Mobley v. Workday to the Massachusetts settlement with Earnest, were brought under those laws. New AI-specific statutes add vague definitions of “high-risk systems” and “algorithmic discrimination,” impact assessments and paperwork, and fifty different versions of them, while adding little protection. The costs fall hardest on smaller firms and on the employers and lenders who could gain most from tools that are more consistent than human judgment, and the result is that companies keep the biased humans and drop the auditable software. Where gaps exist, Congress should fill them with a single, risk-based national standard, sector by sector, and regulators should enforce the laws they have.
Best evidence: Colorado spent two years, including a 2025 special session that delayed the law, trying to make its 2024 law workable before repealing its central duties in 2026 in favor of disclosure and human review. New York City’s audit mandate produced audits from 18 of 391 employers checked and two complaints in two years. Chamber of Progress estimated that Virginia’s bill would have cost the state’s AI developers and deployers $290 million in compliance [unverified: figure from a secondary summary of the group’s analysis]. The major enforcement actions of recent years, including the Earnest settlement and the Workday litigation, proceeded under laws already on the books.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that existing law cannot reach algorithmic discrimination because victims cannot see the model or get the data. The cure for that is targeted: transparency to regulators, adverse action explanations already required under credit law, and discovery rules, not a new regulatory regime for every model. And if federal agencies have stepped back from disparate-impact enforcement, that is an argument about enforcement priorities under existing statutes, which private plaintiffs and state attorneys general still use, not a reason for states to build fifty separate AI codes.
Variation inside the camp: Members differ on disparate impact itself. Some, like Chamber of Progress, want existing civil rights laws strengthened and enforced; others in business are content with the federal retreat; the Reason Foundation also objects to outcome-based standards and so overlaps with the equal treatment camp. Members also differ on whether a national AI law should preempt state civil rights enforcement or only state AI-specific statutes.

Camp: Ban predictive tools from policing and criminal justice
Adherents:
- Amnesty International UK (institution), Automated Racism report, February 20, 2025; chief executive Sacha Deshmukh: “These systems have been built with discriminatory data and serve only to supercharge racism,” and “The UK Government must prohibit the use of these technologies across England and Wales”: https://www.amnesty.org.uk/latest/uk-police-forces-supercharging-racism-crime-predicting-tech-new-report/
- Pretrial Justice Institute (institution), updated position, 2020: “pretrial risk assessment tools perpetuate structural racism and institutional inequities,” and “these tools can no longer be part of the solution for building fair pretrial justice processes”: https://www.pretrial.org/research/updated-position-on-pretrial-risk-assessment-tools
- Stop LAPD Spying Coalition (institution), the Los Angeles group whose campaign preceded the end of the LAPD’s LASER program in 2019 and its PredPol program in 2020, and which describes its goal as abolishing, not reforming, data-driven policing: https://stoplapdspying.org/category/data-driven-policing/
- Also in this camp: the ACLU on police face recognition; Fair Trials in Europe, which campaigned for the AI Act’s ban on predictive policing; Ruha Benjamin, author of Race After Technology; and the cities that have banned police face recognition, beginning with San Francisco in 2019.
Case: In policing and criminal courts, the question “how do we make this algorithm fair?” starts in the wrong place. The data these tools learn from are records of police activity, arrests, stops and calls, not of crime itself, and they reflect decades of heavier policing in Black and poor neighborhoods. A tool trained on those records sends officers back to the same blocks and flags the same people, producing more records that confirm the prediction. No audit can fix that, because the bias is in what is being predicted. Many of the tools also simply do not work, and they shift responsibility for depriving people of liberty from accountable officials to software that defendants cannot cross-examine. Where the stakes are a person’s freedom, the answer is not a better score but no score: end predictive policing, end police face recognition, and replace risk assessment with release as the default.
Best evidence: Fewer than 100 of 23,631 Geolitica predictions in Plainfield matched a reported crime of the predicted type and place. COMPAS’s 137 features predicted no better than untrained people or a two-variable rule. When Kentucky mandated pretrial risk assessment, release rates rose only slightly and then fell below their earlier level within a couple of years (Stevenson). The ACLU counts 14 publicly known wrongful arrests after face recognition, and Britain’s own police database search produced false matches for Black women at 9.9 percent at one setting against 0.04 percent for white subjects. The European Union, hardly hostile to technology, prohibited person-based crime prediction based solely on profiling.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that human judgment is worse, and that banning tools leaves police and judges to their own biases. But the choice is not between a biased human and a fair machine: the machine is trained on the biased human’s records and then lends them false authority, and judges overrode risk tools when they disagreed anyway. Reducing bias in policing and pretrial detention means changing what police and courts do, fewer stops and arrests for minor offenses, release unless there is specific evidence of danger, not adding a prediction layer to the same system.
Variation inside the camp: Some members would ban only person-based prediction and face recognition, others place-based hot-spot forecasting too; the Stop LAPD Spying Coalition rejects reformed or “community” versions of data-driven policing as well. Some, like the Pretrial Justice Institute, oppose risk tools for detention decisions while supporting release-focused reforms.

Camp: Replace human judgment with algorithms built to be fairer
Adherents:
- Jon Kleinberg, Jens Ludwig, Sendhil Mullainathan and Cass Sunstein, Discrimination in the Age of Algorithms, Journal of Legal Analysis (2018): “With the appropriate requirements in place, algorithms create the potential for new forms of transparency and hence opportunities to detect discrimination that are otherwise unavailable,” and “with the right safeguards, they can be a potential positive force for equity”: https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/doi/10.1093/jla/laz001/5476086
- Upstart (institution), 2025 Access to Credit Report, which says its model approves 52 percent more Black applicants and 46 percent more Hispanic applicants than a traditional model and states, “We believe our AI underwriting model is one of the most accurate, fair, and inclusive underwriting models in the market”: https://www.upstart.com/lenders/regulatory-compliance/access-to-credit-report
- Also in this camp: the Center for Applied Artificial Intelligence at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, whose Algorithmic Bias Playbook by Ziad Obermeyer, Sendhil Mullainathan and colleagues shows organizations how to inventory, measure and retrain biased algorithms (https://www.chicagobooth.edu/research/center-for-applied-artificial-intelligence/research/algorithmic-bias/playbook); lenders and fintech firms that use machine learning and cash-flow data to reach thin-file borrowers; and supporters of pretrial risk tools such as the Public Safety Assessment as an alternative to money bail.
Case: The real choice is not between algorithms and fairness but between algorithms and people, and people are the more biased and the less correctable of the two. Loan officers, recruiters and judges carry biases they cannot see and will not admit, make inconsistent decisions and leave no record that could prove discrimination. A well-built algorithm can be inspected, tested against every group, retrained and tested again; its trade-offs between accuracy and fairness are explicit numbers rather than hidden instincts. Used properly, algorithms can find the creditworthy borrowers traditional scores miss, the strong candidates from unfamiliar schools, the defendants who can safely go home. So the goal should be to adopt them faster, with the right requirements: the data and objective disclosed to regulators, outcomes measured by group, fairness built into how predictions are used, and humans prevented from overriding them in ways that reintroduce bias.
Best evidence: Kleinberg and colleagues found that judges released nearly half of the defendants a model rated riskiest, and that a model-based rule could have cut jail populations by about 42 percent with no rise in crime while reducing the share of Black and Hispanic defendants jailed. Bartlett and colleagues found algorithmic mortgage lenders discriminated 40 percent less in pricing than face-to-face lenders and not at all in approvals. Upstart reported to the CFPB 27 percent more approvals and 16 percent lower APRs than a traditional model. Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan and Rambachan showed in an admissions example that including group information in prediction can improve both equity and efficiency.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that algorithms learn from biased data and that real-world deployments, like Kentucky’s, have not delivered the gains simulations promise. Both are problems of design and use, not of algorithms as such. Biased labels can be identified and replaced, as the Booth playbook shows; the Kentucky results came partly from judges ignoring the law’s presumption of release in more than two-thirds of cases, which argues for making the tools more binding, not abandoning them. Where an independent monitor found Upstart’s model had approval disparities, the dispute was over how much accuracy to trade for a less discriminatory model, a trade-off that only an algorithm makes visible enough to argue about.
Variation inside the camp: Academics in this camp support regulation that requires transparency to regulators and outcome measurement, and some support disparate-impact law; lenders in it generally favor existing fair lending law over new AI statutes and so overlap with the existing-law camp. They differ on whether models should use protected traits to correct bias or remain blind to them.

## Crux

When an automated system decides who gets a loan, a job interview or the attention of police, what should count as fair, and do these systems, as actually built and used, deliver more of it than the human decisions they replace?

What evidence could settle: How automated decisions compare with human ones in the same settings, measured on real outcomes rather than simulations: approval, pricing, hiring and detention rates by group, error rates by group, and repayment, job performance and reoffending, before and after adoption. Whether the gaps that remain after controlling for credit, income and qualifications reflect unequal treatment or unmeasured differences, which requires the data lenders, employers and vendors hold and seldom release. How often less discriminatory models of nearly equal accuracy exist, and what accuracy they cost. Whether audit and disclosure laws change outcomes for applicants or only produce paperwork, and whether police prediction and face recognition reduce crime enough, at the thresholds actually used, to offset their errors. Whether the records these tools learn from, such as arrests, measure the thing predicted or the behavior of those who made the records.

What stays a value choice: Which kind of fairness should govern when, as the mathematics shows, they cannot all hold at once: treating each person by the same rule, giving each group the same error rates, or giving each group comparable outcomes. Whether the law should judge a decision by its intent or by its effects, and how much accuracy, efficiency or profit is worth giving up to narrow a gap between groups. Whether it is acceptable to use race or sex to correct a model that would otherwise disadvantage a group. Whether some decisions, about a person’s liberty above all, should be made by accountable humans even if a machine would be more accurate. Who should bear the risk of an untested system, the companies and governments that use it or the people it judges, and whether those rules should be set by each state or once for the nation.
