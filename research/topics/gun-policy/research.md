# Gun Policy — research

## Overview

This topic is about how societies regulate civilian firearms: who may own a gun, how they may acquire it, where they may carry it, and which kinds of weapons and accessories they may have at all. It sets the claim that owning and carrying arms is an individual right, protected in the United States by the Second Amendment, against the claim that tighter limits on access, carry and weapon types would save lives. It covers background checks and licensing, waiting periods and minimum ages, red flag (extreme risk) laws, safe-storage laws, concealed and permitless carry, bans on assault-style weapons and large-capacity magazines, untraceable “ghost guns,” and the court cases that now decide much of this, along with the evidence on gun deaths, suicide, mass shootings and defensive gun use. The debate is overwhelmingly American, because the United States has far more civilian guns and far higher gun death rates than any other rich country, but it draws on the experience of countries such as Australia, Canada and the United Kingdom. It deliberately excludes policing and police use of force (covered in the policing reform topic), sentencing and incarceration (covered in the crime and punishment topic), general theories of constitutional interpretation except as they shape gun cases (covered in the constitutional interpretation topic), the military arms trade, and hunting and wildlife regulation.

Why now: on 30 June 2026 the US Supreme Court agreed, for the first time, to decide whether the Second Amendment protects AR-15-style rifles, taking up challenges to Cook County, Illinois’s ban (Viramontes v. Cook County) and Connecticut’s ban (Grant v. Higgins), with argument set for 2 December 2026 (SCOTUSblog, https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/09/court-announces-cases-it-will-hear-in-december-including-challenges-to-the-constitutionality-of-/; grant date from the Second Amendment Foundation, https://saf.org/supreme-court-grants-cert-in-saf-assault-weapons-cases/). The question the Court agreed to answer is “Whether the Second and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee the right to possess AR-15 platform and similar semiautomatic rifles.” The grant came at the end of a term in which the Court ruled 6–3 in Wolford v. Lopez (25 June 2026) that Hawaii could not bar licensed carriers from private property open to the public unless the owner expressly allowed guns (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1046_nmio.pdf), and held in United States v. Hemani (18 June 2026) that the federal ban on gun possession by drug users could not be applied to someone solely because he habitually used marijuana (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1234_g2bh.pdf). Policy is moving in both directions at once. Virginia’s governor signed a ban on the sale of assault firearms and magazines holding more than 15 rounds on 14 May 2026, effective 1 July, bringing to 12, with the District of Columbia, the states with broad assault weapons bans (WTVR, https://www.wtvr.com/news/virginia-politics/assault-weapons-ban-may-14-2026; Giffords count, https://giffords.org/lawcenter/gun-laws/policy-areas/hardware-ammunition/assault-weapons/); the federal Justice Department sued Virginia over the law on 1 July (https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-commonwealth-virginia-unconstitutional-weapons-bans), and on 18 September 2026 its Office of Legal Counsel concluded that the federal ban on licensed dealers selling handguns to 18- to 20-year-olds is unconstitutional and will not be criminally enforced (https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-concludes-federal-ban-handgun-sales-18-20-year-olds-unconstitutional-and). Abroad, after two gunmen killed 15 people at a Hanukkah celebration on Bondi Beach in Sydney on 14 December 2025, Australia’s governments agreed to tighten gun laws again and announced a national buyback (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Australia_gun_buyback_program).

The debate is live because the facts cut in more than one direction and the law is being rewritten by courts. US gun deaths fell for a third straight year in 2024, to 44,447, as gun homicides dropped sharply from their 2021 peak; but gun suicides, which are six in ten gun deaths, reached a record (Pew Research Center, https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/28/what-the-data-says-about-gun-deaths-in-the-us/). Since District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) recognized an individual right to keep a handgun at home, and New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen (2022) required gun laws to fit “the Nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation,” courts rather than legislatures increasingly decide which laws survive. On one side are gun-safety organizations such as Everytown for Gun Safety, Giffords and Brady, medical bodies such as the American Academy of Pediatrics, and Democratic-led states, who want universal background checks, licensing, assault weapons bans and red flag laws. On the other are the National Rifle Association, Gun Owners of America, the Second Amendment Foundation, Republican-led states that have made permitless carry the majority rule, and a federal Justice Department that created a Second Amendment section in its Civil Rights Division and now sues states over their gun laws. Between them are lawmakers who passed the 2022 Bipartisan Safer Communities Act by targeting dangerous people rather than types of guns, and violence-reduction practitioners who argue that shootings are concentrated among a few people and places that neither gun-rights nor gun-control politics addresses directly. Public opinion is steady and split: a majority has favored stricter gun laws in most years for three decades, while about half of Americans say gun ownership makes the country safer and half say it makes it less safe.

Which part of this total a policy targets shapes almost every argument about it: carry laws and assault weapons bans are debated mainly as homicide and mass-shooting policy, while waiting periods, safe storage and red flag laws are argued largely as suicide prevention.

Context: This study counted suicide deaths and suicide attempts treated in emergency departments or hospitals across the United States. Firearms and hanging accounted for 8.8 percent of suicidal acts but 75.3 percent of suicide deaths; drug poisoning, the most common method, accounted for 59.4 percent of acts but 13.5 percent of deaths. Because attempts not treated in a hospital are missed, the true overall fatality rate is lower than 8.5 percent. The figure is the core of the public-health case on suicide: many suicidal crises are brief, most people who survive an attempt do not later die by suicide, and a gun makes an attempt far more likely to be fatal, so limiting access during a crisis, through waiting periods, safe storage, voluntary temporary storage or red flag orders, is expected to save lives. Gun-rights advocates generally accept the lethality point and favor voluntary measures over legal mandates.

Low estimates come from the National Crime Victimization Survey, which asks only people who first report being victims of a crime; high estimates come from surveys that ask directly about defensive use and count brandishing and verbal threats, and are vulnerable to over-reporting of rare events. RAND notes that research on how gun laws affect defensive gun use is almost entirely missing.

## Questions

Question: Does having more guns in a place cause more people there to die, or do gun numbers mostly reflect other forces?
Type: empirical
Poles: Guns raise deaths / Guns not the driver
Why contested: No one can randomly assign guns to communities, so the evidence is observational: comparisons across states and countries, case-control studies of households, and studies of changes over time. The strong association between gun ownership and gun deaths is not disputed; what it means is. Gun suicides and gun homicides behave differently, and the places with the most guns differ from those with the fewest in rurality, poverty, age and culture.
Case for “Guns raise deaths”: Across US states, higher household gun ownership goes with higher total suicide rates, not only higher gun suicide rates, and case-control studies find that a gun in the home roughly triples the risk of suicide in that household. Because about 90 percent of suicide attempts with a gun are fatal, against 8.5 percent of attempts overall, and because many suicidal crises are brief, the method available at the moment of crisis decides who lives. For homicide, the United States has broadly similar rates of assault and robbery to other rich countries but far higher rates of death, because more of its conflicts involve guns; its firearm homicide rate is about six times Canada’s and dozens of times that of most of Western Europe. Within the United States the states with the lowest gun death rates are those with the fewest guns and strictest laws. The mechanism is not mysterious: guns make violence and self-harm more lethal.
Case for “Guns not the driver”: The places with the most guns are not the most violent. Rural states such as Idaho, Maine and New Hampshire have high ownership and some of the lowest gun homicide rates in the country, while gun homicide is concentrated in particular urban neighborhoods and among a small number of people with long criminal histories. Countries such as Switzerland and Finland pair high ownership with low homicide, and Japan and South Korea, with almost no civilian guns, have high suicide rates. The US homicide rate without guns is itself higher than in most rich countries, pointing to causes beyond gun access. Ecological correlations cannot separate the effect of guns from the effect of the conditions that lead people to buy them, and US gun homicides fell by more than a quarter between 2021 and 2024 while the number of guns kept rising.

Question: Should AR-15-style rifles and large-capacity magazines be banned, and does the Constitution allow it?
Type: value
Poles: Ban them / Protect them
Why contested: The rifles and magazines are used in a small share of gun deaths but in many of the deadliest mass shootings, and they are among the most commonly sold guns in the country. The Supreme Court will decide in its 2026–27 term whether the Second Amendment protects them, which turns partly on history and partly on how the justices weigh common ownership against danger.
Case for “Ban them”: Semiautomatic rifles with detachable large-capacity magazines let one person fire dozens of rounds in seconds, and they were the weapons in many of the deadliest US mass shootings, from Sandy Hook to Las Vegas, Parkland and Uvalde. Everytown reports that shootings with assault weapons from 2015 to 2022 left nearly six times as many people shot per incident, and that all ten of the deadliest mass shootings from 2016 to 2025 involved an assault weapon and a large-capacity magazine. These guns are not needed for self-defense in the home, which a handgun serves better, and the Second Amendment has always coexisted with bans on especially dangerous weapons, from machine guns to sawed-off shotguns. Lower courts have repeatedly upheld the bans as consistent with that tradition, and twelve states and DC have them. RAND finds limited evidence that high-capacity magazine bans reduce mass shootings.
Case for “Protect them”: Tens of millions of these rifles are owned by law-abiding Americans for home defense, hunting and sport; the industry estimates more than 32 million have been sold since 1990, and they are the best-selling rifle design in the country. Under Heller, arms “in common use” for lawful purposes are protected, and it is hard to see what that phrase means if the most popular rifle is not. Rifles of all kinds are involved in about 3 percent of gun murders where the weapon is known, so bans burden millions of owners while barely touching the violence that kills most people. The label “assault weapon” describes cosmetic features, the 1994–2004 federal ban produced no clear drop in gun deaths, and RAND finds the evidence that assault weapon bans affect mass shootings inconclusive.

Question: Does letting more people carry guns in public raise or lower violent crime?
Type: empirical
Poles: Raises crime / Deters crime
Why contested: Permitless carry now covers 29 states, and Bruen ended discretionary permit systems, so the question has moved from theory to national practice. The studies disagree in part because states changed carry laws at different times amid national crime swings.
Case for “Raises crime”: RAND’s 2026 review of the evidence rates as “supportive,” its strongest category, the conclusion that shall-issue carry laws increase total homicides, firearm homicides and other violent crime, and it recommends that states with shall-issue or permitless carry consider other rules to align them with public safety. The leading studies, using synthetic comparisons of states before and after the laws, find violent crime roughly 13 to 15 percent higher ten years after adoption. More guns carried in public means more arguments, road-rage incidents and thefts that can turn fatal, more guns stolen from cars, and more difficulty for police in telling a threat from a lawful carrier. Stand-your-ground laws, often passed alongside carry expansions, are also associated with more firearm homicides.
Case for “Deters crime”: Permit holders commit crimes at very low rates, and violent criminals already carry illegally regardless of the law. The expansion of carry since the 1980s coincided with a long fall in violent crime, and the steep declines in gun homicide in 2023, 2024 and 2025 came as permitless carry spread to 29 states and Bruen opened carry in the rest. Surveys of gun owners find hundreds of thousands to more than a million defensive gun uses a year, most with no shot fired, a benefit that crime statistics do not capture. Economists including Carlisle Moody and Thomas Marvell have argued that the leading estimates are sensitive to modeling choices, and RAND itself finds research on how carry laws affect defensive gun use almost entirely missing.

Question: Should gun laws be judged by the nation’s historical tradition of regulation or by their benefits for public safety today?
Type: value
Poles: History and tradition / Weigh public safety
Why contested: Bruen replaced the balancing test most lower courts used with a test that asks whether a modern law is consistent with historical regulation. That shifted the question from what works to what the founding generation would have recognized, and judges applying the test have reached conflicting results on age limits, drug users and sensitive places.
Case for “History and tradition”: The Second Amendment is a right, and rights are not subject to case-by-case balancing by the officials they limit. Judges weighing public-safety benefits against burdens nearly always sided with the government before Bruen, which gave the right less protection than any other in the Bill of Rights. Tying the right to its historical scope gives courts an objective anchor and leaves room for longstanding laws, such as bans on possession by the dangerous; in Rahimi in 2024 the Court upheld the federal ban on gun possession by people under domestic violence restraining orders on exactly that basis. If the public wants broader restrictions, the Constitution can be amended.
Case for “Weigh public safety”: Historical analogy is an unreliable guide to modern weapons, cities and threats, and it hands judges wide discretion dressed up as neutrality: courts applying Bruen have split on whether 18-to-20-year-olds, drug users or people in parks may be disarmed. Every other constitutional right is subject to some weighing of government interests, and democratic majorities should be able to respond to 44,000 gun deaths a year with laws that evidence shows save lives. Heller itself said the right is “not unlimited,” and the founding era regulated gunpowder storage, carrying in public and possession by groups it considered dangerous far more than the new test allows modern legislatures to.

Question: Would requiring background checks on all sales, or a license to buy a gun, reduce gun deaths enough to justify the burden on lawful buyers?
Type: empirical
Poles: Worth the burden / Burden without payoff
Why contested: Federal law requires checks only on sales by licensed dealers, so private sales, including many online and at gun shows, go unchecked in about half the states. Most criminals obtain guns through theft, straw purchases or informal networks, and whether closing the private-sale gap reaches them depends on enforcement and record-keeping.
Case for “Worth the burden”: RAND finds moderate evidence that dealer background checks reduce firearm homicides and that checks on both dealer and private sales reduce total homicides, and for the first time finds limited evidence that permit-to-purchase licensing reduces homicides. Studies of Connecticut, which adopted licensing in 1995, and Missouri, which repealed it in 2007, found fewer gun homicides and suicides after adoption and more after repeal. Checks take minutes for almost everyone and stop prohibited buyers at the counter; without universal checks, anyone denied at a store can buy privately. Surveys show about 80 percent of Americans, including most gun owners, support checks on private sales.
Case for “Burden without payoff”: Studies of California and other states that expanded checks to private sales found no clear change in gun deaths, and RAND rates the evidence on private-sale checks alone as limited. Criminals rarely buy from strangers who would run a check; they steal, use straw buyers or buy on the street, and universal checks cannot be enforced without knowing who owns which gun, which leads toward a registry that gun owners see as the first step to confiscation. Licensing adds fees, fingerprints, classes and weeks of delay that fall hardest on poor and minority residents of high-crime neighborhoods who most need to defend themselves. Resources are better spent prosecuting the straw buyers and traffickers who already break existing law.

Question: Should courts be able to take guns temporarily from people judged dangerous before they commit a crime?
Type: value
Poles: Prevent first / Due process first
Why contested: Red flag laws, in 21 states and DC, let family members or police ask a judge to remove someone’s guns for a limited period, often at a first hearing the person does not attend. They sit on the line between prevention and punishment without conviction, and the evidence on their effect is thin because the orders are relatively rare.
Case for “Prevent first”: Many mass shooters and many people who die by suicide give clear warnings that families and police see but cannot act on under criminal law. A temporary order with a prompt full hearing is a narrow, individualized tool that leaves everyone else’s rights untouched, and it has drawn support from conservatives such as David French and from Republican-led states including Florida and Indiana. RAND finds limited evidence that the laws reduce suicides, and studies from Connecticut and Indiana estimate roughly one suicide prevented for every 10 to 20 orders issued. The Supreme Court in Rahimi upheld disarming people found by a court to pose a credible threat.
Case for “Due process first”: The orders strip a constitutional right on a civil standard of proof, often after a hearing the gun owner does not know about, on the word of an estranged relative or on a police officer’s judgment about speech. They remove guns but leave a person judged dangerous free and untreated, and they can be used as leverage in family disputes. Evidence of benefits is limited, the one to 10-to-20 estimates come from a few studies, and the laws’ critics argue that the same people could be dealt with through existing involuntary commitment and criminal threat laws, which carry stronger protections.

Question: How often do guns protect people compared with how often they are used to harm them?
Type: empirical
Poles: Protection matters more / Harm outweighs protection
Why contested: The harms of guns are counted on death certificates; the benefits mostly are not, because most defensive uses involve no shot fired and leave no record. Estimates of defensive gun use range from a little more than 100,000 to 4.7 million a year depending on how the question is asked.
Case for “Protection matters more”: Surveys that ask gun owners directly find large numbers of defensive uses: the 2021 National Firearms Survey estimated about 1.67 million a year, with no shot fired in about 82 percent of them, and earlier private surveys produced estimates from the hundreds of thousands to several million. Criminals surveyed in prison report fearing armed victims more than police. Seventy-two percent of gun owners give protection as a major reason for owning a gun, and for people in high-crime areas or far from police help, the ability to defend oneself is not an abstraction. A policy calculus that counts only deaths from guns and none of the crimes they prevent is lopsided.
Case for “Harm outweighs protection”: The federal National Crime Victimization Survey, which asks only people who report a crime, finds far fewer defensive uses, and survey methodologists argue that direct questions about rare events produce large overestimates, because even a small share of false positives inflates the national figure. Studies consistently find that a gun in the home is associated with higher risks of suicide, homicide of a household member and accidental shootings, and very few household gun deaths are justifiable homicides. An analysis of victimization data by David Hemenway and Sara Solnick found that using a gun in self-defense was no more effective at preventing injury than other protective actions, while the 27,000-plus gun suicides a year are concrete.

Question: Should gun rules be set nationally or left to each state?
Type: value
Poles: National standard / State choice
Why contested: Gun laws differ sharply between neighboring states, guns travel across state lines, and the Supreme Court is now setting national limits on what states may do. Both sides favor national rules where they win and state control where they lose.
Case for “National standard”: Guns move freely across state lines: in strict states such as New York and New Jersey, most crime guns traced by the ATF were first sold in other states with weaker laws, so one state’s protections are only as strong as its neighbors’. A national floor, such as universal background checks, would close the interstate pipeline, and a clear national rule on carry and permits would spare lawful gun owners from becoming criminals by crossing a state line. Gun-rights advocates make the mirror argument for national concealed-carry reciprocity, and the Supreme Court’s rulings already set a national floor for the right.
Case for “State choice”: The country is too varied for one rule: what suits rural Montana, where the police may be an hour away and most gun deaths are suicides, does not suit dense cities with concentrated gang violence. States are laboratories, and the variation lets researchers learn which laws work. National standards risk either imposing big-city restrictions on rural owners or overriding the democratic choices of states that want stricter rules, which is why both gun-safety advocates and gun-rights advocates object when the federal government, through Congress, the Justice Department or the courts, overrides their states.

## Positions

Camp: Tighten gun laws to reduce gun deaths
Adherents: Everytown for Gun Safety, the largest US gun-safety organization, whose litigation arm responded to the Supreme Court’s grant in the AR-15 cases on 30 June 2026 that “Assault weapons cause massive devastation. They are the weapons of choice for mass shooters” (https://everytownlaw.org/press/united-states-supreme-court-grants-review-in-assault-weapon-cases-everytown-law-responds/); the American Academy of Pediatrics, which states that it “supports firearm regulation, including a ban on assault weapons and high capacity magazine sales, as an effective strategy to reduce firearm-related injuries” (https://www.aap.org/en/advocacy/state-advocacy/assault-weapons-bans/); Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger, who on signing her state’s ban on 14 May 2026 said “firearms designed to inflict maximum casualties do not belong on our streets” (https://www.wtvr.com/news/virginia-politics/assault-weapons-ban-may-14-2026). Giffords, Brady and most Democratic officeholders hold the same view.
Case: More than 44,000 Americans die from gunshots every year, more than in car crashes, and firearms are now the leading cause of death for American children and teens. No other rich country lives like this, and the difference is not that Americans are more troubled or more violent in general; it is that Americans have easy access to far more guns, and guns turn suicidal crises, arguments and grievances into deaths. Other democracies decided that the freedom to live without that fear outweighs unrestricted access, and they have a small fraction of our gun deaths. The Second Amendment, as the Supreme Court said in Heller, is “not unlimited,” and the policies this camp wants leave law-abiding adults able to own guns for self-defense and sport: background checks on every sale, licensing so buyers show they can handle a gun safely, waiting periods, red flag laws, safe-storage requirements, a minimum age of 21, and an end to the sale of military-style rifles and large-capacity magazines built to kill many people fast. These measures are popular, including with many gun owners, and states that have adopted them have the lowest gun death rates in the country.
Evidence: US firearm homicide rates are about 6 times Canada’s and dozens of times Western Europe’s (UNODC via Our World in Data, https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/homicide-rates-from-firearms); the five states with the lowest gun death rates in 2024, Hawaii, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York and Rhode Island, all have strong laws, and the highest have weak ones (Pew, https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/28/what-the-data-says-about-gun-deaths-in-the-us/); RAND’s 2026 review finds supportive evidence that child-access prevention laws reduce youth suicides and injuries and that minimum-age laws reduce youth firearm suicides, moderate evidence that background checks and waiting periods reduce homicides and suicides, and supportive evidence that loosening carry laws and stand-your-ground laws increase homicides (https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4360-2.html); about 90 percent of suicide attempts with a gun are fatal (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31791066/); Australia had 13 fatal mass shootings in the 18 years before its 1996 reforms and none in the two decades after (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27332876/); Everytown reports that shootings with assault weapons from 2015 to 2022 left nearly six times as many people shot per incident as other shootings.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that these laws burden millions of law-abiding owners to stop a few criminals, the camp answers that most gun deaths are not committed by career criminals but by ordinary people in a moment of crisis, most of them suicides, and that the burdens it proposes, a background check, a license, a few days’ wait, are the same kinds of conditions society puts on driving a car. To the objection that the Constitution forbids it, it answers that Heller and Bruen themselves accept many regulations, that bans on unusually dangerous weapons are part of the historical tradition the Court asks about, and that the courts have upheld assault weapons bans in case after case. To the point that gun homicide is falling, it answers that the decline has been strongest in states and cities that invested in both gun laws and violence intervention, and that gun suicides are still at a record high.
Variation: Adherents differ on how far to go. Everytown and Giffords concentrate on background checks, licensing, red flag laws and assault weapons bans; public-health groups such as the AAP add that the absence of guns in the home is the most reliable protection for children. A smaller strand, including former Justice John Paul Stevens in a 2018 New York Times essay, has called for repealing the Second Amendment itself, and some advocates favor a mandatory buyback of assault weapons on the Australian model; most in the camp do not.

Camp: Protect and expand the right to keep and carry arms
Adherents: The National Rifle Association, which said after the AR-15 grant on 1 July 2026 that “The Supreme Court now has an opportunity to reaffirm that bans on commonly possessed firearms violate the Second Amendment and permanently strike down ‘assault weapon’ bans once and for all” (https://www.nraila.org/articles/20260701/scotus-agrees-to-hear-challenges-to-assault-weapon-bans), and which called Virginia’s ban “a blatant violation of Second Amendment rights” (https://www.wtvr.com/news/virginia-politics/assault-weapons-ban-may-14-2026); the US Department of Justice under the Trump administration, whose Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights, Harmeet Dhillon, said in suing Virginia on 1 July 2026 that “Law-abiding Americans should not have to live under threat of criminal sanction for simply exercising their Second Amendment right” (https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-commonwealth-virginia-unconstitutional-weapons-bans); the Second Amendment Foundation, which brought both cases the Supreme Court will hear in December (https://saf.org/supreme-court-grants-cert-in-saf-assault-weapons-cases/). Gun Owners of America, the Firearms Policy Coalition and most Republican officeholders hold the same view.
Case: The right to keep and bear arms is a fundamental individual right, written into the Bill of Rights because free people must be able to defend themselves, their families and, in the last resort, their liberty. It belongs to every law-abiding American, not only to those who can persuade an official they deserve it. Gun control asks tens of millions of peaceful owners to give up rights because of crimes committed by a few, and it does not work: criminals do not submit to background checks, register their guns or obey magazine limits, and the cities with the strictest laws have often had the worst violence. The guns targeted by “assault weapon” bans are the most popular rifles in America and are used in a tiny share of murders. An armed citizen is often the only protection available when the police are minutes away, and Americans use guns to stop crimes hundreds of thousands to millions of times a year, usually without firing a shot. The right answer to violence is to enforce the laws against violent criminals, restore rights that states have made hard to exercise, and let the Supreme Court’s Heller and Bruen decisions be applied as written.
Evidence: Rifles of all kinds were used in 3 percent of 2024 gun murders where the weapon was reported, and handguns in 53 percent (FBI data via Pew, https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/28/what-the-data-says-about-gun-deaths-in-the-us/); the NSSF estimates more than 32 million AR-style rifles have entered circulation since 1990 (https://shootingindustry.com/industry-news/nssf-report-reveals-record-32-million-modern-sporting-rifles-in-circulation/); the 2021 National Firearms Survey estimated about 1.67 million defensive gun uses a year, with no shot fired in most (William English, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3887145); gun homicides fell 27 percent from 2021 to 2024, and further in 2025, as permitless carry spread to 29 states and Bruen opened public carry everywhere; RAND finds the evidence that assault weapon bans affect mass shootings inconclusive and research on how gun laws affect defensive gun use, hunting and the gun industry almost entirely missing (https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4360-2.html); the 1996 Australian reforms were followed by a larger fall in non-gun suicide and homicide than in gun deaths, so the JAMA study of them could not attribute the change to the laws (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27332876/).
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that the United States has far more gun deaths than peer countries, the camp answers that the gap is driven by suicide, which is rooted in despair and isolation rather than law, and by homicide concentrated among a small number of repeat offenders in particular neighborhoods, and that the country’s non-gun homicide rate is also higher than Europe’s, so the difference is not simply guns. It supports voluntary safe storage and suicide prevention efforts led by gun owners, and many in the camp supported the Fix NICS Act of 2018 to improve records for existing prohibitions. To the charge that permissive carry raises crime, it answers that the studies RAND relies on are sensitive to modeling choices and that crime fell while carry expanded. To the charge that it ignores mass shootings, it answers that most mass shooters bought their guns legally after passing a background check or took them from family members, and that armed security and faster police response save more lives than bans.
Variation: Adherents differ in how far they would go. The NRA has at times supported narrow measures, such as the 2018 Fix NICS Act to improve background-check records and additional regulation of bump stocks after the 2017 Las Vegas shooting, while Gun Owners of America and the Firearms Policy Coalition oppose nearly all federal gun regulation, including the National Firearms Act’s registration of suppressors and short-barreled rifles. Within the federal government the camp has moved from defense to offense: the Justice Department created a Second Amendment Section in its Civil Rights Division, has sued Virginia, California, Colorado and others over their laws, and concluded on 18 September 2026 that the federal ban on handgun sales to 18- to 20-year-olds is unconstitutional.

Camp: Keep guns from dangerous people while leaving lawful owners alone
Adherents: Senator John Cornyn, Republican of Texas and lead Republican author of the 2022 Bipartisan Safer Communities Act, who wrote on its fourth anniversary that he was proud to have “reject[ed] the calls for extreme measures that would have encroached on the Second Amendment rights of law-abiding Texans” while creating “narrow, targeted provisions … to keep dangerous weapons out of the hands of criminals and the mentally ill” (https://www.cornyn.senate.gov/news/cornyn-op-ed-four-years-later-the-bipartisan-safer-communities-act-is-making-a-difference/); the National Sheriffs’ Association and the Major Cities Chiefs Association, which endorsed that law, the latter saying it would “keep communities safe while upholding the 2nd Amendment and protecting due process” (as listed on https://www.cornyn.senate.gov/news/cornyns-bipartisan-safer-communities-act-endorsed-by-key-groups-editorial-boards/); the writer David French, who argued in National Review that most gun-control proposals “represent a collective punishment” of the law-abiding but that gun-violence restraining orders “make us all safer while empowering the individual and protecting liberty” (https://www.nationalreview.com/2018/02/gun-control-republicans-consider-gvro/).
Case: The right to own a gun is real, and most gun owners will never harm anyone, so laws that restrict what weapons ordinary citizens may own punish millions for the acts of a few. But the Second Amendment has always coexisted with disarming people who show by their conduct that they are dangerous: felons, domestic abusers, people found to be a threat to themselves or others. The practical path is to make those prohibitions work: get juvenile and mental-health records into the background check system, prosecute straw purchasers and traffickers, give families and police a way to ask a judge to remove guns temporarily from someone in crisis with full due process, fund mental health care and school safety, and support safe storage. These measures target the people who cause harm rather than the objects they use, can pass with votes from both parties, as the 2022 law did, and are likelier to survive in the courts.
Evidence: The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act, passed after Uvalde, was the first major federal gun law in nearly three decades; Cornyn reports that nearly 500 people have been convicted under its straw-purchase and trafficking provisions and 716 more face charges (Cornyn op-ed, above); RAND finds moderate evidence that prohibiting gun possession by people under domestic violence restraining orders reduces intimate partner homicides, moderate evidence that background checks reduce homicides, and limited evidence that extreme risk protection order laws reduce suicides (https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4360-2.html); the Supreme Court upheld the domestic violence prohibition 8–1 in United States v. Rahimi in 2024, on the ground that disarming people found to pose a credible threat fits the historical tradition; 88 percent of Americans, including 88 percent of Republicans, favor preventing people with mental illness from buying guns (Pew, https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/06/28/americans-views-of-specific-gun-policy-proposals/).
Answer to the strongest objection: To gun-safety advocates who say this leaves the weapons of mass shootings on the shelves and does nothing about the number of guns, the camp answers that bans on common rifles are unlikely to survive the Supreme Court, would take decades to reduce the existing stock, and cost the political coalition that made any progress possible, while the measures it favors reach both suicides and mass shooters, who so often show warning signs. To gun-rights advocates who say red flag laws and expanded record checks strip rights without a criminal conviction, it answers that it insists on prompt hearings, time limits and penalties for false petitions, and that disarming the demonstrably dangerous is part of the tradition Bruen and Rahimi recognize.
Variation: Some in this camp accept universal background checks on the grounds that they target prohibited buyers, not lawful ones, while others, including many Republican supporters of the 2022 law, oppose them as a step toward registration. A related strand led by gun owners themselves, including partnerships between the firearms industry’s National Shooting Sports Foundation and the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention and groups such as Walk the Talk America, pursues suicide prevention through voluntary safe storage and temporary storage at gun shops rather than through new laws.

Camp: Concentrate on the few people and places that drive shootings
Adherents: The National Network for Safe Communities at John Jay College, led by criminologist David Kennedy, whose Group Violence Intervention rests on the finding that group members “typically make up around half a percent of a city’s population but are involved in as much as 70 percent of its homicide and gun violence” (https://nnscommunities.org/strategies/group-violence-intervention/); the Manhattan Institute’s Rafael Mangual, who told the Senate Judiciary Committee in 2023 that “our most pressing problem isn’t the possibility that more law-abiding citizens will now be able to carry firearms for self-defense … it’s that … policy choices have exacerbated the risks associated with failing to arrest, prosecute, and meaningfully incapacitate high-rate, high-risk criminal offenders” (https://manhattan.institute/article/testimony-before-the-u-s-senate-committee-on-the-judiciary). Community violence intervention programs such as Advance Peace and Cure Violence work from the same premise with an outreach rather than enforcement emphasis.
Case: The national gun debate argues about 400 million guns and 80 million owners, but gun homicide is intensely concentrated: in most cities a few hundred people, often in a handful of street groups on a few blocks, account for most of the shootings, and they are well known to police, outreach workers and each other. Laws aimed at every gun buyer barely reach them, and fights over rifles and magazines consume political energy that could go to what demonstrably works: telling the people at highest risk directly that the violence must stop, offering real help to those who want out, following through with focused enforcement on those who keep shooting, solving more shootings so that retaliation is not the only justice available, and fixing the specific places where violence clusters. Whether one leans toward outreach or incarceration, the lesson is the same: target the violence, not the national gun supply.
Evidence: A Campbell Collaboration systematic review of 24 evaluations found that focused deterrence strategies are associated with a statistically significant, moderate reduction in crime, though effects were smaller in more rigorous studies (Braga, Weisburd & Turchan, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8356499/); NNSC reports Boston’s original Operation Ceasefire was followed by a 63 percent drop in youth homicide and lists a 42 percent reduction in gun homicide in Stockton and a 41 percent reduction in group member-involved homicide in Cincinnati (https://nnscommunities.org/strategies/group-violence-intervention/); US gun homicides fell by nearly 40 percent between 2021 and 2025, from 20,958 to about 12,850 on provisional data, without major new federal gun restrictions, with cities such as Baltimore crediting coordinated violence-intervention and investigation strategies (The Trace, https://www.thetrace.org/2026/08/gun-deaths-homicide-suicide-cdc-data/); the lowest gun homicide rates in the country are in high-ownership rural states such as New Hampshire, Maine and Idaho (Pew, https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/28/what-the-data-says-about-gun-deaths-in-the-us/).
Answer to the strongest objection: To gun-safety advocates who say focusing on people ignores the easy supply of guns that makes street conflicts deadly, the camp answers that it does not oppose supply-side measures such as prosecuting traffickers, but that the evidence for interventions aimed at people is stronger and faster than for national gun laws, and that the homicide decline since 2021 came without them. To gun-rights advocates who say its outreach programs are soft on crime, the enforcement wing answers that focused deterrence is built on swift consequences for those who keep shooting; to civil-liberties critics who say the enforcement wing means more incarceration, the outreach wing answers that the whole point is to act on very few people instead of whole neighborhoods. It concedes that its approach has little to say about gun suicide, the majority of gun deaths.
Variation: The camp spans the political spectrum and divides sharply on means. The Manhattan Institute and the Trump administration emphasize arrest, prosecution and incarceration of repeat violent offenders; Kennedy’s network emphasizes deterrence with minimal arrest; groups such as Advance Peace and hospital-based programs emphasize mentoring, cash stipends and outreach. In April 2025 the Justice Department terminated 373 Office of Justice Programs grants, including violence intervention grants worth about $145 million, roughly half of the $300 million invested in such programs since 2022 (Council on Criminal Justice, https://counciloncj.org/doj-funding-update-a-deeper-look-at-the-cuts/), a decision the outreach wing opposed and the enforcement wing largely did not.

## Crux

Crux: How many deaths would broad limits on civilian access to guns and to particular kinds of guns actually prevent, beyond what measures aimed at dangerous individuals and violent places would achieve, and how much of a constitutional liberty that tens of millions of Americans value for self-defense should the country give up to prevent them?

Evidence could settle: The size of the effects. That means better estimates of how much specific laws, such as licensing, universal background checks, waiting periods, carry rules and bans on rifles and magazines, change gun suicides, gun homicides and mass-shooting deaths once other trends are accounted for; whether people denied a gun in a crisis die by other means as often; how often guns are used to prevent crimes, measured with methods both sides accept; and how much of the recent fall in gun homicide came from laws, from focused violence-reduction work, from policing or from forces outside policy altogether. RAND’s 2026 review found that of more than 200 combinations of policies and outcomes, many have received little rigorous study, and that research on outcomes gun owners care most about, such as defensive gun use, is almost entirely missing. Better national data on defensive gun use, crime-gun sources and nonfatal shootings, and more years of variation as states move apart, could narrow the range considerably.

Value choice: How to weigh an individual right against statistical lives, and who carries the burden of proof. One side holds that the ability to own and carry the arms of one’s choosing for self-defense is a fundamental liberty, like speech, which the government may restrict only for those who have shown themselves dangerous, and that the burden of restrictions falls on the many who have done nothing wrong. The other holds that a free society may place reasonable conditions on a lethal product, as it does on cars and medicines, and that the lives of the tens of thousands killed each year, and the freedom of everyone else to live without fear of being shot, weigh at least as heavily as the convenience or preferences of owners. A related choice is who decides: legislatures weighing costs and benefits, or courts applying a historical test that sets the question of effectiveness aside. No study can settle how much risk a free people should accept in exchange for liberty, or whether the right to arms belongs among the rights that are not balanced at all.
