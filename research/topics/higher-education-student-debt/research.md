# Higher Education & Student Debt — research

## Overview

This topic covers three linked questions about education after high school, centered on the United States, where the debt is largest and the fight sharpest, with comparisons to England and other rich countries that answer the questions differently. First, whether a college degree is still worth what it costs: for whom, in which fields, and for the student who starts but may not finish. Second, who should pay for it: students and their families, through savings and loans; taxpayers, through grants, state funding of public colleges and loan forgiveness; or colleges themselves, through rules that cut off aid to programs whose graduates earn too little or make schools cover part of what their students cannot repay. Third, what to do about the roughly $1.7 trillion that about 42 million Americans owe in federal student loans: cancel it broadly, forgive it in targeted ways, or collect it. Paths that compete with a degree, such as apprenticeships and short-term job training, are in scope where they bear on those questions. The page deliberately leaves to their own pages who gets admitted to selective colleges and on what basis (meritocracy and its discontents), schooling before college (school choice and public education), the overall gap between rich and poor (economic inequality), and disputes over campus speech, research funding and the federal government’s dealings with particular universities.

Why now: on 1 July 2026 the largest overhaul of federal student lending in more than a decade took effect. The law Republicans passed on 4 July 2025 (Public Law 119-21, which the Education Department now calls the Working Families Tax Cuts Act and most others call the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) closed the Grad PLUS program to new borrowers, capped new graduate borrowing at $20,500 a year and $100,000 in total ($50,000 and $200,000 for professional degrees such as medicine and law), capped Parent PLUS loans, and replaced the income-driven repayment plans for new loans with a single Repayment Assistance Plan that charges 1 to 10 percent of income (Education Department final rule, Federal Register, 1 May 2026, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/01/2026-08556/reimagining-and-improving-student-education-federal-student-loan-program-final-regulations). On the same day the department published a second rule, effective July 2027, under which undergraduate programs whose graduates do not out-earn typical high school graduates, and graduate programs whose graduates do not out-earn typical bachelor’s holders, lose access to federal loans after failing two years out of three (Federal Register, 1 July 2026, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/01/2026-13286/accountability-in-higher-education-and-access-through-demand--driven-workforce-pell-student-tuition). Meanwhile the Biden administration’s SAVE repayment plan, which had been frozen in court since 2024, was ended by a court order on 10 March 2026; servicers began sending its enrollees 90-day notices to choose another plan on 1 July, and the first deadlines fell on 29 September 2026 (MOHELA, a federal loan servicer, https://mohela.studentaid.gov/DL/resourceCenter/SAVEPlanFaq.aspx). Federal Student Aid data show 6.5 million borrowers still enrolled in SAVE and 9.3 million borrowers in default, owing $234.1 billion, as of 30 June 2026, up from 5.2 million in default nine months earlier (Federal Student Aid Data Center, https://studentaid.gov/data-center/student/portfolio). The department paused wage garnishment and the seizure of tax refunds from defaulted borrowers on 16 January 2026 while the new rules took effect (CNBC, https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/16/student-loan-collections-paused.html).

The debate is live because the evidence points two ways at once. On average a degree still pays well: in 2025 the median full-time worker with a bachelor’s degree earned 63 percent more per week than one with only a high school diploma, and the OECD finds the lifetime financial return to tertiary education is higher in the United States than in any other member country. Yet public faith has fallen: Gallup found 38 percent of Americans with a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in higher education in June 2026, down from 57 percent in 2015, and Pew found only 22 percent who say a four-year degree is worth the cost if a student has to borrow. Averages also hide wide spread: about 4 in 10 starting students do not finish within six years, many programs pay back less than they cost, and recent graduates have faced a higher unemployment rate than the workforce as a whole since 2021. The participants are the Education Department and Congress, where the 2025 law passed on party lines; borrowers and advocacy groups ranging from the Debt Collective to the Institute for College Access & Success; colleges and their associations; think tanks across the spectrum, including the American Enterprise Institute, Heritage Foundation, Third Way and American Compass; economists who study college returns and tuition pricing; the courts, which struck down the Biden administration’s broad cancellation plan in 2023 and ended its SAVE plan in 2026; and the states, which fund public colleges and have gone to court on both sides of the forgiveness fight.

The gap is the most cited evidence that college pays, but it compares different people: those who finish a degree differ from those who do not in ability, family background and motivation, so part of the gap would exist without the degree. People who start college without finishing earn little more than high school graduates ($1,062 a week), which is why completion is central to whether college pays for a given student.

## Questions

Question: Does college still pay off for the students deciding whether to go?
Type: empirical
Poles: Still pays off / Often does not pay
Why contested: Everyone agrees the average graduate out-earns the average high school graduate. The dispute is about the student at the margin: the one with middling grades, uncertain plans or a field with modest pay, who is the person policy actually affects. Averages mix in students who would have done well anyway, leave out those who start and do not finish, and say nothing about the cost of the specific program chosen.
Case for “Still pays off”: The premium is large, persistent and visible at every level of the earnings table: in 2025 bachelor’s holders earned a median $1,578 a week against $966 for high school graduates, with lower unemployment, and the OECD finds the lifetime return in the United States is the highest of any member country. Studies built to isolate the effect of college on the marginal student find real gains: Seth Zimmerman (Journal of Labor Economics, 2014) compared Florida students just above and just below a state university’s admission cutoff and found that those admitted earned substantially more years later, and Sandra Black and coauthors (American Economic Review, 2023) found that students given access to more federal loans were more likely to finish and earned more afterward. Seen this way, high non-completion and underemployment argue for helping students finish and choose well, not for steering them away.
Case for “Often does not pay”: For a large minority the bet fails. About 39 percent of students who started in fall 2019 had no credential six years later, and those who leave without one earn little more than high school graduates while still owing. Preston Cooper’s program-level analysis finds 28 percent of bachelor’s programs leave the typical student worse off once the risk of not finishing is counted, and about 42 percent of recent graduates work in jobs that do not require a degree. Recent graduates’ unemployment has run above the national rate every month since 2021. If much of the premium reflects who goes to college, or what a degree signals to employers rather than what it teaches, as the economist Bryan Caplan argues, then the average return overstates what an extra student gains.

Question: Who should pay for college: the public or the student?
Type: value
Poles: Mostly the public / Mostly the student
Why contested: College produces private gains, which reach the graduate as higher pay, and public ones, such as tax revenue, civic participation and a skilled workforce. How to weigh them is a judgment about fairness and the role of government rather than a measurable quantity, and Americans split almost evenly and along party lines.
Case for “Mostly the public”: Education after high school has become what high school was a century ago, the minimum for a secure working life, and a country that made high school free should do the same for its successor. The benefits spill over: graduates pay more in taxes, rely less on public programs and raise the productivity of the people around them. Making individuals carry the cost through debt deters the low-income and first-generation students who stand to gain most, and other rich countries fund far more of it publicly: government pays 38.7 percent of tertiary education costs in the United States against an OECD average of 67.4 percent.
Case for “Mostly the student”: The largest beneficiary of a degree is the graduate, who on the OECD’s numbers gains several hundred thousand dollars over a lifetime. Asking all taxpayers, most of whom do not hold bachelor’s degrees, to pay for that private gain moves money from people who on average earn less to people who on average will earn more. When students pay at least part of the price, they and their colleges have reason to weigh cost against value; when someone else pays, prices and enrollment in low-value programs rise. Need-based grants for low-income students fit this view; universal free tuition does not.

Question: Should the government cancel existing student debt broadly?
Type: value
Poles: Cancel it broadly / Borrowers should repay
Why contested: Whether broad cancellation is fair depends on whom borrowers are compared with: people who never went to college, people who already paid their loans off, or people of the same age who were told college was the only route to the middle class. Its distributional effect also depends on how it is measured. Counted by balances forgiven, cancellation helps graduate degree holders most; counted by hardship relieved, it helps borrowers in default most.
Case for “Cancel it broadly”: The country told a generation that borrowing for college was the responsible choice, then let the debt grow through interest, servicing failures and forgiveness programs that, by the Education Department’s own count, had granted public service forgiveness to only 7,000 borrowers before 2021. Debt delays home buying, family formation and saving, and it falls hardest on Black borrowers, who in Judith Scott-Clayton and Jing Li’s Brookings analysis (2016) owed $7,400 more than white graduates at graduation and about $25,000 more four years later. With 9.3 million borrowers in default, broad cancellation would end the most damaging part of the system at once, and the government has absorbed larger costs to rescue banks and businesses.
Case for “Borrowers should repay”: Broad cancellation pays the most to those with the most debt, who are disproportionately people with graduate degrees and high future earnings. Sylvain Catherine and Constantine Yannelis (NBER Working Paper 28175, 2020) estimated that universal forgiveness would deliver as much to the top tenth of earners as to the bottom three-tenths combined, because low earners on income-driven plans would never have repaid most of their balances anyway; they found enrolling struggling borrowers in income-driven repayment cheaper and more progressive. Cancellation also treats unfairly those who chose cheaper schools, worked through college or repaid, does nothing about the next cohort’s debt, and signals that future loans may be forgiven too. The Supreme Court held in Biden v. Nebraska (2023) that the executive branch lacked the authority to cancel about $430 billion on its own, so broad relief now requires Congress.

Question: Does federal student aid drive up tuition?
Type: empirical
Poles: Aid inflates prices / Aid widens access
Why contested: The idea, called the Bennett hypothesis after Education Secretary William Bennett, who argued it in 1987, is hard to test because aid, enrollment, state funding and costs all changed at once. Studies find different answers for different kinds of aid and colleges, so each side can cite credible research.
Case for “Aid inflates prices”: When colleges know students can borrow whatever they charge, they have little reason to hold prices down. David Lucca, Taylor Nadauld and Karen Shen (Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report 733, published in the Review of Financial Studies, 2019) estimated that colleges passed through about 60 cents of each dollar increase in subsidized federal loan limits as higher tuition, with the largest effects at expensive private and vocational programs. Sandra Black, Lesley Turner and Jeffrey Denning (NBER Working Paper 31291, 2023) found that uncapping graduate borrowing through Grad PLUS raised prices in the programs most exposed to it without improving access or completion. Capping federal loans, on this view, is the most direct lever on price.
Case for “Aid widens access”: The pass-through evidence is strongest for graduate programs and for-profit colleges, where most of the 2025 law’s critics accept tighter rules. At public colleges, which enroll most undergraduates, rising tuition has tracked falling state support per student more closely than federal aid, and net tuition at public four-year colleges has fallen by about half since 2012-13 in real terms as grant aid grew, according to the College Board. Federal loans also expand opportunity: the 2023 study by Black, Denning, Dettling, Goodman and Turner found that higher loan limits for undergraduates raised degree completion, earnings and repayment rates. Cutting aid to discipline prices, on this view, would mostly shut out students who need it.

Question: Will the 2025 loan caps lower prices or shut students out?
Type: empirical
Poles: Prices will adjust / Access will shrink
Why contested: The caps took effect on 1 July 2026, so evidence is only beginning to arrive. The answer will depend on how much graduate and professional programs cut prices or add aid, how much private lenders fill the gap, and on what terms.
Case for “Prices will adjust”: Programs that charged more than students could borrow under the new limits will face pressure to cut prices or offer more of their own aid, as they did before Grad PLUS existed. Preston Cooper’s analysis for the American Enterprise Institute estimates that most students are unaffected: under 20 percent of master’s students, 8 percent of doctoral students and 3 percent of dependent undergraduates whose parents borrow would be constrained. Where students do need more, private lenders, who price risk, will lend more readily for programs that pay off than for programs that do not, which is the point.
Case for “Access will shrink”: Private loans require credit checks and often co-signers, which students from low-income families and students of color are least likely to have, and they lack federal protections such as income-driven repayment and public service forgiveness. Professions with long, expensive training and modest early pay, such as social work, counseling and some medical fields, will lose applicants first. Early reports from campuses describe students cut off mid-degree by confusing rules: Inside Higher Ed reported in September 2026 on a law student whose registration was put on hold after she hit the new $257,500 lifetime cap (https://www.insidehighered.com/news/government/student-aid-policy/2026/09/21/students-and-colleges-grapple-new-loan-limits).

Question: Should programs lose federal aid when their graduates earn too little?
Type: value
Poles: Cut off low earners / Earnings miss the value
Why contested: Measuring programs by graduates’ earnings is simple and comparable but counts only one kind of value. Fields that serve the public at modest pay, regional labor markets and students who choose lower-paying work all make the test harder to apply fairly.
Case for “Cut off low earners”: Taxpayers lend the money and absorb the losses, so they are entitled to ask whether a program leaves students better off. The 2025 law’s test is a low bar: undergraduate programs must show their graduates out-earn typical high school graduates in their state, and graduate programs must beat typical bachelor’s holders. A program that fails it two years out of three is, on average, making students poorer. Holding all colleges to the same rule ends a long fight over earlier rules that targeted mainly for-profit colleges.
Case for “Earnings miss the value”: Pay is not the only return on education, and some of the lowest-paying programs, such as early childhood education, social work and the arts, train people for work society needs and chooses to pay poorly. Earnings measured a few years after graduation miss later gains, vary with local labor markets and penalize colleges that serve students who start further behind. An earnings test also risks closing the programs most open to low-income and rural students, unless it is paired with support for colleges that serve them.

Question: Should public money keep favoring college over other paths to good jobs?
Type: value
Poles: Widen access to college / Fund other paths
Why contested: Federal and state support for education after high school flows mostly to colleges, while apprenticeships and employer training get much less. Whether that is right depends on whether one sees college as the main ladder of opportunity or as one route among several that policy has overbuilt.
Case for “Widen access to college”: Degrees remain the most reliable route to stable, well-paid work, and the OECD finds that in the United States a high school diploma alone brings almost no earnings advantage for young adults. Steering students, especially low-income students, toward vocational tracks has a long history of limiting their options. Apprenticeships and short-term credentials have a thinner and more mixed record, and many of the best of them run through community colleges.
Case for “Fund other paths”: Only about a third of Americans earn a bachelor’s degree by 25, by Oren Cass’s count, yet policy treats college as the default and leaves everyone else with little support. Senator Tom Cotton points out that federal spending on college is about eight times federal spending on job training. Employer-led training, apprenticeships and dropping needless degree requirements would help the 37.6 million working-age adults who left college without a credential and the many more who never went, at lower risk than borrowing for a degree that may not be finished or used.

Question: Will artificial intelligence lower the value of a college degree?
Type: empirical
Poles: Degrees gain value / Degrees lose value
Why contested: It is too early to tell. Recent graduates’ weak job market fits several explanations, and forecasts of how AI will change work disagree about whether it will replace or complement the skills college teaches.
Case for “Degrees gain value”: Past waves of technology raised the demand for educated workers who can learn new tools; Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz’s history of the college premium (The Race between Education and Technology, 2008) describes a century in which technology kept raising the reward for schooling. College graduates’ overall unemployment rate, 2.9 percent in June 2026, remains far below that of other workers. If AI automates routine tasks, the judgment, communication and adaptability that college aims to build may become more valuable, not less.
Case for “Degrees lose value”: AI is the first technology aimed squarely at the entry-level writing, analysis and coding tasks that new graduates are hired to do. Recent graduates’ unemployment has risen faster than the overall rate, from 4.9 percent in June 2025 to 5.7 percent in June 2026 in the New York Fed series, and 46 percent of Americans told Gallup in 2026 they expect AI to make degrees less important over the next five years, against 20 percent who expect the reverse. If employers can screen for skills directly, the degree’s value as a signal may also weaken.

## Positions

Camp: Expand need-based aid and keep federal loans affordable
Adherents: The Institute for College Access & Success (TICAS), a nonprofit research and advocacy group, whose president Sameer Gadkaree condemned the 2025 law for “increasing the amount, riskiness, and duration of student loan debt” and cutting about $300 billion in federal higher education support (statement of 3 July 2025, https://ticas.org/affordability-2/federal-budget-and-tax-policy/reconciliation-full-passage-statement-july-2025/). Third Way, a center-left think tank, whose report “Moving Beyond Free” by David Feldman and Christopher Marsicano proposes larger Pell Grants, a federal-state partnership rewarding states that invest in public colleges, extra support for under-resourced colleges and risk-sharing for colleges, in place of universal free tuition (28 July 2021, https://www.thirdway.org/report/moving-beyond-free-a-college-affordability-compact-for-the-next-generation). The Biden administration’s Education Department took this approach in office, approving $188.8 billion in targeted forgiveness through existing programs (16 January 2025, https://protectborrowers.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/IDR-AA-ED-Release-Email_01-16-2025.pdf).
Variation: Members differ on one-time cancellation. Many supported the Biden administration’s 2022 plan to cancel up to $10,000 per borrower ($20,000 for Pell recipients); others, including Third Way’s authors, warn that blanket subsidies send much of their money to higher-income families. They differ too on how much weight to put on accountability rules versus new spending.
Case: College remains the surest route into the middle class for students from low-income families, and the problem is that price and risk keep many of them out or leave them with debt they cannot manage. Public money should go where it changes outcomes: a bigger Pell Grant, which has stayed at $7,395 since 2023-24 while covering about a quarter of the average in-state public four-year budget; state funding, which sets public tuition; and support for the colleges that enroll the most low-income students. Loans should work like insurance, with payments tied to income, forgiveness after a reasonable period, and the discharges the law promises to public servants, disabled borrowers and students defrauded by their colleges. Programs that leave students worse off should lose federal money, but the burden of fixing the system should fall on colleges and on government, not on the students least able to bear it. Free college for everyone spends money on families who do not need it; caps on federal loans and a stingier repayment plan shift risk to those who need help most.
Best evidence: When the federal government raised undergraduate loan limits, students who could borrow more were more likely to finish and later earned more and repaid more reliably (Black, Denning, Dettling, Goodman and Turner, American Economic Review, 2023, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20210926). Repayment tracks income: in the Federal Reserve’s survey of 2025, fewer than half of borrowers with incomes under $50,000 paid the full amount due in the prior month, against 92 percent of those earning $100,000 or more (https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-credit.htm), which is the case for income-driven repayment. The economists most critical of broad forgiveness, Sylvain Catherine and Constantine Yannelis, found that enrolling struggling borrowers in income-driven repayment was the cheapest and most progressive form of relief (https://www.nber.org/papers/w28175). Targeted programs reached large numbers once they were repaired: Public Service Loan Forgiveness went from 7,000 borrowers before 2021 to more than one million by January 2025.
Answer to objections: To those who say aid inflates tuition, this camp answers that the strongest evidence for that effect concerns graduate programs and for-profit colleges, which is why it supports holding programs accountable, and that net tuition at public four-year colleges has fallen by about half in real terms since 2012-13 as grant aid rose. Capping loans punishes students for colleges’ pricing; holding colleges to outcomes punishes the colleges. To those who want free college and full cancellation, it answers that the same money spent through need-based grants and income-driven repayment does more for the students who need it, and that universal programs are both harder to pass and less progressive per dollar.

Camp: Limit federal lending and tie it to graduates’ earnings
Adherents: The American Enterprise Institute, a center-right think tank, whose senior fellow Preston Cooper argues that the 2025 law’s loan caps, single income-driven plan and earnings test will save taxpayers about $307 billion over a decade by preliminary Congressional Budget Office estimates and push colleges to justify their prices (16 July 2025, https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/an-analysis-of-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-acts-effect-on-student-loans/). Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, Republican chair of the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee, who wrote the Senate’s version of those provisions and argued that limiting graduate borrowing “puts downward pressure on rising college costs” (remarks of 21 May 2025, https://www.help.senate.gov/rep/newsroom/press/chair-cassidy-delivers-remarks-during-hearing-on-state-of-higher-education). The Trump administration’s Education Department is implementing the law.
Variation: Members differ on how far to go. The 2025 law kept federal lending but capped it. The Heritage Foundation has argued for going further, phasing out federal loans in favor of private lending (https://www.heritage.org/education/report/private-lending-the-way-reduce-students-college-costs-and-protect-americas). Some in the camp also favor making colleges repay part of the loans their students do not, an idea Republicans included in a 2024 House bill.
Case: For two decades the federal government lent students whatever colleges chose to charge, with no check on whether the program would pay off, and colleges raised prices accordingly. Students took on debt for degrees that did not raise their earnings, taxpayers absorbed the losses through ever more generous forgiveness, and an executive branch tried to cancel hundreds of billions of dollars without a vote of Congress. The fix is to restore limits and price signals: cap what the government will lend, especially for graduate school, where prices have risen fastest; replace a maze of repayment plans with one income-based plan that keeps balances from ballooning but expects borrowers to repay; and stop lending for programs whose graduates earn no more than people who never enrolled. Colleges that want students to borrow more will have to show that it pays, or cut their prices. Cancellation does none of this: it rewards the institutions that caused the problem, shifts debt from people who chose to borrow onto taxpayers who did not, and invites the next cohort to borrow expecting the same.
Best evidence: Colleges raised tuition by about 60 cents for each dollar increase in subsidized federal loan limits, in a study by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York (Lucca, Nadauld and Shen, https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr733.html), and uncapping graduate loans through Grad PLUS raised prices in exposed programs without improving access or completion (Black, Turner and Denning, https://www.nber.org/papers/w31291). By Preston Cooper’s estimates 28 percent of bachelor’s programs leave the typical student financially worse off once non-completion is counted (https://freopp.org/whitepapers/is-college-worth-it-a-comprehensive-return-on-investment-analysis/). Graduate students, about 17 percent of students, now take 48 percent of new federal loans. Universal forgiveness would deliver as much to the top tenth of earners as to the bottom three-tenths combined (Catherine and Yannelis). And 9.3 million borrowers were in default in mid-2026 under the old system.
Answer to objections: To the charge that caps will shut low-income students out of graduate and professional school, the camp answers that Grad PLUS did not widen access when it was created, according to the Black, Turner and Denning study, and that AEI estimates most students will be unaffected: under 20 percent of master’s students and 8 percent of doctoral students would be constrained, and only 3 percent of dependent undergraduates by the new Parent PLUS limits. Programs that cannot fit within the caps should lower their prices. To the charge that an earnings test ignores the value of low-paid public-service fields, it answers that the bar is set at what a typical high school graduate earns, which any program worth its cost should clear, and that the Repayment Assistance Plan still adjusts payments to income and qualifies for Public Service Loan Forgiveness.

Camp: Make public college free and cancel student debt
Adherents: The Debt Collective, a debtors’ union that campaigns to cancel all student debt and “build a pathway to tuition free college for all” (https://debtcollective.org/). Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont, who with Representative Pramila Jayapal introduced the College for All Act on 21 May 2025 to make public colleges and universities tuition-free for students from households earning up to $150,000 ($300,000 for married couples), make community college free for all, and double the maximum Pell Grant (https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-jayapal-colleagues-introduce-bill-to-make-public-colleges-and-universities-tuition-free/).
Variation: Members differ on how much debt to cancel. The Debt Collective calls for canceling all of it, as Sanders proposed in a 2019 bill; Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representative Ayanna Pressley led calls for the president to cancel up to $50,000 per borrower by executive action (1 April 2021, https://pressley.house.gov/media/press-releases/pressley-warren-healey-renew-calls-biden-cancel-50000-student-debt-through). They also differ on whether free tuition should be universal or limited by income, as the 2025 bill is.
Case: Higher education is a public good, as high school became in the last century, and the United States is unusual among rich countries in charging students so much for it. The country shifted the cost of public college onto students as states cut support, then built a lending system that traps millions in debt for decades: 9.3 million borrowers are in default, facing seized tax refunds and garnished wages. The debt falls hardest on Black borrowers and on people who never finished, and it holds back home buying, family formation and saving. Free public college would end the cycle for future students, and cancellation would release current borrowers from a system that failed them. As Sanders puts it, in a competitive global economy “we need the best educated workforce in the world,” and the GI Bill and countries such as Germany and the Nordic states show that public funding works.
Best evidence: Government pays 38.7 percent of the cost of tertiary education in the United States against an OECD average of 67.4 percent, and US public-college tuition is the highest among OECD countries (OECD, https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/09/education-at-a-glance-2025-country-notes_9749f4ff/united-states_e87f109d/784df67f-en.pdf). Black graduates owed about $25,000 more than white graduates four years after graduation (Scott-Clayton and Li, Brookings, 2016, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/black-white-disparity-in-student-loan-debt-more-than-triples-after-graduation/). Only 22 percent of Americans say a four-year degree is worth it if a student has to borrow (Pew, 2024). A majority of Americans, 54 percent, say government should bear more of the responsibility for funding higher education because it benefits society (New America, 2025).
Answer to objections: To the charge that cancellation is regressive, the camp answers that measures based on lifetime earnings count dollars, not hardship: the borrowers in default and the ones whose wages are garnished are disproportionately low-income and Black, and relief would reach them immediately. Income limits like those in the College for All Act keep free tuition from subsidizing the rich. To the charge that it is unfair to those who did not attend or who already repaid, it answers that public programs routinely help some people and not others, that a better-educated workforce benefits everyone, and that refusing relief now because earlier borrowers suffered only extends the harm. To the charge that free college would raise costs, it answers that public funding comes with public control over prices, as in other countries.

Camp: Fund paths to good jobs outside college
Adherents: American Compass, a conservative think tank, whose founder Oren Cass proposed a Workforce Training Grant of $10,000 a year to employers for each worker in certified on-the-job training, paid for by gradually shifting money from the more than $200 billion a year in federal and state higher education subsidies, and called the “overwhelming emphasis on college as the path to productive employment” a mistake (7 April 2022, https://americancompass.org/the-workforce-training-grant/). Senator Tom Cotton of Arkansas, who with then-Senator J.D. Vance introduced the American Workforce Act to give $9,000 training vouchers to young people without bachelor’s degrees who take employer-run training, funded by a tax on the largest college endowments, saying the government has “spent billions of dollars propping up bloated colleges” (8 May 2024, https://www.cotton.senate.gov/news/press-releases/cotton-colleagues-introduce-bill-to-overhaul-workforce-education); he reintroduced it as S. 2987 on 8 October 2025 (https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119s2987is).
Variation: Members differ on how much to cut college funding. Cass and Cotton would redirect a modest share toward training. The economist Bryan Caplan of George Mason University, author of The Case Against Education, argues that most of the college premium is signaling and calls for sharp cuts in education subsidies, suggesting that “ideally about 5 to 10% of Americans should get a bachelor’s degree” (interview with AEI, 16 March 2018, https://www.aei.org/education/higher-education/the-case-against-education-a-long-read-qa-with-bryan-caplan/). Allied efforts such as Opportunity@Work’s campaign to drop degree requirements in hiring focus on employers rather than on public funding.
Case: The debate over who should pay for college assumes that college should be the default for everyone, and that assumption is the mistake. Only about a third of Americans earn a bachelor’s degree by 25; most of the rest get little public help preparing for work, while the federal government spends about eight times as much on college as on job training. College-for-all has produced credential inflation, debt for students who never finish, and graduates working in jobs that do not need their degrees. A better system would fund employer-led training and apprenticeships that pay people while they learn, drop degree requirements for jobs that do not need them, and leave college for those who want and benefit from it. That would shrink the debt problem at its source instead of arguing over who pays for it afterward.
Best evidence: About 42 percent of recent graduates work in jobs that do not require a degree (New York Fed, https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market). Of students who started college in fall 2019, 38.8 percent had no credential six years later, and 37.6 million working-age Americans have left college without one (National Student Clearinghouse). The OECD finds that in the United States a high school diploma brings young adults almost no earnings advantage, a sign of how few alternatives to college exist. Half of Americans (49 percent) told Pew in 2023 that a four-year degree is less important for getting a well-paying job than it was 20 years ago.
Answer to objections: To the charge that the college premium is large and rising, the camp answers that the average premium describes graduates, not the marginal student who is less likely to finish, and that much of it reflects the sorting and signaling a degree provides, which is why credential requirements spread to jobs that did not use to need them. To the charge that steering people from college limits opportunity for low-income students, it answers that nobody would be barred from college; the aim is to give the majority who do not finish a degree a supported path of equal standing, as apprenticeship systems do in Germany and Switzerland. To the charge that training programs have a mixed record, it answers that employer-led training, where the employer has hired the trainee, avoids the problem of programs that train for jobs that do not exist.

## Crux

When college does not pay off, who should carry the loss: the student who enrolled, the college that charged for it, or the public that lent or granted the money? Every camp accepts that a degree pays well for many students and leaves others worse off. They divide over how often the second outcome happens, whether public money makes it more or less likely, and who should bear it when it does.

What evidence could settle: How large the gain from college is for the students actually deciding whether to enroll, by field and by chance of finishing, rather than for graduates on average. How much of that gain comes from skills a degree builds and how much from what it signals to employers. Whether more federal lending raises tuition, raises completion, or both, and in which sectors; the first year of the 2025 loan caps, which took effect on 1 July 2026, will add direct evidence on whether graduate programs cut prices or students lose access. Whether employer-led training and apprenticeships, at scale in the United States, deliver earnings gains comparable to a degree for students who would otherwise enroll and not finish. And whether artificial intelligence is weakening the entry-level market for graduates or only coinciding with a slow hiring cycle.

What no data can close: How much financial risk an 18-year-old should be asked to carry for a choice made with little information, and how much the public should absorb instead. Whether education after high school is a public good that, like high school, should be free at the point of use, or a private investment whose main beneficiary should pay. Whether fairness is judged between borrowers and the majority of adults who never borrowed, between today’s borrowers and those who already repaid, or between this generation and earlier ones who paid less. And whether the goal of public policy should be more people with degrees, or more people with good jobs by whatever route.
