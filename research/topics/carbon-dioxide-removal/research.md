# Carbon Dioxide Removal — research

Slug: carbon-dioxide-removal
Scope: Removing CO2 already in the atmosphere. Not solar geoengineering.
Researched: 2026-11-25

## Overview

Carbon dioxide removal, or CDR, means human activities that take carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere and store it durably: in trees and soils, in rock underground, in the ocean, or in long-lived products. The IPCC defines it this way and separates it from carbon capture on smokestacks, which only keeps new fossil emissions out of the air. Removal comes in two families. Conventional removal is mostly forests and soils: planting and restoring forests, managing them for more carbon, and storing carbon in wood products; it accounts for almost all of the removal happening today. Novel removal covers newer methods: biochar (charcoal from plant waste worked into soil), bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS, burning or processing plant matter and burying the CO2), direct air capture with storage (DACCS, machines that pull CO2 from ambient air and inject it underground), enhanced rock weathering (spreading crushed rock that binds CO2), and ocean methods such as adding alkalinity to seawater. This topic covers how much removal the world needs, what it costs, whether it works and can be verified, who should pay for it, and whether counting on it helps or harms the effort to cut emissions. It deliberately leaves aside solar geoengineering, which tries to cool the planet by reflecting sunlight rather than removing carbon (a separate topic), the pace of reaching net zero and the phase-out of fossil fuels, and carbon pricing in general, touching each only where it bears on removal.

The why-now event: the UN climate summit COP31 in Antalya closed on 22 November 2026 without adopting its main political text. The final draft cover decision, which the presidency had renamed the “Antalya pact,” contained a paragraph “welcoming” carbon dioxide removal while stressing that these methods “should ensure environmental safeguards and respect the rights of Indigenous peoples”; at least 32 countries objected to the draft in the closing plenary, partly because it did not respond to the world’s overshoot of 1.5 °C, and it was not adopted, so no COP31 decision welcomes removal (Carbon Brief, 24 November 2026, https://www.carbonbrief.org/cop31-key-outcomes-for-food-forests-oceans-and-nature-at-the-un-climate-talks-in-antalya; Carbon Brief, https://www.carbonbrief.org/cop31-key-outcomes-agreed-at-the-un-climate-talks-in-antalya). Carbon Brief also reports that the Center for International Environmental Law counted a “record” 700 carbon capture and storage lobbyists at the summit. Three days earlier, on 19 November 2026, Canada set up 19 national standing offers to buy permanent removal credits across five methods, which its Treasury Board says makes it “the first national government to use competitive procurement for permanent CDR credits,” under a commitment to buy at least C$10 million of credits by 2030 (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, https://www.canada.ca/en/treasury-board-secretariat/news/2026/11/national-standing-offers-for-carbon-dioxide-removal-credits.html). The summit capped a year that tested the industry. In April 2026 Bloomberg and Heatmap reported that Microsoft, which had bought most of the durable removal sold anywhere, was pausing new deals; Microsoft said its program “has not ended” and that it may “adjust the pace or volume” of its buying, and signed a 650,000-tonne deal with the biogas producer BioCirc in May (TechCrunch, 20 May 2026). The US Department of Energy, which cancelled 10 of the 21 awards in its US$3.5 billion direct air capture hubs program in October 2025, kept the two flagship hubs in Texas and Louisiana in April 2026 while its 2027 budget request asked Congress to rescind US$2.3 billion of the program (Heatmap, 16 April 2026). Occidental’s Stratos plant in Texas, designed to capture up to 500,000 tonnes a year and first expected in late 2024, is now due to start at the end of 2026 (E&E News, 7 August 2026). On 17 July 2026 the European Commission proposed adding 250 million allowances to the EU Emissions Trading System between 2031 and 2040 and using the auction revenue to buy certified permanent removals, which would make the carbon market itself a large buyer of removal (ICAP, 17 July 2026).

The debate is live because the world is close to running out of room on its temperature goal. Human-induced warming reached 1.37 °C in 2025, and the remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C, about 130 billion tonnes of CO2 from the start of 2026, would be used up in around three years at current emissions (Copernicus, Indicators of Global Climate Change, 11 June 2026). Every IPCC pathway that reaches net zero includes some removal, and returning temperatures to 1.5 °C after overshooting it would require removing more CO2 than the world emits. Yet removal today is about 2.2 billion tonnes a year, almost all of it from forests, and novel methods remove about 2 million tonnes (State of CDR, 3rd edition, June 2026). The argument runs between scientists, start-ups, corporate buyers and governments who want to build a removal industry now as a necessary complement to cutting emissions; climate-justice groups and some climate scientists who see reliance on removal as a gamble that delays cuts and props up fossil fuels; oil producers and petrostates who see capture and removal as the way to keep producing oil and gas while meeting climate goals; and a group of academics and advocates who want fossil fuel producers themselves made to store, tonne for tonne, the carbon they sell.

- Comparison rows (same source, same unit): total removal — 2.2; conventional (forests, soils, wood products) — about 2.2 (99.9 percent of the total); novel methods (biochar, BECCS, DACCS, enhanced weathering and others) — 0.002
- Data year: latest available years, reported June 2026 (conventional removal is a 10-year average to 2023; novel figures run to 2025)
- Source label: The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal, 3rd edition (2026)
- Source URL: https://www.stateofcdr.org/asset/State-of-Carbon-Dioxide-Removal-Edition-3_June-2026.pdf
- Note: Humans remove about 2.2 billion tonnes of CO2 from the atmosphere a year, equal to about 5 percent of gross CO2 emissions.
- Context: The State of CDR report, an independent assessment led by researchers at Oxford, the Mercator Research Institute (MCC), IIASA, the World Resources Institute and others, is the standard global count. It finds that total removal is 2.2 GtCO2 per year, “equivalent to 5% of gross CO2 emissions,” and that conventional, land-based removal is 99.9 percent of it, with the largest contributions from China, the United States, the European Union, Brazil and Russia. Most of this is managed forests growing back; it can be reversed by fire, drought, pests or logging, which the IPCC notes makes vegetation and soil storage less durable than storage in rock. The figure counts only removal caused by deliberate human activity, not the much larger natural uptake by oceans and undisturbed land that would happen anyway.

- Note: Novel removal methods remove about 2 million tonnes of CO2 a year, about a thousandth of all removal.

- Comparison rows (same source, 2025 average, US$ per tonne): mineral products — 1,301; direct ocean capture — 1,100; DACCS — 593; enhanced weathering — 407; BECCS — 364; bio-oil storage — 362; ocean alkalinity enhancement — 279; biomass sinking — 250; biomass burial — 127; biochar — 136; peatland and coastal wetland restoration — 29; afforestation, reforestation and forest management — 17

- Note: A tonne of removal from direct air capture sold for about US$593 on average in 2025, against about US$17 for a tonne from forests.
- Context: Prices span nearly two orders of magnitude, and the spread tracks durability: methods that lock carbon in rock or deep storage for thousands of years cost hundreds of dollars a tonne, while forest credits that can be reversed by fire or logging cost tens. The average DACCS price rose from US$315 in 2024 to US$593 in 2025. For comparison, the World Bank puts the average direct carbon price worldwide at about US$21 a tonne in 2026, far below what durable removal sells for.

### 7. What direct air capture costs
- Label: Estimated cost of direct air capture with storage
- Value: 400 to 2,500
- Unit: US dollars per tonne of CO2 removed (levelized cost of gross removal, current estimates)
- Comparison rows (different bases, noted): current levelized cost, systematic review by van der Spek et al. (2025) — US$400 to 2,500; projected cost once deployment reaches gigatonne scale, same review — US$100 to 600; IPCC AR6 range for DACCS (2022) — US$100 to 300; US Department of Energy Carbon Negative Shot goal — under US$100
- Data year: review published 2025, reported in the State of CDR (2026); IPCC 2022
- Source label: The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal, 3rd edition (2026), citing van der Spek et al. (2025); IPCC AR6 WGIII Summary for Policymakers (2022)
- Source URL: https://www.stateofcdr.org/asset/State-of-Carbon-Dioxide-Removal-Edition-3_June-2026.pdf
- Note: Current estimates put direct air capture at US$400 to US$2,500 per tonne of CO2, with projections of US$100 to US$600 at very large scale.
- Context: The State of CDR report says the wide range reflects different technologies and assumptions about energy, plant design and sorbents, and that many studies leave out compression, transport, storage and supporting infrastructure, so “more comprehensive cost assessments are, therefore, needed.” The IPCC’s 2022 summary gave a lower range, US$100 to 300 per tonne (https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGIII_SPM.pdf). Those who favor building removal now point to learning curves in solar and batteries; those skeptical point out that the energy needed to separate CO2 from air, where it makes up about 0.04 percent, sets a floor that learning cannot remove. The Department of Energy’s Carbon Negative Shot goal, removal for less than US$100 per net tonne by 2032, is a target, not an estimate (https://www.energy.gov/fecm/carbon-negative-shot).
- Visualization: range (current cost on a scale from the US$100 target to US$2,500)

- Data year: IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group III (2022)

- Note: The IPCC’s 1.5 °C pathways use between 30 and 780 billion tonnes of removal by bioenergy with carbon capture between 2020 and 2100, a range that shows how much the models disagree.
- Context: The IPCC concludes that removal “to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable if net zero CO2 or GHG emissions are to be achieved” (paragraph C.11), and lists three roles for it: lowering net emissions in the near term, balancing residual emissions from sectors such as agriculture, aviation, shipping and industry in the mid-term, and achieving net negative emissions in the long term. The IPCC also says reliance on removal, pressure on land and biodiversity are “significantly reduced” in pathways that assume more efficient resource use or a shift toward sustainable development (C.3.6). - Label: Remaining carbon budget to hold warming to 1.5 °C
- Value: 130
- Unit: billion tonnes of CO2, from the start of 2026 (central estimate)

- Data year: start of 2026

- Note: About 130 billion tonnes of CO2 can still be emitted for 1.5 °C, about three years at current emissions.
- Context: The annual Indicators of Global Climate Change update, led by Piers Forster with dozens of climate scientists, finds that human-induced warming reached 1.37 °C in 2025 and that the central estimate of the remaining 1.5 °C budget “will be exhausted in around three years at current levels of CO2 emissions.” This is why removal has moved from a mid-century question to a present one: once the budget is spent, the only way to bring temperatures back toward 1.5 °C is to remove more CO2 than is emitted. - Note: The European Commission has proposed raising the EU carbon market cap by 250 million allowances and spending the auction revenue only on certified permanent removals.
- Context: Under the proposal, the Commission would auction the extra allowances between 2031 and 2040 and use the revenue “exclusively to purchase BioCCS and DACCS certified under the Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming (CRCF) Regulation,” in effect backing each extra allowance with a tonne removed. The EU’s own scientific advisory board had recommended in February 2025 only a “progressive integration” of permanent removals, once robust certification is in place and on condition that it does not deter emission cuts (Euronews, 21 February 2025). ## Questions

### 1. Will planning on carbon removal slow down emission cuts?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Delays real cuts / Adds to cuts
- Why it is contested: This is the “mitigation deterrence” or moral hazard question, and it sits under almost every other argument. If the prospect of future removal lets governments and companies cut less today, removal could leave the climate worse off even if it works; if removal is an addition to cuts, it is a pure gain. The evidence is thin on both sides because large-scale removal does not yet exist to test, and what exists is indirect: how models, pledges and corporate targets have treated removal so far.
- Best case for “Delays real cuts”: Kevin Anderson and Glen Peters argued in Science in 2016 that the models behind the Paris goals assumed vast future removal, which made deep near-term cuts look unnecessary and passed the burden to later generations. Net-zero targets, which net removals against emissions, give every emitter a reason to promise future removal instead of cutting now. Microsoft, the largest buyer of removal, has seen its emissions rise by more than half since 2020 even as it signed most of the world’s removal contracts. Oil producers openly present capture and removal as the reason they can keep producing, and the United States in 2025 raised the subsidy for using captured CO2 to pump more oil. Once a removal industry exists, it creates a constituency for the continued emissions that feed it.
- Best case for “Adds to cuts”: The IPCC finds removal unavoidable for net zero, so the question is not whether to rely on it but how to keep it from crowding out cuts, and that is a design problem with known fixes: separate targets for gross emissions and for removals, caps on how much removal counts, and like-for-like rules that only permanent removal can balance fossil emissions. The EU’s scientific advisory board recommended exactly these safeguards. The scale of removal available in the next decade, a few million tonnes a year against roughly 40 billion tonnes of CO2 emitted, is far too small to be a credible excuse for anyone. And emissions are not falling slowly because removal is on offer; they are falling slowly for political and economic reasons that would exist without it, while overshoot of 1.5 °C means removal will be needed to repair damage either way.

### 2. How much carbon removal will the world actually need?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Billions of tonnes yearly / Much less, if cuts are fast
- Why it is contested: The answer depends on how fast emissions fall, how large the residual emissions from farming, aviation, cement and steel turn out to be, and whether the world chooses to bring temperatures back down after overshooting 1.5 °C. Model pathways span an order of magnitude, and every number is conditional on the others.
- Best case for “Billions of tonnes yearly”: The State of CDR report finds that the most ambitious 1.5 °C pathways need about 8.75 billion tonnes of removal a year by 2050 and about 11 billion tonnes at the point net-zero CO2 is reached. The 1.5 °C carbon budget will be spent in about three years at current emissions, so any return toward 1.5 °C means net-negative emissions for decades. Residual emissions from agriculture and long-haul aviation have no full technical substitute in sight. Planning for less than the models show, and then needing more, would be the costlier mistake, because removal industries take decades to build.
- Best case for “Much less, if cuts are fast”: The IPCC itself notes that dependence on removal is “significantly reduced” in pathways that assume more efficient resource use or a shift toward sustainable development, and the State of CDR report’s own first lesson is to prioritize emission cuts to limit removal dependence. Each tonne not emitted is a tonne that never has to be removed, at a fraction of the cost. Residual emissions are not fixed: diets, materials efficiency, electrified industry and less flying shrink them. The large removal numbers in models partly reflect model assumptions that make future removal cheap, so they describe a modeling choice as much as a physical need.

### 3. Can engineered removal become cheap and large enough to matter?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Costs will fall fast / Stays costly and small
- Why it is contested: Direct air capture sells for about US$600 a tonne and current cost estimates run from US$400 to 2,500; BECCS, enhanced weathering and biochar are cheaper but limited by biomass, land and verification. Whether these follow the learning curves of solar panels and batteries, or the stubborn costs of nuclear plants and fossil carbon capture, will decide whether billions of tonnes are affordable.
- Best case for “Costs will fall fast”: Novel removal has been growing about 40 percent a year, a pace similar to early solar, and the State of CDR report finds the growth needed for 2030 goals “not unprecedented” against 181 historical technologies. Direct air capture plants are built from standardized modules, the kind of technology that learns quickly with repetition; Climeworks reported in 2026 that upgrades doubled throughput and halved operating cost per tonne at its Iceland plant. A systematic review projects US$100 to 600 a tonne at gigatonne scale. Learning only happens through deployment, which is why early buyers like Frontier and governments like Canada are paying high prices now.
- Best case for “Stays costly and small”: Separating CO2 from air, where it is 0.04 percent of the gas, takes a minimum amount of energy that no learning curve can remove, and gigatonne-scale direct air capture would need a large share of the world’s clean electricity. The flagship plants have underdelivered: Climeworks’ Mammoth plant captured just over 100 tonnes in 2024 against a design capacity of 36,000, and Occidental’s Stratos plant is two years late. The IPCC finds that mitigation options costing US$100 a tonne or less could halve global emissions by 2030, with options under US$20 making up more than half of that potential, so removal at several hundred dollars competes with far cheaper cuts that have not been made.

### 4. Should removals be allowed to offset ongoing emissions, or be kept in separate targets?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Count against emissions / Keep separate ledgers
- Why it is contested: A tonne removed and a tonne not emitted have the same effect on the atmosphere, which argues for letting them trade. But a removal promised for the future is less certain than an emission avoided today, and letting the two trade freely is exactly what critics fear will slow cuts. Corporate net-zero claims, the EU carbon market proposal and the design of Paris Agreement credit markets all turn on this choice.
- Best case for “Count against emissions”: Carbon markets work because they find the cheapest tonnes; walling removal off from emissions raises the cost of climate goals and starves removal of the demand it needs to scale. A company or country that pays for a verified, permanent tonne of removal has done the same climate good as one that cut a tonne, and often more than one that bought cheap avoided-emission credits. The European Commission’s July 2026 proposal to fund 250 million tonnes of permanent removal through the carbon market, and Microsoft’s use of removal to reach a carbon-negative goal, are how a market for removal gets built at all.
- Best case for “Keep separate ledgers”: The EU’s scientific advisory board recommended separate legally binding targets for gross emission cuts, permanent removals and temporary removals, because merging them sends the wrong signal to investors and lets cheap promises stand in for hard cuts. Separate targets let governments plan removal on its own merits while making sure emissions fall as fast as they can. Adding removal-backed allowances to a cap also loosens the cap today on the strength of removals delivered years later; if those removals fail, the emissions have already happened.

### 5. Should tree and soil carbon count the same as carbon stored in rock?
- Tag: value
- Poles: All removal counts / Only durable storage
- Why it is contested: Forests and soils provide almost all removal today and are cheap, about US$17 a tonne in voluntary markets, with benefits for biodiversity and livelihoods. But carbon in trees and soils can return to the air through fire, drought or logging within decades, while fossil CO2 warms the climate for millennia. Whether a forest tonne can balance a fossil tonne divides scientists, standard-setters and governments.
- Best case for “All removal counts”: Land-based removal is the only removal operating at billion-tonne scale, it is available now while novel methods are still in pilots, and it pays communities and landowners to protect and restore ecosystems that matter for reasons beyond carbon. Refusing to count it would strip finance from forests, especially in the Global South, in favor of expensive machines in rich countries. Temporary storage still buys time during the decades when warming peaks, and good accounting can discount for the risk of reversal through buffers and monitoring.
- Best case for “Only durable storage”: The IPCC notes that vegetation and soil storage “can be reversed by human or natural disturbances” and is “prone to climate change impacts,” while geological storage lasts 10,000 years or more. Myles Allen and colleagues argue for “geological net zero”: every tonne of fossil CO2 emitted should be matched by a tonne returned to permanent geological storage, because counting land sinks against fossil emissions would leave warming continuing even at nominal net zero. The EU advisory board warns that Europe’s own land sink is already declining under drought and fire. Treating a forest tonne as equal to a fossil tonne lets emitters buy the least durable form of balance for the most durable form of harm.

### 6. Who should pay for carbon removal?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Taxpayers and buyers / Fossil producers pay
- Why it is contested: Removal at billions of tonnes a year would cost hundreds of billions of dollars a year, and today almost all of it is paid for by one technology company and a few government programs. Each funding route implies a different view of responsibility: public budgets treat removal as a public good, voluntary buyers treat it as corporate responsibility, carbon markets treat it as a compliance cost, and producer obligations treat it as cleaning up a product.
- Best case for “Taxpayers and buyers”: Removal is a public good like flood defenses or vaccine stockpiles, and early, expensive tonnes are a research investment that only governments and committed buyers will make. Public procurement, such as Canada’s 2026 standing offers and the US purchase prize, and advance market commitments like Frontier’s pledge of US$1.8 billion by 2040, give suppliers the predictable demand they need, without waiting for politically hard obligations on fossil producers. Spreading the cost through budgets and markets also avoids raising energy prices for households during the transition.
- Best case for “Fossil producers pay”: Those who extract and sell fossil carbon should bear the cost of returning it underground, the polluter-pays principle applied to the carbon in their products. A carbon takeback obligation, requiring producers to store a rising share of the CO2 from what they sell, would fund removal and storage at scale without relying on taxpayers or a few voluntary buyers, and the cost would pass gradually into fuel prices, shifting demand toward clean energy. The EU already requires 44 oil and gas producers to provide 50 million tonnes a year of storage capacity by 2030, and an August 2026 open letter from Oxford, UCL, Imperial and Bristol academics proposed making such an obligation a condition of approving new UK oil and gas extraction.

### 7. Should fossil fuel companies be the ones building removal?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Use their expertise / Conflict of interest
- Why it is contested: Oil and gas companies know how to drill wells, move gases by pipeline and manage reservoirs, which is most of what geological storage needs, and Occidental is building the world’s largest direct air capture plant. They also have an interest in removal as a reason to keep selling oil and gas, and captured CO2 can be used to push more oil out of old fields.
- Best case for “Use their expertise”: Storage underground is the bottleneck for every durable removal method, and the people who have spent decades injecting fluids into rock are the ones who can do it safely and at scale. The EU’s storage obligation rests on the same logic: producers already have the subsurface data, wells and know-how. Excluding them would slow removal for reasons of image rather than climate, and every tonne removed from the air counts the same regardless of who removes it.
- Best case for “Conflict of interest”: Occidental’s then chief executive Vicki Hollub said in 2022 that dealing with emissions offers “a way to continue the production of oil and gas for the foreseeable future,” which is the opposite of what removal is for in climate pathways. Since July 2025 the US tax credit pays the same for captured CO2 used to produce more oil as for CO2 stored permanently. The Center for International Environmental Law counted a record 700 carbon capture lobbyists at COP31. A removal industry led by fossil producers has every incentive to promise removal and slow the phase-out of the fuels that make removal necessary.

### 8. Can removal be measured and verified well enough to pay for?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Verifiable now / Not yet reliable
- Why it is contested: A buyer pays for a tonne nobody can see. For direct air capture with geological storage, the tonne is metered at the plant and the well. For enhanced rock weathering, ocean alkalinity enhancement, soil carbon and forests, the tonne must be estimated from models and sampling of open, variable natural systems, and estimates can be off by large margins.
- Best case for “Verifiable now”: Measurement, reporting and verification protocols have matured fast: the EU adopted its Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming certification regulation, registries such as Puro.earth and Isometric publish methodologies and issue credits after third-party audits, and the IPCC is preparing a 2027 methodology report on counting removal in national inventories. Field trials and coordinated research networks for enhanced weathering are building the evidence base year by year. Conservative crediting, which discounts uncertain tonnes, lets buyers pay for what is known while science narrows the rest.
- Best case for “Not yet reliable”: The voluntary carbon market has already been through one credibility collapse, after studies found that many forest offset credits did not represent real reductions, and removal methods that rely on modeling open systems face the same risk at higher prices. For ocean methods the CO2 taken up can take months or years to equilibrate and is hard to trace; for enhanced weathering the share of rock that actually reacts depends on soil, climate and time. Paying hundreds of dollars a tonne for tonnes that cannot be confirmed invites a second scandal that would set the whole field back.

### 9. Can removal scale without harming land, food and communities?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Benefits outweigh harms / Too costly to people
- Why it is contested: BECCS, biochar and afforestation at billion-tonne scale would need very large areas of land or biomass, and direct air capture needs land, energy and storage sites. The IPCC finds outcomes range from strong co-benefits to serious harm depending on how projects are done, which leaves the judgment about acceptable risk to values.
- Best case for “Benefits outweigh harms”: The IPCC finds that reforestation, improved forest management, soil carbon, peatland restoration and blue carbon can enhance biodiversity, employment and local livelihoods. Biochar and enhanced weathering can raise crop yields, and BECCS can run on wastes and residues rather than dedicated crops. Policy design, such as sustainability criteria in certification and community benefit agreements, can steer projects toward co-benefits, and removal projects can bring investment and jobs to rural areas.
- Best case for “Too costly to people”: The IPCC also warns that afforestation and biomass crops for BECCS or biochar, “when poorly implemented,” can harm biodiversity, food and water security, local livelihoods and “the rights of Indigenous Peoples, especially if implemented at large scales and where land tenure is insecure.” Model pathways that assume hundreds of billions of tonnes of BECCS imply land use on the scale of entire countries. Groups such as Biofuelwatch oppose BECCS because it depends on “vast amounts of biomass,” and communities near carbon dioxide pipelines have seen leaks: a 2020 pipeline rupture in Satartia, Mississippi, sickened dozens of people, according to Taxpayers for Common Sense.

## Positions

### 1. Scale up carbon removal now, alongside deep emission cuts
- Adherents:
  - Carbon180 (institution), a US nonprofit that describes itself as “a new breed of climate NGO on a mission to reverse two centuries of carbon emissions,” aiming to “bring carbon removal solutions to gigaton scale” and to remove 30 million tonnes of US CO2 a year by 2030, while stating that avoiding the worst harms requires both cutting emissions and removing carbon. https://carbon180.org/
  - Ottmar Edenhofer, chair of the European Scientific Advisory Board on Climate Change, presenting the board’s February 2025 report on scaling up removals: “We recommend a progressive integration of permanent removals in the [emissions trading system] ETS,” with the condition that “this is only acceptable when we can prevent mitigation deterrence.” https://www.euronews.com/2025/02/21/eu-must-not-slow-climate-action-on-hopes-of-future-carbon-removals-top-climate-advisor-war
  - Also in this camp: Frontier, the buyers’ coalition founded by Stripe, Google, Shopify and others, “an advance market commitment to buy $1.8B of permanent carbon removal by 2040” (https://frontierclimate.com/); removal companies such as Climeworks; the Government of Canada, which began competitive purchasing in November 2026; and the European Commission, whose July 2026 proposal would make its carbon market a buyer of permanent removal.
- The case, in the camp’s voice: The science is settled on the basic point. The IPCC finds that removal to balance hard-to-abate residual emissions is “unavoidable” for net zero, and with the 1.5 °C budget nearly gone, removal is also the only way to bring temperatures back down after the overshoot that is now almost certain. Removal at billions of tonnes a year cannot be switched on in 2045; solar, wind and batteries took decades of early, expensive deployment to become cheap, and removal needs the same runway starting now. That means a portfolio, forests and soils now, biochar and BECCS next, direct air capture and weathering as they mature, with strict measurement and permanent storage for the tonnes that balance fossil emissions. Removal is never a substitute for cutting emissions; it is the part of the job cutting cannot do. The bottleneck today is demand, not technology: one company buys most of the world’s durable removal, so governments must become buyers through procurement and compliance markets.
- Best evidence: The State of CDR report finds that even the most ambitious 1.5 °C pathways need about 8.75 billion tonnes of removal a year by 2050 and that novel removal is already growing about 40 percent a year, a pace it calls similar to successful technologies like solar. Buyers have contracted about 50.5 million tonnes of durable removal (CDR.fyi), showing private demand where credible. Costs fall with deployment: Climeworks reported in September 2026 that upgrades at its Mammoth plant doubled throughput and halved operating cost per tonne. Governments in Sweden, Denmark, Canada, the UK and the EU are building policy to buy or support removal.
- Variation inside the camp: Members differ on whether removals should trade freely with emissions or be kept under separate targets, as the EU advisory board recommends; on how much weight to put on land-based versus engineered methods; and on whether public budgets, voluntary corporate buyers or compliance markets should carry the cost. Some, including the Commission, would fold removal into carbon markets now; others would wait until certification is proven.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that counting on removal delays cuts): The risk of mitigation deterrence is real, which is why this camp supports separate targets, limits on how much removal counts, and like-for-like rules that only permanent removal can balance fossil CO2. But the alternative to building removal is not faster cuts; it is arriving at mid-century with residual emissions and an overshoot and no tools to deal with them. The few million tonnes on offer today are too small to excuse anyone, and the deterrence argument cuts both ways: a world that refuses to build removal still faces residual emissions and overshoot, with nothing to answer them.

### 2. Cut emissions at the source instead of relying on engineered removal
- Adherents:
  - Center for International Environmental Law (institution), which says carbon capture “masks the harmful carbon emissions from the underlying source,” lists direct air capture among the variants that “depends on CCS or CCUS to manage the captured carbon,” and calls the premise of BECCS “flawed and scientifically discredited.” https://www.ciel.org/issue/carbon-capture-and-storage/
  - Kevin Anderson, professor of energy and climate change at the University of Manchester and co-author with Glen Peters of “The trouble with negative emissions” (Science, 2016): “Negative emission technologies are not an insurance policy. They are a high risk gamble with tomorrow’s generations.” https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/reliance-on-negative-emissions-technologies/
  - Also in this camp: Biofuelwatch, which opposes BECCS and biochar as “unproven at scale” and dependent on “vast amounts of biomass” (https://www.biofuelwatch.org.uk/).
- The case, in the camp’s voice: The climate crisis is caused by burning fossil fuels, and the only sure way to stop it is to stop burning them. Engineered removal has been promised for decades and delivered almost nothing: novel methods remove about 2 million tonnes a year against roughly 40 billion emitted, the flagship direct air capture plant captured about a hundred tonnes in a year, and most captured CO2 in the United States has been used to pump more oil. Every promise of future removal becomes an excuse to emit today, and the fossil fuel industry knows it, which is why its lobbyists crowd climate summits and why it champions capture and removal while expanding production. Money spent at US$600 a tonne on machines is money not spent on renewables, efficiency and electrification that cut emissions for a small fraction of the cost. Protecting and restoring forests, wetlands and the land rights of the people who live there is worth doing for its own sake, but it must not be turned into credits that let polluters keep polluting.
- Best evidence: The IPCC finds that mitigation options costing US$100 a tonne or less could halve global emissions by 2030, with options under US$20 making up more than half of that potential, while direct air capture sells for about US$593 a tonne (State of CDR, 2025 average). Mammoth captured just over 100 tonnes in 2024 against a 36,000-tonne design (Heimildin, via Latitude Media), Stratos is two years late, and only 1.8 of 50.5 million contracted durable tonnes have been delivered (CDR.fyi). Since July 2025 the US tax credit pays the same for CO2 used in oil recovery as for permanent storage. The IPCC itself warns that biomass for BECCS, “when poorly implemented,” can harm food security, biodiversity and the rights of Indigenous Peoples.
- Variation inside the camp: Some members reject all removal markets, including forest offsets, as false solutions that commodify land; others accept ecosystem restoration and limited research into novel methods but oppose subsidies and any reliance on removal in targets. Fiscal conservatives such as Taxpayers for Common Sense reach a similar conclusion about carbon capture subsidies from the opposite direction, arguing they reward oil recovery without verification.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that the IPCC says removal is unavoidable): The IPCC says removal is unavoidable to balance residual emissions after deep cuts, and how large those residuals are is a choice. Every tonne not emitted is a tonne that never needs removing, at far lower cost and certainty. The honest response to overshoot is to cut faster, not to bet the future on technologies that have not worked at scale; research can continue, but no plan, target or budget should count on them.

### 3. Keep oil and gas in the energy mix and capture or remove their carbon
- Adherents:
  - Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (institution), whose Secretary General Haitham Al Ghais wrote in April 2024 that the industry, by investing in carbon capture, clean hydrogen and direct air capture, is “showing that it is possible to reduce emissions while producing the oil the world needs.” https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/end-oil-not-sight-opec-173849637.html
  - Vicki Hollub, then chief executive of Occidental Petroleum, which owns the Stratos direct air capture plant, speaking in October 2022: “If we focus on emissions, I think there is a way to continue the production of oil and gas for the foreseeable future.” https://finance.yahoo.com/news/vicki-hollub-says-occidental-nearly-140000057.html
  - Also in this camp: Saudi Arabia, which pursues its net-zero goal through a “circular carbon economy” framework built on reducing, reusing, recycling and removing carbon, and Occidental’s current chief executive Richard Jackson, who in August 2026 said the company wants to sell carbon capture services to data centers and power plants (E&E News).
- The case, in the camp’s voice: The world will use oil and gas for decades, because demand keeps growing in developing economies and no alternative yet supplies the same energy at the same scale, reliability and price. The climate problem is the emissions, not the molecules, so the fastest realistic route to net zero is to capture emissions where they are made and remove from the air what cannot be captured. The oil and gas industry is uniquely equipped to do this: it has the geologists, the wells, the pipelines, the storage reservoirs and the capital, and it is already building the world’s largest direct air capture plant. Forcing an early end to fossil fuels would raise energy costs, harm energy security and push production toward less careful producers, while a technology-neutral approach rewards every tonne kept out of the air.
- Best evidence: Stratos is designed to capture up to 500,000 tonnes a year, about 14 times the largest plant now operating, with early credit buyers including Microsoft, Amazon, ANA and Bain & Company (https://www.1pointfive.com/projects/stratos). The US industry has injected CO2 underground for enhanced oil recovery since the 1970s. The US 45Q tax credit, created in 2008, has been extended or expanded by Congress in 2018, 2020, 2022 and 2025, under majorities of both parties (Taxpayers for Common Sense fact sheet). The IPCC’s own pathways rely on carbon capture and storage alongside removal, and the EU’s storage obligation relies on oil and gas producers’ subsurface expertise.
- Variation inside the camp: Oil companies differ on how much they invest: Occidental has made direct air capture a business line, while others focus on capture at refineries and gas processing plants or on selling storage. Producer governments range from Saudi Arabia’s circular carbon economy to the United States, which under the current administration cut most climate programs but kept the carbon capture tax credit and two direct air capture hubs.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that this is a license to keep polluting): A tonne of CO2 captured or removed and stored is a tonne out of the atmosphere, whoever does it and whatever else they sell. Those who would rather phase fossil fuels out have no plan for the demand that remains in the meantime, and refusing to let producers deploy capture means that demand is met with nothing captured at all. The industry also bears real risk: Occidental has spent years and its own capital building Stratos, and its chief executive has said it cannot keep investing without regulatory support and buyers.

### 4. Require fossil fuel producers to store the carbon they sell
- Adherents:
  - Myles Allen, professor of geosystem science at the University of Oxford, a lead author of an August 2026 open letter to the UK energy secretary proposing that companies seeking approval for new extraction be required to permanently store a rising share of their CO2, a Carbon Takeback Obligation: “This is not a ‘get-out-of-jail-free card’ for the fossil fuel industry.” https://www.smithschool.ox.ac.uk/news/oxford-smith-school-academics-co-lead-open-letter-desnz-secretary-state-carbon-storage
  - Bellona Europa (institution), which supports the EU rule requiring oil and gas producers to provide 50 million tonnes a year of CO2 injection capacity by 2030, says producers “have done little to make it happen,” and argues “it must be rigorously enforced.” https://eu.bellona.org/publication/oil-gas-faces-its-accountability-moment/
  - Also in this camp: the Carbon Balance Initiative, whose UK director Stephanie Loo led the August 2026 letter, and the other lead authors from UCL, Imperial College, the University of Bristol and the Oxford Smith School; and the EU’s Net-Zero Industry Act, whose Article 23 put a version of the idea into law.
- The case, in the camp’s voice: The debate is stuck between those who want removal paid for by taxpayers and a few tech companies, and those who want no removal at all; neither will stop warming. Warming stops when every tonne of fossil CO2 put into the air is matched by a tonne put permanently back underground, so the obligation should fall on the companies that bring fossil carbon out of the ground. Require producers and importers to store a rising share of the CO2 from what they sell, reaching 100 percent by the net-zero year, and removal and storage get built at the pace and scale needed, financed by the polluter rather than the public. The cost passes gradually into fuel prices, which steers demand toward clean energy without a sudden shock. Only permanent geological storage should count, because carbon in forests and soils can return to the air and counting it against fossil emissions would leave warming continuing at nominal net zero.
- Best evidence: The EU already requires 44 oil and gas producers to provide 50 million tonnes a year of storage capacity by the end of 2030 (European Commission, May 2025), showing such an obligation can be legislated; producers have brought 15 legal cases against it (Bellona), showing it bites. Allen and colleagues’ 2024 Nature paper on “geological net zero” argues that counting passive land and ocean uptake toward net zero would not halt warming. The State of CDR report notes emerging literature calling for removal that matches the durability of the emission it balances.
- Variation inside the camp: Advocates differ on whether the obligation should be set nationally, as the UK letter proposes, or across a trading bloc like the EU; whether it should cover only new extraction or all fossil carbon sold; and how fast the stored share should rise. Some, like Bellona, focus on enforcing the storage capacity rule already in EU law rather than a full takeback obligation.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that it legitimizes continued extraction): It does the opposite of a license to pollute. A rising storage obligation raises the cost of every barrel and tonne of fossil fuel, year after year, until extraction without matching storage is impossible, which phases out unabated fossil fuels through price rather than by decree. Those who want an outright phase-out still face the residual emissions and overshoot that only storage can address, and this approach makes the people who caused the problem pay to fix it.

## Crux

Will planning on carbon removal add to emission cuts or stand in for them?

Every camp agrees that warming will not stop until the CO2 put into the atmosphere is balanced by CO2 taken out, and that the 1.5 °C budget is nearly spent. They split over whether building and counting on removal now makes that balance more likely to be reached, or less.

The part evidence could settle: whether removal can be delivered at the scale, cost and permanence that plans assume, and whether promises of removal change how fast emissions are actually cut. The first is being tested now, plant by plant and tonne by tonne: whether delivered tonnes catch up with the 50 million contracted, whether direct air capture costs fall toward US$100 to 600 a tonne or stay in the hundreds to thousands, whether enhanced weathering and ocean methods can be measured reliably, and whether BECCS can find biomass without harming land and food. The second can be studied by watching how companies and governments that buy or plan removal behave: whether their gross emissions fall faster or slower than those that do not, and whether removal targets kept separate from emission targets leave cuts intact.

The part no data can close: how much risk of future failure it is acceptable to load onto later generations in exchange for flexibility today, and whether any continued fossil emissions may rightly be balanced by removal at all. One side weighs most heavily the danger of reaching mid-century without the tools to deal with residual emissions and overshoot; another the danger of a promise that lets emissions continue and then fails to deliver. Who should bear the cost, taxpayers, voluntary buyers or the producers of fossil fuels, and whether a tonne stored in a forest can answer for a tonne of fossil carbon, are choices about responsibility and fairness, not facts to be measured.
