# Labor Power — research

## Overview

Labor power is the bargaining strength of people who work for wages: how much say they have over their pay, hours and conditions relative to the firms that employ them or the platforms that dispatch them. This topic covers four linked questions. First, whether pay has fallen behind productivity and, if so, whether weakened worker bargaining power is the reason. Second, unions and collective bargaining: why membership has fallen, whether labor law should make organizing easier, and what unions do to wages, jobs and firms. Third, the minimum wage: how high a legal floor can go before it costs jobs, and who should set it. Fourth, the legal status of people who work through apps such as Uber, Lyft and DoorDash: whether they are employees, independent contractors or something in between. The page is centered on the United States, where the fights are sharpest, with comparisons to Europe, the United Kingdom and other rich countries where the rules differ. It deliberately leaves to their own pages the overall gap between rich and poor (economic inequality), what artificial intelligence and automation do to jobs (AI and the future of work), cash guarantees that do not depend on work (universal basic income), and immigration policy, which appears here only where one camp treats it as a labor-market lever.

Why now: on 9 September 2026 California’s Public Employment Relations Board certified the California Gig Workers Union, formed with the backing of the Service Employees International Union, as the statewide bargaining representative for all drivers working for Uber and Lyft, after at least 30 percent of active drivers signed authorizations under a 2025 state law, AB 1340, that lets drivers bargain collectively while remaining independent contractors (PERB, https://perb.ca.gov/news/perb-certifies-california-gig-workers-union-as-certified-driver-bargaining-organization-under-tnc-act/). It followed Massachusetts, which certified the first US union of rideshare drivers in May 2026 after voters approved a 2024 ballot question. The certification capped a year of unusual movement in labor law. On 9 June 2026 the US House passed the Faster Labor Contracts Act, 230 to 193, after a discharge petition forced a vote that the chamber’s Republican leadership had declined to schedule; the bill, sponsored in the Senate by Republican Josh Hawley and Democrat Cory Booker and backed by the Teamsters, would require bargaining to begin within 10 days of a union win and send stalled first contracts to mediation and binding arbitration, and analysts describe it as what would be the first amendment to the National Labor Relations Act in more than 50 years (McDermott Will & Schulz, https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/house-passes-the-faster-labor-contracts-act/). It now needs 60 votes in the Senate. On 29 June 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 in Trump v. Slaughter that the president may remove the heads of independent agencies at will, a holding that labor lawyers read as ending the job protection of National Labor Relations Board members (Labor Relations Update, https://www.laborrelationsupdate.com/2026/07/what-the-supreme-courts-slaughter-decision-means-for-the-nlrb/). On 7 August the Senate confirmed two nominees who gave the board a 3 to 1 Republican majority, which is expected to revisit Biden-era rulings on organizing (Jackson Lewis, https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/senate-confirms-macy-prouty-labor-board-giving-republican-members-critical-three-vote-majority). In February 2026 the Labor Department proposed replacing the 2024 test for who counts as an independent contractor with a looser one, and in Europe member states must write the Platform Work Directive, which creates a legal presumption that platform workers are employees when a platform controls their work, into national law by 2 December 2026.

The debate is live because the signals point in opposite directions. Unions are more popular than at any time since the 1960s: 71 percent of Americans approved of them in Gallup’s August 2026 poll, tying the 2022 high, and a record 47 percent want them to have more influence. Yet only 10.0 percent of US wage and salary workers belonged to one in 2025, half the 1983 rate, and 5.9 percent in the private sector. The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 since July 2009, which the Center for Economic and Policy Research counts as the longest stretch without a raise since it was created in 1938, while 30 states and the District of Columbia have set their own higher floors and voters have split, rejecting a $15 wage in Oklahoma in June 2026 and an $18 wage in California in 2024 after approving increases in other states. And the political map has scrambled: alongside the long contest between unions and Democrats on one side and business groups and free-market Republicans on the other, a populist conservative wing now sponsors labor bills, and app companies, unions and state governments are inventing new legal categories for gig work. The participants are unions and their federations, business associations such as the US Chamber of Commerce, app-based companies and their trade group Flex, think tanks across the spectrum (the Economic Policy Institute, the Cato Institute, American Compass), labor economists, the National Labor Relations Board and the courts, state legislatures and ballot campaigns, and, in Europe and the United Kingdom, governments now writing new employment rights into law.

Data year: 1979 to 2025
Source label: Economic Policy Institute, The Productivity–Pay Gap
Source URL: https://www.epi.org/productivity-pay-gap/
Note: By the Economic Policy Institute’s measure, US net productivity rose 90.2 percent from 1979 to 2025 while the hourly pay of a typical worker rose 33.0 percent.
Context: This is the most cited chart in the debate and also one of the most contested. The Economic Policy Institute, a labor-aligned think tank, compares “net” productivity (output per hour across the whole economy, minus the wear and tear on capital) with the inflation-adjusted hourly wages and benefits of production and nonsupervisory workers, roughly the 80 percent of private-sector workers who are not managers. It reads the gap as the result of policy choices that weakened workers’ bargaining power, including the decline of unions, an eroded minimum wage and high unemployment. Critics accept that a gap exists but argue its size depends on choices of measure. Productivity is deflated by output prices and pay by consumer prices, which have risen faster; average compensation, which includes higher earners, has tracked productivity far more closely than the typical worker’s; and benefits such as health insurance have grown as a share of pay. Anna Stansbury and Lawrence Summers (NBER Working Paper 24165, 2017, https://www.nber.org/papers/w24165) found that over 1973 to 2016 one percentage point faster productivity growth went with 0.7 to 1 point faster median and average compensation growth, which they read as evidence that the link is weakened but not broken. The same EPI page states that if typical pay had kept pace with productivity, the typical worker would earn $16.40 more per hour. EPI’s page blocks automated retrieval; the figures here are as stated in its page text indexed in October 2026.

Context: The labor share is the slice of everything the economy earns that goes to employees as pay and benefits; the rest goes to profits, interest, rent, the income of the self-employed, depreciation and taxes on production. Economists agree the share has fallen in the United States and many other rich countries since around 2000 but disagree about why: weaker unions and bargaining power, the rise of a few highly profitable “superstar” firms, automation and cheaper capital goods, globalization, and measurement issues such as how housing and depreciation are counted all have proponents.

Note: One in ten US wage and salary workers, 14.7 million people, belonged to a union in 2025, about half the 1983 rate; in the private sector the rate was 5.9 percent.
Context: The Bureau of Labor Statistics measures union membership from the monthly household survey; 1983 is the first year with comparable data, when the rate was 20.1 percent and there were 17.7 million members. Membership has held roughly steady in number but fallen as a share because nonunion employment has grown. The decline is concentrated in the private sector; government workers are more than five times as likely to be members. Rates vary widely by state, from below 5 percent to nearly 25 percent. The 2025 figures were released in February 2026 rather than the usual January. Explanations for the long decline include the shift from manufacturing to services, global competition, employer opposition and consultants, state right-to-work laws (26 states as of 2026, after Michigan repealed its law in February 2024), weak penalties under the National Labor Relations Act, and, in the view of free-market economists, workers’ own preference for nonunion jobs.

Note: About 11 percent of eligible US employees had their pay set by a collective agreement in 2024, against 98 percent in France and an OECD average of about one-third.
Context: Coverage, not membership, is what determines how many workers’ pay is actually bargained. In much of continental Europe, agreements negotiated between unions and employer associations for a whole sector apply to every worker in that sector, and governments often extend them by decree, so France combines a union membership rate of about 10 percent with near-universal coverage. In the United States, bargaining happens firm by firm or workplace by workplace, so coverage barely exceeds membership. The OECD’s December 2025 brief reports that average coverage fell from 47 percent in 1985 to 33.6 percent in 2023/24 and that only 10 of the 27 EU countries exceed the 80 percent threshold that, under the EU’s 2022 minimum wage directive, obliges governments to adopt an action plan to promote bargaining. This “adjusted” measure excludes employees with no legal right to bargain; national figures on other bases can differ, and the OECD notes that high coverage can coexist with weak bargaining.

Context: The nonpartisan budget office analyzed a bill that would have raised the federal minimum in steps to $15 by June 2025 and then indexed it to median wages. Note: In a survey of US minimum wage studies, 79.2 percent of the authors’ preferred estimates of the employment effect were negative, and 46.2 percent were negative and statistically significant at the 5 percent level.
Context: David Neumark, a leading academic critic of minimum wage increases, and Peter Shirley collected the preferred employment estimates from 70 US studies published since 1992, asking the authors to confirm which estimates best represented their conclusions. They report that 79.2 percent of the estimates are negative, 53.8 percent negative and significant at the 10 percent level, and 46.2 percent at the 5 percent level, with the strongest effects for teenagers, young adults and the less educated, and weaker effects in studies of low-wage industries such as restaurants (64.5 percent negative, 32.3 percent significant). The two readings rest on different methods for choosing comparison groups, and the disagreement has not been settled.

Source label: Bloomberg Law, “Analysis: Now It Takes 465 Days to Sign a Union’s First Contract” (2 August 2022)

Note: In Bloomberg Law’s sample of 391 first contracts signed since 2005, newly unionized workers waited on average 465 days, and at the median 374 days, to ratify a first contract.
Context: US law requires employers to bargain “in good faith” with a newly certified union but does not require them to reach an agreement, and the penalties for stalling are weak, so many new unions never win a contract at all. Bloomberg Law found that 53 percent of the first contracts it examined took a year or longer. The delay is the problem the Faster Labor Contracts Act, passed by the House in June 2026, targets, and supporters of the bill cite a figure of 458 days drawn from the same Bloomberg Law series. Opponents of the bill, including the Club for Growth, argue that first contracts take time because they are complex and that imposing terms through federal arbitration would bind workers to contracts they never voted on. The Bloomberg Law sample counts only contracts that were eventually signed, so it understates delay for units that never reach one.

## Questions

Question: Has weak worker bargaining power held pay below what workers produce?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Power held pay down / Pay still tracks output
Why contested: Everyone agrees that the typical US worker’s pay has grown far more slowly than average output per hour since the late 1970s, and that labor’s share of national income has fallen. The fight is over how much of the gap is real and what caused it. The answer depends on which workers are counted, how pay and output are adjusted for inflation, whether benefits are included, and whether falling union density and rising employer concentration can be shown to cause wage losses rather than merely coinciding with them.
Case for power held pay down: For three decades after the Second World War, pay and productivity rose together; after 1979 they split, and the timing matches the collapse of private-sector unions, the erosion of the minimum wage, the decline of large strikes and a long run of high unemployment. The Economic Policy Institute calculates that net productivity rose 90.2 percent between 1979 and 2025 while typical worker pay rose 33.0 percent. The labor share of income fell to 50.9 percent in 2025, its lowest since the 1930s. A large research literature on monopsony, the power of employers in labor markets with few competing buyers of labor, finds that many workers face few realistic alternatives and that employers mark wages down well below what workers add. When labor markets tightened sharply after the pandemic, wages at the bottom rose fastest, reversing about 38 percent of the rise in the gap between high and low wages since 1980 (Autor, Dube and McGrew, NBER Working Paper 31010), which shows that power, not productivity, had been holding them down.
Case for pay still tracks output: Much of the measured gap is an artifact of method. Productivity is deflated by the prices of what the economy produces and pay by the prices of what consumers buy, and the two diverged; benefits such as health insurance take a growing share of compensation; and rising inequality among workers means the median worker’s pay grew more slowly than the average even though average compensation tracked productivity much more closely. Stansbury and Summers found that over 1973 to 2016 each extra point of productivity growth still went with 0.7 to 1 point of extra median and average compensation growth. The fall in labor’s share is concentrated in a period of rapid technological change and the rise of a few very productive firms, and appears across countries with very different union laws, which suggests technology and global competition rather than labor law are the main drivers.

Question: How high can a minimum wage go before it costs low-wage jobs?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Little job loss / Real job loss
Why contested: The question is no longer whether any minimum wage costs jobs but at what level the costs become large, for whom, and over what time. Studies disagree because they choose different comparison groups, time horizons and populations, and recent state and city minimums are higher, relative to local wages, than anything studied before.
Case for little job loss: The modern literature, starting with David Card and Alan Krueger’s 1994 study of fast-food restaurants, finds that moderate minimum wage increases raise pay with little or no loss of jobs. Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer’s study of 138 state increases found the number of low-wage jobs essentially unchanged five years later. When employers have wage-setting power, a higher floor can even raise employment by drawing more people into work and reducing costly turnover. Dube’s review for the UK Treasury found “a very muted effect” on employment and supported a UK floor of up to two-thirds of the median wage, which Britain has since adopted without the job losses critics predicted. Supporters point to cities such as Seattle and states such as California, which have raised minimums well above $16 an hour, and to California’s $20 minimum for fast-food workers, as places where low-wage employment did not collapse as opponents warned.
Case for real job loss: Neumark and Shirley’s survey of 70 US studies found that 79.2 percent of the preferred estimates were negative, with the clearest losses among teenagers, young adults and less-educated workers, the people a minimum wage is meant to help. Job losses can show up as fewer hours, slower hiring, less training, automation of entry-level tasks and firms that never open rather than as layoffs, which many studies are not designed to catch. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that a $15 federal minimum would cost about 1.4 million jobs on average, with a real chance of 2.7 million. A single national floor would bite hardest in low-wage regions: a wage that is modest in Seattle is a large share of the median in rural Mississippi.

Question: Should US labor law make it easier for workers to form unions and win contracts?
Tag: value
Poles: Make organizing easier / Protect individual choice
Why contested: The measures on the table, including recognizing a union when a majority sign cards, banning mandatory anti-union meetings, imposing first contracts through arbitration, heavier penalties for illegal firings and the repeal of state right-to-work laws, each trade the collective power of a majority against the choice of individual workers and employers. How to weigh those is a judgment about what freedom at work means.
Case for make organizing easier: Workers who want a union face an uneven contest under current law. Employers were long allowed to require attendance at anti-union meetings, a practice the National Labor Relations Board banned only in 2024 and that its new majority may restore; they can stall bargaining for more than a year after a union wins, and fire organizers knowing that the penalty is usually back pay years later. Gallup finds that 71 percent of Americans approve of unions, yet only 10 percent of workers belong to one; supporters read that gap as evidence of a law that blocks what workers want. Right-to-work laws let workers enjoy the wages and grievance protection a union must provide to everyone in the unit without paying for them, which starves unions of resources. Strong unions, on this view, are the only force that gives ordinary workers a share of the gains they produce and a counterweight to corporate power in politics.
Case for protect individual choice: The secret-ballot election is the fairest test of whether workers want a union, and card-check campaigns expose workers to pressure from organizers and coworkers. Requiring workers to pay a union as a condition of employment forces them to fund an organization and often a political agenda they may oppose. First-contract arbitration would let a government-appointed arbitrator impose wages and work rules that neither the employer nor the workers voted for, binding even workers who opposed the union. Unions have shrunk largely because the economy changed and many workers do not see value in them, and labor law should not be rewritten to reverse that by compulsion.

Question: Should drivers and couriers who work through apps be legally treated as employees?
Tag: value
Poles: Make them employees / Keep them independent
Why contested: App workers combine features of both categories. The app sets the price, assigns the work and can deactivate them, which looks like employment; but they choose when and whether to work, often for several apps at once, which looks like independent business. Employment status carries minimum wage, overtime, unemployment insurance, workers’ compensation and the right to unionize under federal law, and also costs that platforms say would end the flexibility many workers value. Whether a new third category would combine the best of both or the worst is part of the dispute.
Case for make them employees: Platforms control the essential terms of the work: they set the fares, decide who gets which job, monitor performance through algorithms and can cut a worker off without explanation. That is the definition of an employment relationship, and calling these workers contractors shifts the costs of vehicles, insurance, idle time and injury onto them while denying them a minimum wage. The European Commission estimated that 5.5 million platform workers in the EU might be misclassified and that more than half earned less than the hourly minimum wage of their country. Employment status does not require rigid schedules; employers can and do offer flexible shifts. The EU Platform Work Directive and the California ABC test start from the presumption that someone who is controlled by a platform is its employee.
Case for keep them independent: Most people who work through apps do it part time, around other jobs, school or family, and value the ability to log on and off at will. In the BLS survey, 80.3 percent of independent contractors preferred their arrangement. Reclassification would force platforms to schedule shifts, cap the number of workers and limit multi-apping, as happened when the delivery platform Deliveroo left Spain after the country’s 2021 rider law, shrinking the opportunities it was meant to improve. California voters approved Proposition 22 in 2020 to keep drivers independent with an earnings floor and benefits, and Massachusetts and California have since given drivers bargaining rights without employee status. Better protections can be attached to independent work directly through portable benefits and minimum earnings standards.

Question: Who should set the minimum wage: Congress for the whole country, or states, cities and bargaining?
Tag: value
Poles: One national floor / Local and sector floors
Why contested: Living costs and typical wages vary widely across the United States, so a federal minimum that is modest in Boston is high in rural Alabama. The disagreement is whether a national floor is a basic guarantee owed to every worker or a blunt instrument that should defer to local conditions.
Case for one national floor: A national minimum is a statement that no full-time worker anywhere should earn poverty pay, and the workers left behind by state-by-state policy are concentrated in Southern states whose legislatures have never set their own floor and where the federal $7.25 is still the law. Leaving the floor to states lets employers play states against each other. Prices for many goods are national, and the minimum has lost more than a third of its purchasing power since 2009 because Congress has not acted. Indexing a national floor to median wages, as the Raise the Wage Act proposes, would keep it from eroding again and could be set below the bite that evidence suggests causes harm.
Case for local and sector floors: Thirty states and the District of Columbia have already set their own minimums, many above $15 and indexed to inflation, which shows that local democracy works. A national figure high enough to matter in expensive cities would bite hardest in low-wage regions, where the evidence of job loss is strongest. Some voters have chosen not to raise the floor, rejecting a $15 wage in Oklahoma in June 2026 and an $18 wage in California in 2024, and federal law should respect that. Where floors are needed above the general minimum, sector-specific boards, as in California’s fast-food council, or bargaining can set them with better knowledge of each industry.

Question: Do unions on balance make the economy stronger or weaker?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Unions strengthen economy / Unions weaken economy
Why contested: Unions raise members’ wages and benefits and narrow pay gaps, and almost everyone accepts that. The dispute is over whether those gains come from higher productivity and better management or from a transfer out of profits, investment, consumers and nonunion workers, and whether unionized firms and regions grow more slowly as a result. Evidence is mixed across countries, industries and decades.
Case for unions strengthen economy: Unions give workers a voice that reduces turnover, improves training and safety, and surfaces problems management would miss, effects that can raise productivity. They compress wage inequality and built the broad middle class of the postwar decades, and countries with strong bargaining systems, such as the Nordic countries, combine high productivity with low inequality and high employment. Higher wages sustain consumer demand. Unions also act as a civic institution, a check on corporate political power and a source of training and solidarity for working people outside the workplace.
Case for unions weaken economy: The union wage premium is partly a cost that firms recover by investing less, hiring fewer people or raising prices, and unionized firms in the US have historically grown more slowly and been more likely to fail. Rigid work rules and seniority systems can block new technology and penalize the best workers. The decline of the Big Three automakers, the steel industry and other heavily unionized sectors shows what happens when union contracts outrun competitiveness. Public-sector unions bargain with officials they help elect, raising the cost of government. Nordic success rests on a specific consensual model and generous welfare states, not on adversarial American-style unions.

Question: Should worker voice come mainly through independent unions or through cooperative bodies such as works councils and board seats?
Tag: value
Poles: Independent unions / Cooperative councils
Why contested: US law bans employer-created worker committees in order to prevent company-controlled unions, so the main legal route to collective voice is an adversarial union that wins a majority vote. Germany and other European countries combine unions with elected works councils and worker seats on company boards. Whether to open that path in the United States divides people who otherwise agree that workers need more voice.
Case for independent unions: Only an organization that workers control and finance, and that can strike, has real bargaining power. Works councils without strong unions behind them become talking shops that management can ignore or capture; the ban on company-dominated unions exists because employers in the 1920s and 1930s used them to block real organizing. In Germany works councils work because sectoral unions set wages and law backs the councils with real rights. Proposals to legalize cooperative committees in the United States without strengthening unions would, on this view, give employers a tool to preempt organizing.
Case for cooperative councils: Most American workers say they want more say at work, but many do not want an adversarial union or its politics. Works councils and worker board representation give voice on scheduling, safety, technology and training without the all-or-nothing fight of a union election, and evidence from Europe suggests they raise trust and job satisfaction. American Compass argues that reforming the employer-committee ban and allowing worker board seats would reach the many workers the union model no longer serves, and that unions themselves would benefit from a less hostile relationship with employers.

Question: Is a tight labor market a better route to higher pay than stronger unions and wage floors?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Tight labor markets / Stronger institutions
Why contested: Both sides want higher pay for low-wage workers. One emphasizes macroeconomic conditions and labor supply, low unemployment and, for some, lower immigration; the other emphasizes rules that give workers bargaining power in any economy. The 2021 to 2023 period, when low-wage pay rose fastest amid labor shortages, is read as evidence for both.
Case for tight labor markets: When employers compete for scarce workers, pay rises at the bottom without any new law. After the pandemic, wages for the lowest-paid workers grew faster than for others and reversed nearly 40 percent of four decades of growth in wage inequality, as workers quit for better-paying jobs. Full employment policy at the Federal Reserve, and in the view of some conservatives tighter enforcement against illegal hiring and less low-skill immigration, deliver raises that unions and minimum wage laws cannot match, without the risk of pricing workers out of jobs.
Case for stronger institutions: Tight markets come and go: the post-pandemic compression came after four decades of stagnation and depended on an unusually hot labor market that has since cooled. Institutions such as unions and indexed minimum wages lock in gains so that workers do not lose them in the next downturn. Bargaining also covers what labor shortages do not, including safety, scheduling, pensions and protection from arbitrary firing. Supporters of this view also argue that restricting immigration to tighten labor markets harms immigrant workers and that research on immigration’s effect on native wages finds small effects.

## Positions

Order note: the first two camps are the long-standing poles of the US debate and are the most widely held; the third is a hybrid that has gained weight since 2025, as Republican senators and the Teamsters pushed a first-contract bill through the House; the fourth is narrower, centered on app-based work, and rejects the question as usually posed, denying that platform workers must be either employees or ordinary contractors. A further strand that wants European-style sectoral bargaining or wage boards differs from camp 1 mainly in instrument and is noted inside it; a strand that prefers raising low-wage incomes through tax credits rather than wage mandates is noted inside camp 2.

Camp: Strengthen unions and raise the wage floor
Adherents: American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees (AFSCME), “The PRO Act will loosen billionaires’ grip on our economic future” (10 March 2025), https://afscme.org/blog/the-pro-act-will-loosen-billionaires-grip-on-our-economic-future/ (President Lee Saunders arguing the bill would “level the playing field, giving workers the legal protections they need to organize without fear of retaliation or obstruction”); Senator Bernie Sanders, press release on the reintroduction of the PRO Act (5 March 2025), https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-scott-schumer-jeffries-murray-bipartisan-colleagues-introduce-legislation-to-protect-the-rights-of-american-workers/ (the bill would penalize employers who violate workers’ rights, close loopholes that let employers misclassify workers as supervisors or independent contractors, allow unions to collect fair-share fees and speed first contracts). Also: the AFL-CIO and its president Liz Shuler; the Service Employees International Union; the Economic Policy Institute; Representative Bobby Scott, cosponsor with Sanders of the Raise the Wage Act of 2025 for a $17 federal minimum by 2030; in Europe, the European Trade Union Confederation, which backed the Platform Work Directive.
Case: Pay stopped tracking productivity around 1980 because workers lost the power to claim their share, not because they stopped producing. Employers gained the upper hand through hostile labor law, weak penalties, the spread of right-to-work laws, globalization and a minimum wage Congress has left frozen since 2009. The fix is to restore that power directly. Workers should be able to form a union when a majority want one, without captive-audience meetings or years of stalling before a first contract; employers who fire organizers should face real penalties; unions should be able to collect fees from everyone they are legally required to represent. The minimum wage should rise to a living level and be indexed so it never erodes again. And companies should not be able to escape all of this by labeling the people who do their core work as independent contractors: a driver whose fares, assignments and deactivation are set by an app is an employee. Unions built the American middle class, and the record popularity of unions shows that Americans know it.
Best evidence: Typical worker pay rose 33.0 percent from 1979 to 2025 while net productivity rose 90.2 percent (Economic Policy Institute). The labor share of US income fell to 50.9 percent in 2025, its lowest since 1935 (BEA via FRED). Union members earned a median $1,404 a week in 2025 against $1,174 for nonunion workers (BLS), and adjusted estimates put the union wage premium at 10 to 20 percent over most of the last 50 years (Blanchflower and Bryson). Gallup found 71 percent approval of unions and a record 47 percent wanting unions to have more influence in August 2026. Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer found that state minimum wage increases left the number of low-wage jobs essentially unchanged, and the post-pandemic labor market showed that raising pay at the bottom need not cost jobs.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that stronger unions and higher minimums price workers out of jobs and drive investment away. The camp answers that the best modern research finds small or no employment effects from moderate minimum wage increases and that the scary forecasts made before past increases did not come true; the Congressional Budget Office’s own median estimate for a $15 minimum was far below its headline average, and it also found 17 million raises and 900,000 fewer people in poverty. Countries with high union coverage, such as Sweden, Denmark and the Netherlands, are among the richest and most productive in the world. And when workers have no power, the alternative to a union is not a free market between equals but monopsony, in which employers set pay below what workers add.
Variation inside the camp: Mainstream unions and Democrats center on the PRO Act and a higher federal minimum. A more ambitious wing, including many labor scholars and some state officials, argues that firm-by-firm bargaining can never reach most workers and wants sectoral bargaining or wage boards that set minimum pay and standards for whole industries, as California’s fast-food council does and as most of continental Europe does through extended agreements. On platform work, most of the camp wants employee status through the ABC test, but SEIU accepted California’s AB 1340, which gives drivers bargaining rights while leaving them classified as contractors, as a step toward the same goal.

Camp: Leave pay and work arrangements to the market
Adherents: US Chamber of Commerce, letter opposing the PRO Act, H.R. 20 (8 March 2023), https://www.uschamber.com/employment-law/unions/u-s-chamber-letter (opposing card-check recognition that could override a secret-ballot vote, the repeal of state right-to-work laws, and codifying “the restrictive definition of independent contractors as defined in California’s deeply flawed statute known as AB 5”); Club for Growth, “Key Vote Alert: House & Senate No on the Faster Labor Contracts Act” (3 June 2026), https://www.clubforgrowth.org/key-vote-alert-house-senate-no-on-the-faster-labor-contracts-act/ (calling the bill “Big Labor policy wrapped in populist packaging” and arguing that imposed first contracts would mean “fewer job opportunities, slower wage growth, reduced hours”). Also: the Cato Institute, whose economist Ryan Bourne argued in April 2026 that high minimum wages “still reduce new hiring and shut off entry-level jobs” (https://www.cato.org/commentary/when-minimum-wage-rises-young-economy-pay-price); the National Right to Work Committee and Legal Defense Foundation; the Employment Policies Institute; the Institute for the American Worker; the Coalition for Workforce Innovation, which supports the Labor Department’s 2026 proposal to loosen the independent contractor test.
Case: Wages rise when workers become more productive and when employers compete for them, and the best thing government can do for workers is to keep that competition strong. Mandates that push pay above what a worker’s output can support do not create value; they destroy the jobs of the least experienced and least skilled, who lose the first rung on the ladder where they would learn the skills that lead to higher pay. Compulsory unionism, card check and government-imposed contracts take decisions away from individual workers, many of whom do not want a union, and hand them to organizations with their own political agendas. Flexible independent work through apps is a genuine innovation that tens of millions of people choose because it lets them earn on their own schedule, and forcing it into the employee mold would destroy that flexibility. The declining share of workers in unions reflects choices workers have made in a changing economy, not a conspiracy by employers.
Best evidence: Neumark and Shirley’s survey found that 79.2 percent of preferred estimates in 70 US minimum wage studies show negative employment effects, strongest for teenagers and the less educated. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that a $15 federal minimum would cost an average of 1.4 million jobs, with a one-in-three chance of losses between 1.0 and 2.7 million. In the BLS survey, 80.3 percent of independent contractors preferred their arrangement to a traditional job, and only 8.3 percent wanted employee status. Stansbury and Summers found that productivity growth still translates into pay growth for typical workers. Voters in Oklahoma rejected a $15 minimum wage by about 55 to 45 percent in June 2026, and Californians rejected an $18 minimum in 2024 and kept app drivers independent with Proposition 22 in 2020.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that without unions or a higher minimum, employers with market power hold wages down and the gains of growth go to shareholders. The camp answers that the remedy for employer power is more competition, not more regulation: removing occupational licensing barriers, noncompete clauses and zoning rules that keep workers from moving to high-wage cities, and keeping labor markets tight. The low-wage pay surge of 2021 to 2023 came from employers competing for workers, not from new labor law. Where low earnings are a problem, the earned income tax credit raises incomes without the job losses a wage mandate causes and spreads the cost across all taxpayers rather than loading it on the small businesses that hire entry-level workers.
Variation inside the camp: Libertarian economists and groups such as Cato oppose minimum wage laws in principle; mainstream business groups such as the Chamber of Commerce accept a minimum wage but oppose large federal increases and new union rules. A reform-minded wing, including the American Enterprise Institute economist Michael Strain, supports expanding the earned income tax credit and wage subsidies as the better tool for raising low-wage incomes. On platform work, some in the camp support voluntary portable benefits laws, such as Senator Bill Cassidy’s Unlocking Benefits for Independent Workers Act of 2025, which would let companies contribute to benefits for contractors without that being treated as evidence of employment.

Camp: Rebuild worker power through cooperation and tighter labor markets
Adherents: American Compass, Chris Griswold, “A Better Bargain: Worker Voice and Representation” (8 September 2021), https://americancompass.org/a-better-bargain-worker-voice-and-representation/ (proposing to reform section 8(a)(2) of the National Labor Relations Act to legalize nonunion works councils and to give workers elected seats on corporate boards); Senator Josh Hawley, statement on House passage of the Faster Labor Contracts Act (9 June 2026), https://www.hawley.senate.gov/hawley-applauds-house-passage-of-faster-labor-contracts-act/ (“We need real labor reform that puts workers first,” urging the Senate to pass his bill requiring bargaining within 10 days of a union win and binding arbitration for stalled first contracts; Hawley is also cosponsor, with Democrat Peter Welch, of a $15 federal minimum wage). Also: Oren Cass, founder of American Compass; Republican Senators Bernie Moreno and Roger Marshall, cosponsors of the Faster Labor Contracts Act; the International Brotherhood of Teamsters and its president Sean O’Brien, the bill’s leading union backer.
Case: Conservatives were wrong to treat organized labor as an enemy and the market wage as sacred. A healthy society needs strong mediating institutions, and for working families the most important one is a voice at work. Decades of free trade, offshoring, mass low-skill immigration and a financialized model of the corporation shifted bargaining power from workers to capital, and the remedy is to shift it back. That means fixing the parts of labor law that clearly fail, such as employers who stall first contracts for more than a year, while building new, less adversarial forms of representation that more workers actually want: works councils, worker board seats and labor organizations that deliver training and benefits rather than partisan politics. It also means tightening labor markets, through tariffs that bring production home and strict enforcement against illegal hiring, so that employers must compete for workers. The left’s PRO Act is the wrong vehicle because it would override state right-to-work laws and the secret ballot and bind unions even more tightly to one party.
Best evidence: The House passed the Faster Labor Contracts Act 230 to 193 in June 2026 with bipartisan support, the first serious prospect of amending the National Labor Relations Act in more than 50 years. Newly organized workers waited a mean of 465 days for a first contract in Bloomberg Law’s sample, which is the specific failure the bill targets. Gallup’s 2026 poll found that 52 percent of Republicans approve of unions. American Compass cites survey evidence that 50 to 60 percent of American workers have less influence than they want over workplace issues other than pay, and European evidence that works councils raise trust and job satisfaction. Autor, Dube and McGrew found that the tight labor market of 2021 to 2023 raised the lowest wages fastest and reversed nearly 40 percent of four decades of growing wage inequality.
Answer to the strongest objection: Critics on the left say the camp’s labor agenda is thin and contradicts the Republican Party’s actual record, including the Trump administration’s removal of a Democratic labor board member and its new NLRB majority, while critics on the right say it adopts Big Labor’s agenda. The camp answers that it is building a new coalition, not ratifying either party’s record: the Faster Labor Contracts Act passed the House with votes from both parties, and the Teamsters chose to work with Republicans on it. Works councils would not replace unions but reach the many workers unions no longer serve. And on immigration and trade, the camp argues that tightening the supply of labor raises wages at the bottom in a way no labor law can, as the post-pandemic labor shortage showed.
Variation inside the camp: Some, led by Hawley and the Teamsters, emphasize strengthening traditional union bargaining through faster contracts and bans on captive-audience meetings, close to parts of the PRO Act. Others, led by American Compass, emphasize cooperative alternatives to the union model and are cautious about sectoral bargaining. The camp’s positions on immigration and tariffs are not shared by its union allies, many of whom organize immigrant workers.

Camp: Give app workers a middle status with portable benefits
Adherents: Flex, the trade association of app-based companies including DoorDash, Grubhub, Instacart, Lyft, Shipt and Uber, “Flex Announces Principles for Federal Legislation to Provide Portable Benefits for App-Based Workers” (28 June 2023), https://www.flexassociation.org/flex-announces-principles-for-federal-legislation-to-provide-portable-benefits-for-app-based-workers/ (proposing benefits accounts funded by platform contributions in proportion to earnings, portable across apps, together with a national test that keeps app workers independent contractors); Seth Harris and Alan Krueger, “A Proposal for Modernizing Labor Laws for Twenty-First-Century Work: The ‘Independent Worker’” (Hamilton Project, 7 December 2015), https://www.hamiltonproject.org/publication/policy-proposal/a-proposal-for-modernizing-labor-laws-for-twenty-first-century-work-the-independent-worker/ (proposing a new legal category with the right to organize and bargain, civil rights protections and payroll tax contributions, but not hourly minimum wage or overtime rules). Also: Uber’s chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi, who called in 2020 for a “third way” in which platforms would pay into benefits funds; the backers of California’s Proposition 22; in the United Kingdom, the intermediate “worker” status the Supreme Court applied to Uber drivers in Uber v Aslam (2021).
Case: The employee versus contractor choice is a false one for app work, and forcing every worker into one box or the other serves neither. Employee status would end the defining feature of the work, the freedom to log on whenever and for as long as one likes, often across several apps at once, which is what most of these workers value most. But pure contractor status leaves them without a safety net. The answer is a third path written for how this work actually happens: earnings floors tied to time spent on a job, benefits funds that platforms pay into in proportion to each worker’s earnings and that follow the worker from app to app, occupational accident insurance, and, in some versions, the right to bargain as a sector. This preserves the opportunity that has drawn tens of millions of people while giving them protections the old law never offered contractors.
Best evidence: In the BLS survey, 80.3 percent of independent contractors preferred their arrangement. Pew found that for most people who earn money through gig apps it is not their main job: 9 percent of US adults had done gig work in the past year, but only 3 percent said it was their main job. California voters approved Proposition 22 in 2020, and California and Massachusetts have since created sectoral bargaining for drivers without reclassifying them, with California’s Public Employment Relations Board certifying a statewide drivers’ union in September 2026. Utah, Pennsylvania and other states have enacted or tested portable benefits arrangements, and the UK’s intermediate worker status shows a third category can work in law.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a third status is a way for platforms to escape the costs of employment while keeping control over the work, and that earnings floors measured only while a worker is on a trip leave idle time unpaid. The camp answers that employee status would not deliver what its advocates promise, because platforms would schedule shifts, cap the number of workers and cut those who earn least, as happened when Deliveroo left Spain after its 2021 rider law. Benefits tied to earnings can be designed to cover waiting time, and the new bargaining laws in California and Massachusetts let drivers negotiate exactly these terms. The choice is not between a perfect employment relationship and exploitation but between workable protections now and litigation that has dragged on for a decade.
Variation inside the camp: Platform companies favor mandated earnings floors and benefits stipends in some states, as in Proposition 22, but prefer voluntary contributions at the federal level and a national classification test that settles their status; Harris and Krueger’s version gives independent workers bargaining rights but no hourly minimum. California’s AB 1340, backed by SEIU and accepted by Uber and Lyft as part of a 2025 legislative deal, gives drivers sectoral bargaining while keeping them contractors, and so sits between this camp and camp 1.

## Crux

The question under the others: when a worker and an employer or platform bargain over pay and conditions, does the employer usually hold enough power to set terms below what a competitive market would pay, and if so, should the remedy be collective power and legal floors, or more competition and freedom of choice for each worker?

What evidence could settle: How much wage-setting power employers actually have in typical US labor markets, which researchers estimate from how much workers’ pay responds when a firm’s demand changes and from how easily workers quit for better jobs. How large the job losses from a given minimum wage are at different ratios to the local median, especially above 60 percent, where evidence is thinnest. How much of the gap between productivity and typical pay remains once prices, benefits and the spread of pay among workers are measured consistently. What happens to earnings, hours and flexibility when app workers gain employee status, an earnings floor or sectoral bargaining, which the EU directive, California’s and Massachusetts’ new driver unions and the UK’s worker status are about to test. And whether unions and works councils raise or lower productivity, investment and employment in today’s service economy rather than the factories of the past.

What no data can close: Whether the decision to join a union, or the protections that come with a job, should be made collectively by a majority of workers or individually by each one, and how to weigh one worker’s freedom not to join or pay against the bargaining power of many. How much risk of lost jobs for the least experienced is worth accepting in exchange for higher pay for the many who keep them. Whether flexibility or security is the more important thing work should offer, a choice that app workers themselves make differently. And whether the right level for decisions about wage floors and labor rules is the nation, the state or the industry.
