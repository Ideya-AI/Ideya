# Antimicrobial Resistance — research

## Overview

Antimicrobial resistance is the evolved ability of bacteria, fungi, viruses and parasites to survive the drugs meant to kill them. This topic covers drug-resistant infections, above all bacterial ones including drug-resistant tuberculosis, and the prospect its most worried observers call a “post-antibiotic era,” in which common infections, childbirth and routine surgery become dangerous again. It covers how large the threat is and how fast it is growing; how antibiotics are used in human medicine and in livestock; how new antibiotics get invented and paid for when the market rewards them poorly; and who in the world can get the antibiotics that already exist. It deliberately leaves aside resistance in HIV, influenza and malaria drugs except where a cited figure includes it. Gain-of-function research, drug pricing in general and vaccine mandates have their own topics and appear here only where an argument depends on them, as when vaccines are offered as a way to cut antibiotic use.

Why now: on 23 May 2026 the Seventy-ninth World Health Assembly adopted an updated Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance for 2026–2036, replacing the plan adopted in 2015 (WHO, 25 May 2026, https://www.who.int/news/item/25-05-2026-the-world-health-assembly-adopts-updated-global-action-plan-on-antimicrobial-resistance-(2026-2036)). The plan’s stated aims are a 10% reduction in bacterial AMR-associated human deaths, a reduction in antimicrobial use in agrifood systems, and less environmental pollution by resistant microbes and drug residues by 2030; the same document reports that only 10% of countries had dedicated domestic funding for their national AMR action plans in 2024 (WHA79 document A79/5 Add.2, https://apps.who.int/gb/ebwha/pdf_files/WHA79/A79_5Add2-en.pdf). The year around it has been busy on every front of the debate. In the United States, the PASTEUR Act, which would have the federal government pay fixed annual subscriptions of $75 million to $300 million for qualifying new antimicrobials, was reintroduced in the House on 4 February 2026 (H.R. 7352) and in the Senate on 23 June 2026 (S. 4875), its fourth Congress without a floor vote (Senator Bennet, 24 June 2026, https://www.bennet.senate.gov/2026/06/24/bennet-young-colleagues-reintroduce-bipartisan-pasteur-act-to-fight-antimicrobial-resistance/). In February 2026 the US Food and Drug Administration finalized guidance asking drug sponsors to set voluntary duration limits on medically important antibiotics fed to livestock, two months after reporting that US sales of those drugs for food animals rose 16% in 2024 (FDA, 5 December 2025, https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/cvm-updates/fda-releases-annual-summary-sales-and-distribution-antimicrobials-2024-use-food-producing-animals). The Pan American Health Organization adopted a regional AMR plan for 2027–2031 on 30 September 2026 (https://www.paho.org/en/news/30-9-2026-antimicrobial-resistance-americas-adopt-new-regional-action-plan), and the Fifth Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on AMR is scheduled for Abuja, Nigeria, on 6–8 December 2026 (conference site, https://5thghlamr.health.gov.ng/).

Almost no one in this debate denies that resistance is real, growing and dangerous; the World Health Organization’s surveillance system reported that about one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide was resistant to antibiotics in 2023. The live disagreements are about how big the coming toll will be, which lever matters most, and who bears the cost. Public health and environmental groups want routine antibiotic use in healthy farm animals ended, while livestock producers and animal-drug makers want those decisions left to veterinarians. Infectious disease physicians, biotech investors and a bipartisan group in Congress want governments to guarantee payments for new antibiotics, while Médecins Sans Frontières and allied groups argue public and nonprofit development would serve patients better. Researchers and nonprofits focused on low- and middle-income countries argue that, in much of the world, lack of access to antibiotics kills more people than resistance does, and that conservation policies written for rich countries can make that worse.

Data year: 2021
Source label: GBD 2021 Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, “Global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance 1990–2021: a systematic analysis with forecasts to 2050,” The Lancet 404:1199–1226, 2024 (abstract on PubMed)
Source URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39299261/
Note: In 2021 an estimated 1.14 million deaths were directly attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance, and 4.71 million deaths were associated with it.
Context: The two numbers answer different questions. “Attributable” deaths are those that would not have happened if the resistant infection had been a drug-susceptible one; “associated” deaths are those in which a resistant infection was involved, compared with no infection at all. The 95% uncertainty range is 1.00 to 1.28 million attributable deaths and 4.23 to 5.19 million associated. The same research group’s earlier estimate for 2019 was 1.27 million attributable and 4.95 million associated deaths (Murray et al., The Lancet, 2022); the 2019 figure is the baseline the United Nations used for its target, and part of the gap between the two years reflects a drop in non-COVID infectious deaths in 2020 and 2021 that the 2024 paper notes. Trends differ sharply by age: from 1990 to 2021 AMR deaths among children under five fell by more than half, while deaths among adults 70 and older rose by more than 80%. Among individual pathogen-drug pairs, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus grew the most, to 130,000 attributable deaths in 2021, and carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria rose to 216,000. These are modeled estimates covering 204 countries, built from 520 million records, and many low-income countries contribute little direct data.

Note: The reference forecast is 1.91 million deaths attributable to bacterial AMR and 8.22 million associated with it in the year 2050.
Context: Across 2025 to 2050 the forecast adds up to 39 million attributable deaths and about 169 million associated deaths (Wellcome, 16 September 2024, https://wellcome.org/insights/articles/new-forecasts-reveal-39-million-deaths-will-be-directly-attributable-bacterial-antimicrobial). The rise is driven mostly by aging: people 70 and older are forecast to account for 65.9% of attributable deaths in 2050, which is why disability-adjusted life-years rise only 9.4% while deaths rise 69.6% between 2022 and 2050. The study also ran two alternative scenarios. A “better care” scenario, with improved treatment of severe infections and better access to antibiotics, would avert 92.0 million deaths cumulatively from 2025 to 2050; a scenario in which a new pipeline of drugs against Gram-negative bacteria arrives would avert 11.1 million AMR deaths. The two scenarios are not built on identical baselines, but both camps that favor health-system investment and camps that favor new drugs cite them. A forecast 25 years out depends on assumptions about health care quality, drug development and population aging that may not hold.

Context: The rows do not share a basis. In 2016 the epidemiologists Marlieke de Kraker, Andrew Stewardson and Stephan Harbarth argued in PLOS Medicine that the 10 million figure rested on weak data and that better burden estimates were needed (https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1002184). The 10 million figure is still widely quoted; those who think the threat is overstated point to the far lower attributable estimate, while those who think it is understated point out that the associated figure for 2050 is close to 10 million.

Note: Estimated global human antibiotic consumption was 49.3 billion defined daily doses in 2023, a 20.9% increase since 2016, and the authors project 75.1 billion by 2030 without policy change.
Context: In the 67 countries with reported sales data, consumption rose 16.3%, from 29.5 to 34.3 billion doses, and the rate per person rose 10.6%, from 13.7 to 15.2 doses per 1,000 people per day. ## Questions

Question: How many people will drug-resistant infections kill in the coming decades?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Looming catastrophe / Serious, manageable burden
Why contested: The two best-known numbers differ by a factor of five. The 2016 O’Neill review projected 10 million deaths a year by 2050; the 2024 GBD forecast is 1.91 million deaths attributable to resistance in 2050 and 8.22 million associated with it. Data are thinnest exactly where the burden is thought to be highest, and every forecast depends on assumptions about aging, health care and new drugs.
Case for “Looming catastrophe”: Resistance is rising in more than 40% of the pathogen-drug combinations WHO monitors, by 5–15% a year, and the drugs of last resort are failing in the bacteria that cause the most bloodstream infections. The GBD forecast already implies 39 million attributable deaths between 2025 and 2050, and its associated-death figure for 2050 is close to O’Neill’s 10 million. Forecasts built on past trends cannot capture a tipping point: if carbapenems and colistin fail broadly, as they have for some patients already, the protection antibiotics give to cancer chemotherapy, cesarean sections, joint replacements and organ transplants would erode with them, multiplying deaths that are now counted under other causes.
Case for “Serious, manageable burden”: The best current estimate of deaths caused by resistance, as distinct from deaths in which a resistant bug was present, is about 1.1 million a year, and it fell between the 2019 and 2021 estimates. Among children under five, AMR deaths fell by more than half from 1990 to 2021 as vaccines, sanitation and care improved, showing the burden responds to ordinary public health. The forecast rise is driven mostly by an aging population, not by runaway resistance. Apocalyptic numbers, on this view, risk crowding out the less dramatic investments in infection control, water, vaccines and diagnostics that the Lancet’s 2024 series estimates could prevent hundreds of thousands of deaths a year now.

Question: Will guaranteed government payments bring companies back to antibiotic development?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Pull payments revive pipeline / Public development works better
Why contested: Most large drug companies have left antibiotics, and the small firms that remain often fail after approval. England has begun paying subscriptions and the US PASTEUR Act would follow, but no subscription scheme has run long enough to show whether it produces new drugs that would not otherwise exist.
Case for “Pull payments revive pipeline”: New antibiotics should be used as little as possible to slow resistance, which means they cannot earn their development costs through sales. Most small companies that brought an antibacterial to FDA approval since 2010 went bankrupt or exited at a loss. A predictable reward, paid regardless of volume, fixes the specific market failure that keeps investors away, as Jim O’Neill’s review and the Infectious Diseases Society of America argue. Push funding for early research already exists through CARB-X and others; what is missing is a payoff at the end, and only a large, credible pull payment from rich-country governments can supply it.
Case for “Public development works better”: Subscription payments transfer public money to companies without guaranteeing that the drugs reach patients in poor countries or are priced and stewarded in the public interest. Médecins Sans Frontières argues PASTEUR would raise prices worldwide and is an inefficient way to buy innovation. Nonprofit and public developers have already delivered: zoliflodacin, approved in December 2025, came from the nonprofit GARDP. Public development lets governments set access and stewardship terms from the start rather than negotiating them afterward with a company whose obligation is to its shareholders.

Question: How much does antibiotic use in farm animals drive resistant infections in people?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Major human health driver / Minor, separate reservoir
Why contested: Animals receive more antimicrobials than humans by weight, and resistance genes move between bacteria. But tracing a particular human infection back to a farm is hard, and genomic studies have reached different answers in different places.
Case for “Major human health driver”: Animal consumption of antimicrobials is roughly threefold that of humans, according to a 2019 study in Science. When China banned colistin as a feed additive, colistin-resistant E. coli fell from 34% to 5% in pig samples and human carriage of the resistance gene fell from 14.3% to 6.3%. A 2025 genomic study led by Lance Price estimated that strains of E. coli from food animals caused 18% of urinary tract infections in Southern California. A WHO-funded 2017 review found that restricting farm antibiotic use was associated with lower resistance in animals and a 24% lower prevalence of resistance in the human groups studied.
Case for “Minor, separate reservoir”: Most resistance in human infections comes from human antibiotic use, in hospitals and clinics. A 2019 genomic study in the East of England found that E. coli from livestock and from patients with bloodstream infections were genetically distinct populations, with limited sharing of the mobile elements that carry resistance genes. The 2017 review that found an association in humans also noted that implications for the general population were unclear because of the small number of studies, and that the effect was strongest among people with direct contact with farm animals. Only 8% of antimicrobials used in animals in 2022 were in the highest-priority classes for human medicine, by WOAH’s count.

Question: Where antibiotics are scarce, should widening access come before restricting use?
Tag: value
Poles: Access first / Stewardship first
Why contested: The same pills that save a child with pneumonia in a clinic without a laboratory also breed resistance when sold without prescription. Rich countries built their stewardship rules after decades of easy access, and low- and middle-income countries are now asked to do both at once.
Case for “Access first”: Lack of access to effective antibiotics kills more people than resistance does, a point the 2024 UN political declaration itself makes. In eight low- and middle-income countries, only 6.9% of carbapenem-resistant infections were matched by suitable treatment courses procured. Ramanan Laxminarayan and GARDP argue that restrictive policies designed for over-prescribing countries can starve under-served places of drugs, and that the answer is to get the right drug to the right patient, including new drugs, while preventing infections in the first place.
Case for “Stewardship first”: Expanding access without controls repeats the history that produced resistance in the first place; consumption in middle-income countries rose 18.6% from 2016 to 2023, and Watch antibiotics, the broader-spectrum class WHO wants used sparingly, exceed 70% of use in nearly a third of countries. Every unnecessary course raises the odds that the drugs will fail for the next patient, including the poor patient the access argument aims to help. On this view, access programs must be built with prescription controls, diagnostics and Access-group drugs from day one, or they will burn through the few antibiotics that still work.

Question: If antibiotic use falls, will resistance fall with it?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Reversible with restraint / Locked in once established
Why contested: Resistance often costs bacteria some fitness, which predicts it should fade when the drug is withdrawn. But bacteria evolve compensating mutations, and resistance genes travel together, so using one drug can keep resistance to another alive.
Case for “Reversible with restraint”: After China withdrew colistin from animal feed, colistin resistance fell sharply in pigs, in chickens and in human carriers within about three years. Countries with low antibiotic use, such as the Nordic countries, have long had low resistance, which advocates read as evidence that restraint keeps rates down. If restraint works, conservation is the cheapest and most durable tool.
Case for “Locked in once established”: In Kronoberg County, Sweden, a 24-month voluntary restriction cut trimethoprim use by 85%, yet resistance in E. coli changed only marginally; the researchers found no measurable fitness cost and strong co-selection with other drugs, and concluded that reversing resistance once established is unlikely and new drugs are urgently needed (Sundqvist et al., 2010). If resistance mostly persists, restraint can slow its growth but cannot recover lost drugs, and the world needs a steady supply of new ones.

Question: Should a doctor treating today’s patient give weight to the risk of resistance for future patients?
Tag: value
Poles: Patient in front first / Future patients count
Why contested: Each prescription helps or protects one person now and adds slightly to a collective risk later. Medical ethics is built around the individual patient, while resistance is a shared, slow-moving harm that no single prescription causes.
Case for “Patient in front first”: A clinician’s duty is to the person in front of them, and withholding or delaying an antibiotic for a sick patient to protect an abstract future is a harm with a face. Underuse also kills: sepsis can turn fatal within hours, and WHO’s and GARDP’s figures show that undertreatment is common. Stewardship should come from better diagnostics, narrower drugs and shorter courses, not from asking doctors to ration.
Case for “Future patients count”: Antibiotics are a shared resource, like a fishery, and every unnecessary course depletes it for everyone. CDC estimates at least 28% of US outpatient antibiotic prescriptions are unnecessary, and those prescriptions buy little benefit for the patient while adding to a risk that falls on others. Professional norms already accept limits on individual choice for collective goods, as with vaccination and infection control, and antibiotic prescribing should be treated the same way.

Question: Should reductions in farm antibiotic use be binding targets, or left to national and veterinary judgment?
Tag: value
Poles: Binding reduction targets / National and veterinary discretion
Why contested: A draft of the 2024 UN declaration proposed cutting antimicrobial use in agrifood systems by at least 30% by 2030; the final text committed only to “meaningfully reduce” it. The European Union bans routine farm use by law, while the United States relies largely on voluntary guidance and veterinary oversight.
Case for “Binding reduction targets”: Voluntary approaches have stalled; US sales of medically important antibiotics for food animals rose 16% in 2024 and 22% of countries surveyed by WOAH still use antimicrobials as growth promoters. Advocates such as the Alliance to Save Our Antibiotics point to the EU’s legal ban on routine preventive use as proof that intensive farming can adapt. Haileyesus Getahun, chief executive of the South-South HeDPAC health partnership, said the animal food industry was able to “influence some member states and even divide” the UN agencies to remove the 30% target (Health Policy Watch, 26 September 2024, https://healthpolicy-watch.news/un-high-level-meeting-approves-milestone-commitment-to-reduce-deaths-from-antibiotic-resistance-10-by-2030/).
Case for “National and veterinary discretion”: Farms, climates, diseases and production systems differ, and a single quantity target can push producers to stop treating animals that need treatment, harming animal welfare and food safety. The Animal Health Institute argues that veterinarians “are in the best position to make decisions about the proper use of antimicrobials,” and that disease prevention through vaccines and diagnostics is the real route to lower use. Where animal health is poor and veterinary services thin, a hard cut could cost farmers their herds before the alternatives arrive.

Question: Can vaccines, phages and other alternatives take the place of new antibiotics?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Alternatives can carry it / New antibiotics still essential
Why contested: Forty of the 90 antibacterial candidates in clinical trials are non-traditional approaches such as bacteriophages, antibodies and microbiome treatments, and vaccines can prevent infections before they need treatment. None of these has yet replaced antibiotics at scale for treating an established infection.
Case for “Alternatives can carry it”: WHO estimates vaccines against 23 pathogens could cut the antibiotics needed for those diseases by 22%, and new tuberculosis and Klebsiella vaccines could avert 543,000 AMR-associated deaths a year. Infection prevention, clean water and vaccines together could prevent hundreds of thousands of AMR deaths in poorer countries with tools that exist today. Phages and antibodies can be targeted at a single pathogen, which avoids the broad selection pressure that drives resistance. Resources spent chasing ever-scarcer antibiotic chemistry might do more good here.
Case for “New antibiotics still essential”: A patient already infected with a pan-resistant organism needs a drug that works now; prevention cannot help them. Phage therapy remains largely experimental and is hard to standardize, and the key vaccines against tuberculosis and Klebsiella do not yet exist. The GBD forecast estimates that a pipeline of new drugs against Gram-negative bacteria alone could avert 11.1 million deaths between 2025 and 2050. Alternatives complement antibiotics but, on this view, do not replace the need to keep inventing them.

## Positions

Camp: Cut antibiotic use in farms and clinics
Adherents: Alliance to Save Our Antibiotics, a UK coalition founded by Compassion in World Farming, the Soil Association and Sustain, which calls for “a ban on all routine use of antibiotics and all preventative use of antibiotics in groups of animals” and a complete ban on colistin in livestock (https://www.saveourantibiotics.org/our-campaign/); Steven Roach, director of the Safe and Healthy Food Program at the Food Animal Concerns Trust, who attributed the 2024 rise in US livestock antibiotic sales to “a failure to put in place common sense steps such as eliminating antibiotic use in animals without diagnosed bacterial infection and failure to set targets for reduction” (CIDRAP, December 2025, https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/antimicrobial-stewardship/us-sales-antibiotics-livestock-climbed-16-last-year-fda-report-shows). Others in this camp include the Natural Resources Defense Council and the Environmental Working Group in the United States, and researchers such as Lance Price of George Washington University.
Case: Resistance is evolution under selection pressure, and the pressure is antibiotic use. Every dose given where it is not needed, whether to a healthy pig to prevent disease in a crowded barn or to a patient with a cold, breeds resistant bacteria that spread through food, water, soil and people. Most of the world’s antimicrobials go to animals, much of that to animals that are not sick, and the solution is to stop it: ban routine and preventive group dosing, ban last-resort human drugs like colistin on farms, set binding reduction targets, and publish use data farm by farm. In human medicine the same logic applies to unnecessary prescriptions, which CDC puts at no less than 28% of US outpatient prescribing. Conserving the drugs we have is cheaper, faster and more certain than waiting for new ones, and any new drug will be lost just as quickly if overuse continues.
Variation: Members differ on emphasis. Farm-focused groups such as the Alliance and FACT concentrate on livestock and husbandry reform, including lower stocking densities and outdoor access; medical stewardship programs concentrate on hospital and clinic prescribing. Some, like the Alliance, also want trade rules to block imported meat produced with growth promoters.
Best evidence: After China banned colistin as a feed additive in 2017, colistin-resistant E. coli fell from 34.0% to 5.1% in pig samples and human hospital carriage of the mcr-1 resistance gene fell from 14.3% to 6.3% (Wang et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases, 2020). A 2025 genomic study led by Lance Price attributed 18% of urinary tract infections in Southern California to E. coli strains from food animals (Aziz et al., mBio, 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41128542/). A WHO-commissioned 2017 meta-analysis found that restricting farm antibiotic use was associated with 10–15% lower absolute prevalence of resistance in animals and 24% lower prevalence in the human groups studied (Tang et al., Lancet Planetary Health, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29387833/). Europe shows the policy is workable: sales of veterinary antibiotics in 25 European countries fell 53% between 2011 and 2022 (European Medicines Agency, https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/consumption-antimicrobials-animals-reaches-lowest-level-ever-europe).
Answer to the strongest objection: Livestock producers argue that cutting preventive use will leave animals sick and that farm use contributes little to human infections. The camp answers that Europe’s large reductions came without collapse in meat production, that the right fix for disease in crowded barns is better husbandry and vaccines rather than permanent medication, and that genomic studies finding little overlap in one region do not rule out the gene transfer and food-borne spread documented elsewhere. Where some uncertainty remains about how much farm use harms people, they argue, the burden of proof should fall on continuing a practice with a plausible and partly demonstrated risk.

Camp: Guarantee payments for new antibiotics
Adherents: Infectious Diseases Society of America, whose president Ronald Nahass said the 2026 PASTEUR Act “addresses many of the root causes behind the lack of antimicrobial development” (IDSA, 4 February 2026, https://www.idsociety.org/news--publications-new/articles/2026/newly-introduced-legislation-provides-pathway-to-spur-antimicrobial-development/); Jim O’Neill, the economist who chaired the UK Review on Antimicrobial Resistance, which called for “market entry rewards” of around $1 billion for each successful new antibiotic, paid on conditions that prevent over-marketing and ensure availability wherever patients live (final report, May 2016, https://amr-review.org/sites/default/files/160525_Final%20paper_with%20cover.pdf). Others include the AMR Action Fund, the Biotechnology Innovation Organization, the PASTEUR Act’s bipartisan sponsors, and NHS England, which runs the first national subscription scheme.
Case: The antibiotic market is broken in a way no other drug market is. Society wants new antibiotics developed and then kept on the shelf, used as little as possible so resistance does not catch up with them. A drug that is supposed to be rarely used cannot recover a development cost of hundreds of millions of dollars through sales, so investors stay away, large companies have left the field, and the small firms that remain go bankrupt even after success. The fix is to pay for the capability rather than the pills: a subscription, set by the drug’s value to public health and paid regardless of volume, which makes it rational to invent the drug and rational to hold it in reserve. This aligns profit with stewardship instead of setting them against each other. Prevention and stewardship matter, but bacteria will keep evolving, and only new drugs can treat the patient whose infection already resists everything.
Variation: Supporters differ on design. Some favor annual subscriptions (England, PASTEUR), others one-time market entry rewards (the O’Neill review), and others transferable exclusivity vouchers; some insist on strict access and stewardship conditions, others on maximum certainty for investors. The AMR Action Fund, backed by drug companies, invests directly in small developers while pull incentives are built, aiming to bring two to four new antibiotics to patients by 2030 (https://www.amractionfund.com/).
Best evidence: Most small and medium-sized firms that sponsored an FDA-approved antibacterial since 2010 went bankrupt or exited at a loss, among them Achaogen, which went bankrupt in 2019 the year after plazomicin was approved (Wells, Nguyen & Harbarth, 2024). WHO counted only 90 antibacterials in clinical trials in 2025, down from 97 in 2023, with just 5 that are innovative and active against its critical-priority pathogens, and 90% of preclinical developers are small firms. The GBD forecast estimates that a pipeline of new drugs against Gram-negative bacteria could avert 11.1 million AMR deaths from 2025 to 2050.
Answer to the strongest objection: Critics call subscriptions corporate welfare that raises prices and need not deliver. Supporters answer that the scale is modest next to the cost of resistance, which the World Bank put at up to 3.8% of global GDP by 2050; that payments are tied to scored public-health value and to stewardship and supply obligations, and the 2026 PASTEUR bill reduces payments by a company’s net sales; and that public and nonprofit developers can compete for the same contracts. Without a predictable reward, they argue, the private capital and expertise that produced nearly every antibiotic in use today will not return.

Camp: Close the access gap before restricting use
Adherents: Global Antibiotic Research and Development Partnership, whose executive director Manica Balasegaram said its 2025 study showed “there is now a massive treatment gap and people are dying because of it” (GARDP, 30 April 2025, https://gardp.org/gardp-research-reveals-that-people-with-multidrug-resistant-infections-in-low-and-middle-income-countries-dont-have-access-to-appropriate-treatment/); Ramanan Laxminarayan, economist and founder of the One Health Trust (formerly the Center for Disease Dynamics, Economics & Policy), who said “lack of access to antibiotics kills more people currently than does antibiotic resistance” (CDDEP release via ScienceDaily, 9 April 2019, https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/04/190409135849.htm).
Case: The AMR debate was framed in rich countries, where the problem is too many antibiotics. Across much of Africa and South Asia the bigger killer is too few: children die of pneumonia and newborns of sepsis because the right drug is unaffordable, out of stock or never registered. Policies that only restrict use, such as tighter prescription rules or higher prices to discourage sales, deny treatment to people who need it most. The priority is to prevent infections in the first place with clean water, sanitation, infection control in hospitals and vaccines, and to make sure that when someone is infected, an affordable and appropriate antibiotic, including newer drugs for resistant infections, is actually there. Stewardship and access are not opposites; good stewardship means the right drug for the right patient, which in most of the world means more access, not less.
Variation: This is a hybrid camp. It accepts the stewardship camp’s goal of using antibiotics wisely and the innovation camp’s case for new drugs, but insists both be judged by whether they reach patients in low- and middle-income countries. GARDP develops and licenses drugs for those markets; Laxminarayan and the 2024 Lancet series he coordinated emphasize prevention through infection control, water and vaccines.
Best evidence: In eight low- and middle-income countries in 2019, suitable antibiotics were procured for only 6.9% of an estimated 1.5 million carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative infections. The 2024 UN political declaration states that lack of access to antimicrobials and diagnostics “is responsible for more deaths than antimicrobial resistance.” Lewnard, Laxminarayan and colleagues estimate that infection control, water and sanitation and childhood vaccines could each prevent between about 180,000 and 340,000 AMR-associated deaths a year in those countries, and the GBD better-care scenario averts 92 million deaths from 2025 to 2050. Only 42% of people who developed drug-resistant tuberculosis in 2024 were treated for it.
Answer to the strongest objection: The stewardship camp warns that expanding access will speed resistance. This camp answers that it does not want more antibiotics sold over the counter but more of the right ones delivered through health systems, with diagnostics and with Access-group drugs as the default. It notes that preventing infections cuts antibiotic use and deaths at the same time, and that a resistant infection left untreated or treated with the wrong drug is itself a driver of spread.

Camp: Keep farm antibiotic decisions with veterinarians and farmers
Adherents: Animal Health Institute, the trade group for US animal-drug makers, which says “veterinarians need a wide range of tools to combat animal disease and are in the best position to make decisions about the proper use of antimicrobials” (statement on FDA duration guidance, 13 February 2026, https://ahi.org/statement-from-ahi-on-the-final-guidance-from-fdas-center-for-veterinary-medicine-on-the-duration-of-use-of-antimicrobials/); National Pork Producers Council, which called a WHO-proposed ban on disease-prevention uses of antibiotics in food animals “ill-advised and wrong” (7 November 2017, https://nppc.org/press-releases/nppc-statement-on-who-call-for-ban-on-prevention-uses-of-antibiotics/) and which says it “opposes efforts [to] undermine veterinarians’ ability to care for pigs, including mandated antimicrobial reductions” (https://nppc.org/antimicrobials/).
Case: Antibiotics are medicines for animals too, and animals that get sick suffer, die and can carry food-borne pathogens to people. Responsible producers have already ended growth-promotion use of medically important drugs in the United States and put every medically important antibiotic under veterinary oversight. What remains is treatment, control and prevention of disease decided by a licensed veterinarian who knows the herd. Blanket bans on preventive use, quantity targets or fixed duration caps substitute a regulator’s formula for a clinician’s judgment, and can force a choice between letting animals sicken and breaking the law. The way to reduce use is to reduce disease, through vaccines, diagnostics, biosecurity and better housing, which the industry is investing in. Most resistance in human infections, they argue, comes from human medicine.
Variation: Industry groups mostly oppose new mandates outright. A more academic version of the argument comes from veterinary researchers such as J. Scott Weese of the University of Guelph, who supports stewardship but argues against global mass-reduction targets: “Use as little as possible but use enough,” he writes, noting that “zero AMU in animals is not a realistic goal” and that a country expanding livestock production while following treatment guidelines may rightly use more antimicrobials in total (“Antimicrobial use in animals: when more might be better,” Canadian Veterinary Journal, 2025, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12330789/).
Best evidence: Only 8% of antimicrobials used in animals in 2022 were in the highest-priority critically important classes for human medicine, and global use per kilogram of animal fell 5% from 2020 to 2022 (WOAH). A 2019 genomic study in the East of England found that E. coli from livestock and from patients with bloodstream infections were distinct populations, concluding that, within its sampling, there was limited evidence that resistant pathogens causing serious human infection had originated from livestock (Ludden et al., mBio, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30670621/). The Animal Health Institute reports that between 2019 and 2023 the industry brought 28 new diagnostic tools and 71 new vaccines to market to reduce the need for antimicrobials.
Answer to the strongest objection: Restriction advocates point to the 16% rise in US sales in 2024 as proof that voluntary approaches have failed. This camp answers that sales do not equal use, as FDA itself cautions, that sales move with disease outbreaks and herd sizes from year to year, and that sales in 2024 remained 27% below their 2015 peak. It notes that the colistin case involved a feed-additive growth promoter of the kind US producers no longer use for medically important drugs, and argues that a ban on preventive use would raise animal disease and suffering without proven benefit to human patients.

Camp: Develop new antibiotics through public and nonprofit R&D
Adherents: Médecins Sans Frontières Access Campaign, which argues the PASTEUR Act would “drive up prices of PASTEUR-supported novel antimicrobials in all markets,” “compromise stewardship” and “be an inefficient means of incentivizing meaningful antimicrobial innovation,” and urges “expanding investment in public and non-profit R&D” that keeps public authority over production and distribution (22 February 2024, https://msfaccess.org/pasteur-act-not-way-us-government-address-antimicrobial-resistance); ReAct – Action on Antibiotic Resistance, which says “the public sector needs to get involved to get the highest priority antibiotics, diagnostics and vaccines developed” and calls for “a new alternative to the traditional patent-based business model, where the return on investment does not depend on high volume sales or high prices of novel antibiotics” (https://www.reactgroup.org/antibiotic-resistance/what-do-we-need/).
Case: If antibiotics are a public good that should be used sparingly and shared fairly, the public should own their development rather than rent it from companies at a premium. Subscription schemes pay firms billions while leaving them free to set prices and decide which markets to serve, so patients in low- and middle-income countries, who bear most of the burden, may never see the drugs their governments helped pay for. Plazomicin, developed with more than $100 million in US government grants, was not widely marketed after its developer’s bankruptcy. Public and nonprofit developers can fund the science directly, license on condition of affordable access and stewardship, and keep the knowledge open. This is not a rejection of new antibiotics but a different way to get them: fully delinking development from sales and keeping control in public hands.
Variation: Some in this camp accept pull payments if they carry strong, enforceable conditions on global access, price and stewardship; others oppose any reward that leaves the patent and pricing power with a private company.
Best evidence: Zoliflodacin, approved by the FDA in December 2025 as a first-in-class oral treatment for gonorrhea, was developed by the nonprofit GARDP with Innoviva Specialty Therapeutics (GARDP, https://gardp.org/us-fda-approval-of-nuzolvence/). A 2024 financial analysis of plazomicin found that US government biosecurity grants of $106 million had not resulted in access, because the Indian and Chinese companies that bought the rights after Achaogen’s bankruptcy did not widely market it (Wells, Nguyen & Harbarth, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03452-0).
Answer to the strongest objection: Supporters of pull incentives say governments are poor at drug development and that private capital is needed. This camp answers that public agencies and philanthropies already fund much early antibiotic research, through programs such as CARB-X, that the nonprofit route has now produced an approved first-in-class antibiotic, and that public developers can contract private firms for manufacturing and trials without ceding control. The question, it argues, is not whether firms are involved but who sets the terms.

## Crux

How much can using antibiotics less hold resistance back, and when it cannot, whose needs should give way: today’s patients, farmers and the poor who lack drugs, or the future patients whose drugs are being used up?

Every camp on this page agrees that resistance is rising and that both conservation and new drugs matter. They split over which lever does most of the work and who carries the cost. If cutting use, on farms and in clinics, reliably pushes resistance down, then conservation is the cheapest and most durable policy, the burden falls mainly on producers and prescribers, and large payments to drug companies look less urgent. If resistance mostly persists once established and bacteria keep outrunning restraint, then a steady supply of new drugs, and the money to bring them to market, becomes the core of any answer. If most AMR deaths happen where people cannot get any suitable antibiotic at all, then neither restriction nor rich-country innovation addresses the largest harm, and access and prevention move to the center.

What evidence could settle: how much and how fast resistance falls when use falls, where the record runs from the large drop in colistin resistance after China’s 2017 feed ban to the near-zero effect of an 85% cut in trimethoprim use in Kronoberg County, Sweden; what share of resistant human infections traces back to farm use, where genomic studies in Southern California and the East of England point in different directions; whether subscription payments in England, and in the United States if PASTEUR passes, actually produce new antibiotics that would not otherwise have been developed; and how many deaths better access, infection control, water and vaccines would avert compared with new drugs, where the 2024 GBD scenarios (92 million deaths averted by better care and 11.1 million by new Gram-negative drugs, 2025–2050) are an early answer built on assumptions that are open to challenge.

What no data can close: how much weight a sick person or a farmer today should give up for patients who are not yet born; whether a shared, slowly depleting resource should be managed by binding rules or by professional judgment; whether public money that pays for new drugs should buy private returns or public ownership; and how the obligations of rich countries, which used antibiotics freely for decades, compare with the claims of poorer countries that still lack the basic antibiotics rich countries take for granted.
