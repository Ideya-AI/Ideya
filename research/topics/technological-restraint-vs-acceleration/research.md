# Technological Restraint vs. Acceleration — research

## Overview

This topic is the general question of how fast a society should develop a technology that could do great harm: whether it should deliberately slow, pause or give up such a technology, speed it up, or steer which parts of it arrive first. It covers the competing philosophies of pace: the precautionary principle (a new technology should be shown safe before it spreads), its opposite numbers in “permissionless innovation” and the techno-optimist and “effective accelerationist” movements (progress is the safest course and delay has its own victims), the middle positions of differential technological development and “defensive acceleration” (speed up the technologies that protect and hold back the ones that endanger), and the critique that the real question is not speed at all but who controls a technology and for whose benefit. It covers the historical record that each side argues from: the 1974 to 1976 pause on recombinant DNA research and the Asilomar conference, the treaties banning biological and chemical weapons and nuclear test explosions, the near-universal national bans on heritable human genome editing, the slowdown of civilian nuclear power, the 2014 to 2017 US funding pause on gain-of-function research, the 2024 call by scientists not to create “mirror life,” and the 1973 US ban on overland supersonic flight that the government is now proposing to lift. Today the argument is mostly about artificial intelligence, and the AI pause and superintelligence-ban movements are its sharpest form. The topic deliberately excludes what belongs to its neighbors: the design of AI laws and who should write them (Frontier AI Regulation), how likely AI is to cause a catastrophe (AI Existential Risk), the governance of risky pathogen research (Gain-of-Function Research), whether model weights should be published (Open vs Closed AI), and how much the interests of future people should count (What We Owe Future Generations). Those debates appear here only where they decide whether slowing down is wise, possible or legitimate.

Why now: in the autumn of 2026 restraint moved from open letters to elected politics in the United States. On 12 September Anthropic’s chief executive, Dario Amodei, wrote that “we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models,” while adding that “pacing does not mean halting model training or technical progress” (https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier). Two days later President Trump, phoning Nvidia’s Jensen Huang on stage at the All-In Summit, dismissed fears of AI destroying humanity: “I’m telling you, it’s all a hoax” (Fox Business, 14 September 2026, https://www.foxbusiness.com/technology/trump-calls-nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-during-summit-dismisses-ai-fears-hoax). On 23 September Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would prohibit developing superintelligent AI, pause advanced AI development until a new Cabinet-level Department of Artificial Intelligence has set rules, and carry penalties of up to 20 years in prison for individuals; Sanders said, “When you are racing towards a cliff, you don’t just ease up on the gas pedal. You hit the brakes” (https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-casar-introduce-legislation-to-create-new-federal-agency-to-ban-artificial-superintelligence-pause-advanced-ai-development/). On 3 November Democrats won control of both houses of Congress (OPB/NPR, https://www.opb.org/article/2026/11/04/2026-midterms-democrats-win-the-senate/), and the next day Representative Yassamin Ansari wrote that the “top of the Democratic agenda on day 1 needs to be a pause on advanced AI development,” alongside “a ban on artificial superintelligence” (Yahoo News, 5 November 2026, https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/congresswoman-calls-ai-superintelligence-ban-193920688.html). On the same ballot, voters favored limiting data center development in 40 of 41 local measures, including five full bans, twelve partial bans and three six-month moratoriums (Ballotpedia, 6 November 2026, https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/11/06/voters-favored-limiting-data-center-development-in-40-of-41-local-ballot-measures-on-nov-3/); by 13 November Ballotpedia counted 46 of the 47 data center measures decided in 2026 as limiting development (https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/11/15/voters-have-decided-on-47-measures-related-to-data-centers-in-2026-in-december-they-will-consider-four-more/). The local measures turned mostly on electricity bills, noise and land use rather than on AI risk, but they were the first time voters in many places had a direct say over the physical build-out of AI.

The debate is live because both sides now hold real power and the gap between them is widening. The US government’s stated policy, in its July 2025 AI Action Plan, is that “the United States is in a race to achieve global dominance in artificial intelligence” and that the private sector “must be unencumbered by bureaucratic red tape” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Americas-AI-Action-Plan.pdf); money is moving the same way, with US private AI investment reaching about $286 billion in 2025 by the Stanford AI Index’s count. At the same time a majority of Americans in polling commissioned by the Future of Life Institute said superhuman AI should not be built until it is shown to be safe and controllable or should never be built, the heads of some leading AI labs have asked to be slowed, and a movement that once wanted a six-month pause now wants superintelligence prohibited outright. The argument is older than AI and wider: scientists have paused their own fields before, governments have banned whole classes of weapons, and critics of precaution point to civilian nuclear power and slow drug approval as restraint that cost lives. The participants are AI companies (which disagree among themselves), venture investors and the techno-optimist and accelerationist movements, the Trump administration and a newly Democratic Congress, pause and ban campaigners such as the Future of Life Institute, PauseAI and the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, proponents of differential and “defensive” acceleration such as Vitalik Buterin and the progress-studies community, economists and civil-society researchers who argue the question is power rather than pace, scientists who police their own fields, and governments abroad, above all China’s, whose choices every side cites.

## Questions

Question: Can a dangerous technology be held back once it becomes possible?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Restraint can hold / It gets built anyway
Why contested: Both sides argue from the historical record, and the record has examples for each. The disagreement is over which cases are the right analogy for AI and the other technologies now in dispute.
Case for “Restraint can hold”: Societies have declined, delayed or tightly limited technologies many times, often against strong incentives. Biologists paused recombinant DNA experiments in 1974 and wrote containment rules at Asilomar before resuming. Most countries forbid heritable human genome editing and none explicitly permits it; no verified human reproductive clone has been born; nuclear test explosions fell from hundreds a decade to a handful once states agreed to stop. Nuclear weapons, which President Kennedy warned in 1963 might be held by 15, 20 or 25 countries in the 1970s, are held by nine. Restraint is not a fantasy, its advocates argue, but a normal thing societies do when the stakes are clear, and the claim of inevitability is often made by the people who profit from building the technology. The AI case has a specific handle that most technologies lack: training frontier models takes enormous data centers full of specialized chips made by very few companies, which can be counted, located and licensed.
Case for “It gets built anyway”: The successes are mostly technologies that were expensive, easy to detect, of limited commercial value or already possessed by the states doing the restraining. Nuclear tests shake the ground; a training run does not. The Soviet Union signed the Biological Weapons Convention and then ran a vast covert weapons program; He Jiankui edited embryos despite a global consensus against it. A technology with large economic and military returns, many independent developers and falling costs is a different kind of thing: chip efficiency improves every year, algorithms get cheaper, and capabilities that once required a frontier lab end up on ordinary hardware. On this view a ban does not stop a technology; it decides who builds it, and pushes development toward the least scrupulous and least visible actors.

Question: Would slowing down at home just hand the lead to rivals?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Rivals would follow / Rivals would defect
Why contested: Almost every argument against restraint in AI runs through China, and almost every argument for it depends on international agreement. Neither side can observe the other government’s real intentions, and both cite the same Cold War history.
Case for “Rivals would follow”: China’s government has its own reasons to fear uncontrollable AI, which threatens the Communist Party’s control at least as much as it threatens Western governments, and it has regulated generative AI more tightly than the United States has. Chinese officials and scientists have signed international statements on AI risk, and the United States and the Soviet Union reached verified limits on nuclear arms at the height of mutual hostility. The US lead in private investment, roughly twenty times China’s tracked figure in 2025, and in advanced chips gives Washington the leverage to set terms rather than follow. A race framed as unstoppable becomes self-fulfilling, the advocates of mutual restraint argue, and the more dangerous the technology, the more each side gains from a verifiable agreement not to build it first.
Case for “Rivals would defect”: Restraint by democracies that is not matched by authoritarian rivals is worse than no restraint at all, because it shifts the most powerful technology of the century to the governments least likely to use it well. Even advocates of pacing, such as Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, accept that a unilateral slowdown followed by Chinese defection could give China geopolitical dominance. Software is hard to verify, the best Chinese models now trail the American frontier by months rather than years (Epoch AI estimates the best open-weight models, now mostly Chinese, trailed the closed frontier by about four months on average in 2026, https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-closed-eci-gap), and the history of the Biological Weapons Convention shows that a treaty without inspection can be cheated at scale. On this view the safest path is for the United States and its allies to lead, so that the rules of the technology are written by democracies.

Question: Does extra time make a dangerous technology safer?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Time buys safety / Building buys safety
Why contested: A pause is only worth its cost if the time is used well. People who agree the risks are real still disagree on whether safety comes from waiting or from working with the most capable systems.
Case for “Time buys safety”: Safety work is far behind capability work: by one estimate about 2 percent of AI research articles are about safety, and the methods for understanding what a large model is doing internally are immature. A pause lets understanding catch up, lets institutions for testing and oversight be built, and lets society adapt before the next jump. The recombinant DNA pause produced containment rules that let the field resume with public trust; the gap between nuclear weapons and arms control produced the near-misses of the Cuban missile crisis. Without time, those who favor restraint argue, safety research is a race it cannot win, because every advance in capability creates new failure modes faster than they can be studied.
Case for “Building buys safety”: Much of what is known about making AI systems behave came from studying the most capable systems, and the risks of a system cannot be studied before the system exists. A pause on training frontier models would freeze the safety work along with it, while chips and algorithms kept improving, so that when the pause ended the next model would be a larger jump than if progress had continued (the “overhang” argument). Releasing capabilities gradually lets society see problems while the stakes are low. Those who hold this view, including some of the AI labs themselves, argue that the real choice is not between fast and slow but between learning from deployment and guessing in advance.

Question: How much risk of catastrophe is worth accepting for large benefits?
Tag: value
Poles: No gamble with extinction / Weigh it against benefits
Why contested: Even with an agreed probability, people would disagree on what it implies, because the question turns on how to weigh an irreversible worst case against large, certain-seeming gains and against the people who die while waiting.
Case for “No gamble with extinction”: An outcome that ends humanity, or permanently takes the future out of human hands, is not one bad outcome among others; it forecloses every future good, so it cannot be offset by benefits to the present generation. Bridges, aircraft and reactors are designed to failure probabilities far below one percent; AI researchers’ median estimate of an extinction-level outcome from AI is 5 percent. Nobody consented to that gamble, and the people placing it are mostly the people who stand to profit. When harm is irreversible and the people imposing the risk are not the people bearing it, the burden is on those who want to proceed.
Case for “Weigh it against benefits”: Delay also has a body count. Every year that AI-driven drug discovery, cheaper energy or faster science arrives later, people die who would have lived, and their deaths are no less real for being statistical. Refusing to weigh catastrophic risk against benefit treats a speculative probability as decisive while ignoring certain costs, and if applied consistently would have forbidden most of the technologies that doubled life expectancy. Those who hold this view argue that the right response to a small chance of catastrophe is to reduce it while proceeding, as with aviation or nuclear power, and that a technology which could cure most diseases or end material poverty is worth significant risk.

Question: Who should carry the burden of proof for a new technology?
Tag: value
Poles: Prove it safe first / Prove it harmful first
Why contested: This is the old dispute between the precautionary principle and its rivals, sometimes called the proactionary principle or permissionless innovation. It is a choice about default rules, and evidence cannot settle which default is right.
Case for “Prove it safe first”: Drugs, aircraft and bridges must be shown to be safe before the public is exposed to them, and the burden falls on the party that profits and knows the most. A technology whose failure could be catastrophic and irreversible should meet at least that standard. Proving harm after the fact means waiting for the harm, and with leaded gasoline, asbestos, tobacco and chlorofluorocarbons the evidence of harm arrived decades after the damage was done. Those who favor precaution argue that a requirement to show safety does not stop innovation; it changes who pays for the uncertainty.
Case for “Prove it harmful first”: No new technology can be proven safe in advance, because its uses and failures are learned only in practice, so a prove-it-safe rule is in effect a rule against anything new. The costs of the default fall on people who never see them: the patients who die waiting for a drug approval, the coal miners and asthma sufferers of a grid that did not go nuclear, the supersonic aircraft never designed because speed itself was banned. Marc Andreessen calls the precautionary principle the movement’s “enemy” because, as he puts it, it “would have prevented virtually all progress since man first harnessed fire.” On this view regulation should target demonstrated harms, and the default for new technology should be permission.

Question: Can defensive technology keep ahead of offensive technology?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Defense can win / Offense dominates
Why contested: The middle positions in this debate, differential development and defensive acceleration, depend on the answer. If protective technologies can be built faster than destructive ones, the case for blanket restraint weakens; if not, the case for it strengthens.
Case for “Defense can win”: Many dangerous capabilities have defensive counterparts that can be deliberately prioritized: metagenomic sequencing that spots new pathogens early, far-ultraviolet light and better ventilation that cut airborne transmission, screening of DNA synthesis orders, formal verification of software, AI systems that find and patch vulnerabilities before attackers do. Vitalik Buterin argues that some technologies are defense-favoring and “worth promoting,” and that a world that accelerates them is safer than one that either races indiscriminately or tries to stop everything. Historically, defenses have caught up with most offensive innovations, from fortifications to antibiotics to spam filters.
Case for “Offense dominates”: For the most dangerous technologies the asymmetry runs the other way: one engineered pathogen or one successful cyberattack on a power grid can do damage that thousands of defenders cannot undo, and a single failure of defense may be enough. Nick Bostrom’s “vulnerable world” argument holds that some technologies, once cheap, would let small groups cause catastrophe, and no defensive technology can make that safe; only control of the capability itself can. The faster the offensive capability spreads, the less time there is for defenses to be built and deployed, which is why those who hold this view see restraint, not acceleration of any kind, as the precondition for defense.
Editor addition (fix mode, 2026-11-23), for “Defense can win”: DARPA announced AI Cyber Challenge results on 8 August 2025; finalist systems found 18 real, non-synthetic vulnerabilities (six in C, 12 in Java) and “Teams submitted patches in an average of 45 minutes” (https://darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results).

Question: Who should decide how fast a technology moves?
Tag: value
Poles: The public decides / Builders and markets decide
Why contested: A small number of companies are now making choices about technologies whose effects fall on everyone. Whether that is the normal working of a free society or a democratic failure is a disagreement about legitimacy, not evidence.
Case for “The public decides”: When a technology could change the conditions of life for everyone, or end them, the decision to build it should not rest with a handful of executives and investors. Polling shows large public majorities wanting AI developed more slowly and under strong regulation, and the AI experts Pew surveyed were far more excited about AI than the public was. Those who hold this view, including the Future of Life Institute, whose statement asks for “strong public buy-in” before superintelligence is built, argue that pace should be set through elections, legislation and international agreement, as it was for nuclear weapons.
Case for “Builders and markets decide”: Decisions about what to invent have always been made by inventors, entrepreneurs and the customers who choose to use what they make, and that decentralized process has produced most of the gains in human welfare. Public opinion on unfamiliar technologies is easily moved by fear and by the framing of a poll question, and politicians who set the pace of science would bring the same cost overruns and capture as they bring to everything else. On this view the role of government is to punish concrete harms and set general rules, not to decide in advance how fast knowledge may advance.

Question: Is the bigger danger the speed of a technology or who controls it?
Tag: value
Poles: Speed is the danger / Power is the danger
Why contested: Much of the restraint debate assumes that the problem is the technology moving too fast. A significant body of critics argues that this framing hides a more pressing question about ownership, labor and power, and that pause campaigns and accelerationists share an assumption that the technology’s current direction is fixed.
Case for “Speed is the danger”: The distinctive risks of advanced AI and engineered biology come from the capabilities themselves: a system that can design pathogens or deceive its overseers is dangerous whoever owns it. Fair distribution of a technology that might end humanity is beside the point. Concentration of power is a real problem, those who emphasize speed argue, but it can be addressed after the technology is safe, while a catastrophe cannot be addressed after it happens.
Case for “Power is the danger”: The harms already visible, from job displacement to surveillance to the concentration of wealth and political influence in a few firms, come from who controls the technology and what they choose to build. Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson argue that the direction of technology is a choice, and that across history gains were shared only when institutions forced them to be. On this view both the accelerationist promise of abundance and the pause movement’s fear of superintelligence accept the industry’s story that AI is a single force racing toward a fixed endpoint, and both distract from decisions about who benefits that can be made now. “The future we’re being sold is not inevitable,” in the words of Amba Kak of the AI Now Institute.

Question: Do calls for restraint mainly protect the companies already in the lead?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real safety measures / Regulatory capture
Why contested: Some of the loudest calls to slow AI have come from the companies building it, and rules that impose fixed costs fall hardest on smaller rivals. Whether that pattern reveals motive or coincidence is disputed.
Case for “Real safety measures”: Most of the people calling for a pause or ban, from academic researchers to campaigners to members of Congress, have no commercial stake in AI, and the strongest proposals would halt the leading companies’ own frontier work, which is hard to describe as a gift to them. When lab leaders call for oversight, those who favor restraint argue, the explanation is usually that they have seen evidence the public has not. Dismissing every safety argument as a business strategy is a way of not engaging with the argument.
Case for “Regulatory capture”: Licensing regimes, compute thresholds and mandatory audits are easiest for the largest firms to meet and hardest for open-source developers and start-ups, so they tend to lock in whoever is ahead. US Vice President JD Vance told the February 2025 Paris AI summit that restricting AI with onerous regulation would “unfairly benefit incumbents,” a line the Trump administration’s AI Action Plan quotes. When a company calls for slowing the field while continuing to build, those who hold this view argue, the call works as a moat.

## Positions

Camp: Pause dangerous technology until it is proven safe
Adherents: Future of Life Institute (institution), whose Statement on Superintelligence calls for “a prohibition on the development of superintelligence, not lifted before there is broad scientific consensus that it will be done safely and controllably, and strong public buy-in” (https://superintelligence-statement.org/); PauseAI (institution), which calls for “a temporary pause on the training of the most powerful general AI systems” “until we know how to build them safely and keep them under democratic control,” enforced by a treaty and an international AI Safety Agency (https://pauseai.info/proposal); the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (institution), which says “progress toward ASI needs to be halted until ASI can be made alignable” through “an effective worldwide ban” (https://intelligence.org/the-problem/); the 38 scientists, including George Church and Kevin Esvelt, who wrote in Science in December 2024 that “research with the goal of creating mirror bacteria not be permitted” (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, https://thebulletin.org/2024/12/scientists-urge-halt-to-research-on-creating-synthetic-mirror-bacteria-that-could-evade-human-immunity-disrupt-ecosystems/).
Case: Some technologies are dangerous enough that the right response is to stop pushing them forward until we know how to control them, and the burden of showing that should fall on the people building them. Advanced AI is the clearest case today. The companies racing to build systems smarter than people say openly that they do not know how to make such systems reliably do what we intend, and some of their own leaders say the chance of catastrophe is far from negligible. No one would let an airline fly a plane with those odds, and no one asked the public whether it accepts them. A pause is not the end of the technology; it is the time needed for the science of control to catch up and for institutions that can verify safety to be built. Restraint of this kind has worked before. Biologists stopped recombinant DNA work in 1974 and resumed with rules; nations stopped testing nuclear weapons; almost every country forbids editing the human germline; leading synthetic biologists, some of whom had worked toward mirror life, have asked that no one create mirror bacteria. Frontier AI is easier to restrain than most technologies, because it needs vast data centers and advanced chips made by a handful of companies, so a pause can be verified the way arms control is verified, by counting hardware. The claim that a pause is impossible is made mostly by those who profit from the race.
Best evidence: In polling for the Future of Life Institute in 2025, 64 percent of Americans said superhuman AI should not be built until proven safe and controllable or should never be built, and 64 percent supported an immediate pause on advanced AI until it is proven safe. AI researchers’ median estimate of an extinction-level outcome from AI was 5 percent in the largest survey of the field, and about 70 percent said safety research should be prioritized more. By the Emerging Technology Observatory’s estimate, safety is about 2 percent of AI research. The heritable editing consensus (75 of 96 countries with a policy prohibit it, none permits it) and the near-end of nuclear testing show that rivals can agree to leave a capability unused.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that China would not stop, so an American pause would hand it the lead. The answer is that the pause this camp wants is international and verified, not unilateral: PauseAI calls the involvement of both the United States and China essential, and MIRI wants a treaty with the power to shut down frontier projects anywhere. China’s government has its own reasons to fear a technology it cannot control, the United States and its allies control the supply of the most advanced chips, and a race to build something no one can control has no winner. Those in the camp add that “someone else will do it” has been said of every dangerous technology that was in fact restrained.
Variation: The camp divides on how long and how conditional. PauseAI and the 2023 open letter asked for a pause of fixed or limited length on training beyond a threshold; the Future of Life Institute now asks for a prohibition on superintelligence that lifts only with scientific consensus and public consent; MIRI expects a survivable path to require delay “for a long time.” Members also differ on instrument, from compute caps and chip tracking to licensing and treaty enforcement. Outside AI, the same logic underlies the call not to create mirror life, the campaign against heritable genome editing and moratoria on geoengineering experiments.

Camp: Accelerate defensive technology first and steer the rest
Adherents: Vitalik Buterin, co-founder of Ethereum, who in “My techno-optimism” (November 2023) set out “d/acc,” where “the ‘d’ here can stand for many things; particularly, defense, decentralization, democracy and differential,” arguing that “some technologies are defense-favoring and are worth promoting” and calling d/acc “a subspecies of e/acc - just one that is much more selective and intentional” (https://vitalik.eth.limo/general/2023/11/27/techno_optimism.html); the Roots of Progress Institute (institution), whose founder Jason Crawford wrote in “A plea for solutionism on AI safety” that the best path is “to acknowledge the risks, help to identify them, and come up with a plan to solve them,” and that “we shouldn’t be against AI safety—done right—any more than we are against seat belts, fire alarms, or drug trials” (https://blog.rootsofprogress.org/solutionism-on-ai-safety); Anthropic’s chief executive Dario Amodei, who in September 2026 wrote that “we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models” while insisting that “pacing does not mean halting model training or technical progress” (https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier).
Case: The choice between stopping technology and flooring the accelerator is a false one. Technologies are not a single dial; they are many, and the order in which they arrive matters more than the overall speed. The right strategy is to accelerate, deliberately and with public money if needed, the technologies that make the world more robust: pathogen surveillance, broad-spectrum vaccines, far-ultraviolet light, DNA synthesis screening, secure software, tools for understanding what AI systems are doing inside, and decentralized systems that keep any one actor from gaining unchecked power. At the same time, the most dangerous capabilities should be paced, tested and contained, with independent evaluators who can see inside the labs, but not banned outright, because a ban hands the field to whoever ignores it and freezes the safety work that needs capable systems to study. Safety is not the opposite of progress; seat belts, clinical trials and fire codes are progress. A world that refuses to build will not be safe, because the threats, from natural pandemics to hostile states, keep coming; a world that builds without caution may not survive its own inventions. The middle path is to be optimistic about solutions and serious about problems.
Best evidence: The record of safety engineering in aviation, medicine and electricity shows that risky technologies became safe by being developed with safeguards, not by being stopped: OWID’s data put nuclear power, which grew up under strict safety regimes, at about 0.03 deaths per terawatt-hour, comparable to wind and solar. The gap between capability and safety work, with safety at about 2 percent of AI research articles, argues for shifting effort rather than stopping it. And the AI researchers surveyed in 2023 split almost evenly between wanting AI progress slower (34.7 percent) and faster (38.4 percent), with about 70 percent wanting safety research prioritized more, which is close to this camp’s own position: keep building, change the mix.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from the pause camp, is that offense beats defense for the worst technologies, so steering cannot keep up; from the acceleration camp, it is that any pacing invites the same capture and delay as a pause. To the first, the camp answers that “offense dominates” is a claim about particular technologies, not a law, and that the way to find out is to fund defenses at the scale of the threat, which has never been tried; where a capability truly cannot be defended against, as with mirror bacteria, its members support restraint. To the second, it answers that pacing tied to measurable capabilities and external testing is narrower than a general license to regulate, and that refusing all caution is the surest way to provoke the backlash, and the blunt bans, that accelerationists fear.
Variation: Members differ on how much brake to apply. Buterin’s d/acc is mostly about what to accelerate and treats a pause as, at most, a backup; Roots of Progress emphasizes safety as an engineering problem solved by builders; Anthropic’s pacing plan asks for coordinated slowing of frontier capability among democratic companies, binding on all of them, with independent evaluators embedded in the labs. The older academic form is differential technological development, associated with Nick Bostrom and Toby Ord.

Camp: Accelerate technology and lift the brakes
Adherents: Marc Andreessen of the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz (institution), whose Techno-Optimist Manifesto (16 October 2023) says “we believe any deceleration of AI will cost lives” and that “our enemy is the Precautionary Principle, which would have prevented virtually all progress since man first harnessed fire” (https://a16z.com/the-techno-optimist-manifesto/); the White House (institution), whose July 2025 AI Action Plan states that “the United States is in a race to achieve global dominance in artificial intelligence” and that “America’s private sector must be unencumbered by bureaucratic red tape” (https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Americas-AI-Action-Plan.pdf).
Case: Technology is the reason most people alive today are not poor, hungry or dead in childhood, and the most dangerous thing a society can do is to stop producing more of it. Life expectancy has risen from about 46 years in 1950 to about 73, and the share of the world in extreme poverty has fallen from more than 40 percent in 1990 to about 10, because of technologies that frightened people when they were new. AI could compress decades of medical and scientific progress into years; every year of delay is paid for in lives that would have been saved, even if no one sees those deaths. Precaution has a record too: civilian nuclear power, the safest source of electricity per unit produced, was strangled by fear and regulation while coal kept killing; supersonic flight was banned over land for half a century. Calls to pause AI rest on speculative scenarios, and the people demanding pauses would hand the most important technology of the century to a bureaucracy, or to the Chinese Communist Party, which will not pause. The way to make a technology safe is to build it, learn from it and improve it, as was done with cars, aircraft and medicine. The country that leads in AI will set its standards and reap its benefits; the country that hesitates will live under someone else’s.
Best evidence: Our World in Data’s long series on life expectancy and extreme poverty, and its death rates per unit of energy (nuclear 0.03 deaths per terawatt-hour against 24.62 for coal), are the camp’s core exhibits for what growth and technology deliver and what precaution costs. Nuclear power’s share of world electricity fell from 17.4 percent in 1996 to 8.8 percent in 2025. Private AI investment of about $286 billion in the United States in 2025, against $12 billion of tracked private investment in China, reflects the lead the camp wants to keep, and the 2026 AI Index found that US and Chinese models have traded places at the top of performance rankings several times since early 2025, with the top American model ahead by 2.7 percent in March 2026 (Stanford HAI, https://hai.stanford.edu/news/inside-the-ai-index-12-takeaways-from-the-2026-report), which the camp reads as proof that an American pause would simply hand over the lead.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a technology with even a small chance of ending humanity is different in kind from cars or nuclear power, so the usual record does not apply. The camp answers that the probabilities cited are guesses, not measurements, that professional forecasters put AI-caused extinction far lower than the survey numbers suggest, and that every powerful technology was once described as uniquely dangerous. Where specific harms appear, they should be addressed specifically, by law and by better engineering, but the burden is on those who want to stop progress to show concrete harm, not on builders to prove a negative. And since the technology will be built somewhere, the real choice is whether it is built by free societies that care about safety or by those that do not.
Variation: The camp’s two wings give different reasons. The techno-optimist and “effective accelerationist” wing, associated with Andreessen and the engineer Guillaume Verdon, sees technological and economic growth as close to an unconditional good and distrusts government involvement; the national-security wing, represented by the AI Action Plan and by writers such as Leopold Aschenbrenner, accepts that AI is dangerous and argues that this is exactly why the United States must build it first, sometimes favoring tight security and close government partnership with the labs. The wings disagree on export controls and on how much the state should shape the industry, and agree that slowing down is the greater risk.

Camp: Redirect technology to serve the public
Adherents: The AI Now Institute (institution), whose 2025 report Artificial Power argues that AI should be understood as a question of power rather than of technological progress, and whose executive director Amba Kak said, “The future we’re being sold is not inevitable” (TechCrunch, 31 July 2025, https://techcrunch.com/podcast/who-really-benefits-from-the-ai-boom; report at https://ainowinstitute.org/2025-landscape); the economists Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson of MIT, authors of Power and Progress (2023), who write that “the current path of AI is neither good for the economy nor for democracy,” with Johnson saying, “There’s nothing inherent in technology. It’s within our control” (MIT News, https://news.mit.edu/2023/power-and-progress-book-ai-inequality-0517).
Case: The question “should we go faster or slower?” is the wrong question, and both the accelerationists and the pause movement are asking it. Technology does not move along a single track at a speed we can only adjust; at every point people choose what to build, for whom, and who captures the gains. For most of history, the gains from new technology went to the people who owned it until workers, voters and governments forced them to be shared. Today a handful of companies, flush with hundreds of billions of dollars, are deciding the direction of the most consequential technology of the age, and they have chosen to build systems that replace workers, gather data and concentrate power. The accelerationist story that this is inevitable progress and the pause story that it is a runaway superintelligence both accept the industry’s claim that AI is a single unstoppable force, and both distract from harms that are happening now and decisions that can be made now: who owns the data centers and the models, what they may be used for, whether workers have a say, and whether the public gets a share. The goal is neither speed nor stillness but direction, chosen democratically.
Best evidence: The concentration of AI investment is extreme: US private AI investment reached about $286 billion in 2025, and Epoch AI counts more than $370 billion raised by frontier AI labs. Pew found that 55 percent of US adults and 57 percent of AI experts want more control over how AI is used in their lives, and that majorities of both fear regulation will not go far enough. The 2026 local votes, in which 46 of 47 data center measures went toward limits, turned on electricity bills, water and land, the concrete local costs of the build-out rather than its speed. Acemoglu and Johnson’s historical argument is that the gains of industrialization were shared widely only after the rise of unions, democratic reforms and public investment.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that this camp ignores the catastrophic risks, so that arguing about ownership while a dangerous technology races ahead is fiddling while Rome burns. The camp answers that it does not ignore danger: AI Now’s 2025 report calls for clear prohibitions where safeguards are inadequate [as summarized by the AI Governance Library, https://www.aigl.blog/artificial-power-2025-landscape-report/; report text not fetched], and the surest protection against reckless development is to end the concentration of power that makes it profitable. Speculative future catastrophes, its members argue, have been used to justify handing still more control to the same companies, as the self-appointed stewards of a dangerous technology, while the present harms that could be addressed go unaddressed.
Variation: Some in the camp focus on labor and the economy (Acemoglu and Johnson favor steering AI toward tools that augment workers rather than replace them, through tax and research policy); some on corporate concentration and antitrust; some, such as the writer Brian Merchant, revive the Luddites as a movement against the use of machinery to strip workers of power rather than against machinery itself. Some members support specific moratoria, for example on facial recognition by police, while rejecting the pause movement’s framing.

Camp: Never build the most dangerous technologies
Adherents: Stop AI (institution), which calls for “a permanent and enforceable global ban on the further development of frontier AI technology” (https://www.stopai.info/); Senator Bernie Sanders, whose Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, introduced with Representative Greg Casar on 23 September 2026, provides that no person or entity may develop or deploy artificial superintelligence and would have a new Department of Artificial Intelligence supervise the destruction of any that is built (https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-casar-introduce-legislation-to-create-new-federal-agency-to-ban-artificial-superintelligence-pause-advanced-ai-development/).
Case: Some technologies should not be built at all, not now and not once someone claims to have made them safe. A machine more capable than humans at everything is one of them. Even if it could be controlled, the question “controlled by whom?” has no safe answer, and a world in which human labor, judgment and purpose are no longer needed is not one that people have asked for. Promises that a technology will be made safe later are how dangerous technologies get built; a pause that ends when the builders declare success is a pause in name only. Humanity has decided before that certain things are off-limits regardless of benefit, such as biological weapons and the cloning of human beings, and the same should be decided here: permanently, in law, with real penalties, and by international agreement.
Best evidence: In the Future of Life Institute’s 2025 survey, 30 percent of US adults said superhuman AI should never be developed. The Biological Weapons Convention and the national bans on heritable editing and reproductive cloning show that permanent prohibitions on whole categories of technology exist and are widely accepted. The heads of leading AI companies have themselves said they do not know how to control systems smarter than humans.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a permanent ban forgoes enormous benefits, such as cures for disease, forever, on the strength of a speculative fear. The camp answers that the benefits can be pursued with narrower AI systems that do specific jobs, such as protein-structure prediction, without building a general machine that surpasses humans, and that the burden of a permanent, irreversible risk to everyone cannot be justified by benefits promised by the people who would control it. A ban that is permanent in law can be revisited by future democratic decision; an uncontrolled superintelligence cannot be undone.
Variation: Members differ on scope. Stop AI’s demand covers further development of frontier AI generally; the Sanders–Casar bill bans superintelligence outright while pausing other advanced AI only until a federal regulator is running; some writers in a longer tradition, from Bill Joy’s 2000 essay “Why the Future Doesn’t Need Us” to the essayist Paul Kingsnorth, argue for relinquishing whole classes of technology. This camp shares much with the pause camp and is distinguished by its answer to one question: whether superintelligence should ever be built if it can be shown to be safe.

## Crux

Crux: Would deliberately slowing a dangerous technology, by agreement among those able to build it, leave the world safer than building it as fast as possible?

Almost every camp’s position follows from its answer. Those who want a pause or a ban answer yes, on the condition that the agreement is international and enforced; those who want to accelerate answer no, because they doubt that rivals would comply or that waiting would produce safety; those who want to steer answer yes for some capabilities and no for others; and those who would redirect technology answer that pace matters less than who decides. A reader who changed their answer would usually change camps.

What evidence could settle: Whether the key inputs to a dangerous technology can be counted and verified (for AI, whether chips and large training runs can be tracked well enough that cheating would be detected, as nuclear tests are). Whether the main rivals, above all the United States and China, would accept and keep a mutual limit, which past arms-control agreements, current diplomacy and the behavior of Chinese and American labs can inform. Whether time spent paused actually produces safety, through progress on understanding and controlling AI systems, or mainly produces an “overhang” of unused hardware and algorithms that makes the eventual jump larger. And how large the catastrophic risks really are, a question on which surveyed AI researchers and professional forecasters currently differ several-fold.

What is a value choice: How much certain benefit, including lives that faster medicine or cheaper energy might save, is worth giving up to reduce a chance of irreversible catastrophe, and how much weight future generations get in that trade. Who has the right to make the choice: the companies and scientists who build, the voters and governments who must live with the results, or some international body. And whether some technologies should be refused on principle, whatever their benefits and however safe they are claimed to be, because of what they would do to human work, power and purpose. Evidence can narrow the first of these questions but cannot answer any of them.
