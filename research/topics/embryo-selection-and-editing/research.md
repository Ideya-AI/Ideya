# Embryo Selection & Editing — research

Slug: embryo-selection-and-editing
Scope: Choosing children’s genes: ranking IVF embryos by predicted disease risk and traits, and permanent edits to embryos and gametes. Not somatic therapy for the living.
Researched: 2026-11-23

## Overview

This topic is about how far prospective parents, doctors, companies and governments should go in choosing the genes of children who do not yet exist. It covers two families of technique. The first is embryo selection during in vitro fertilization (IVF): testing several embryos and choosing which to transfer. That ranges from long-established preimplantation genetic testing for chromosome abnormalities (PGT-A) and for single-gene diseases such as cystic fibrosis or Huntington’s disease (PGT-M), to the newer and far more contested polygenic embryo screening (PGT-P), which ranks embryos by scores built from thousands of genetic variants and predicts risks of common diseases and, from some companies, traits such as height and intelligence. The second is heritable genome editing: changing the DNA of an embryo, egg or sperm so that the change is carried by the resulting child and passed to that child’s descendants, which most countries with a policy on it forbid. Mitochondrial donation, a legal form of heritable change in the United Kingdom and Australia, sits at the border and is included where it informs the debate. The topic deliberately excludes somatic gene therapy for living patients (such as the sickle cell treatment Casgevy), whose changes are not inherited; abortion and prenatal screening during pregnancy, except where the disability-rights argument carries over; and the general ethics of IVF, except where the moral status of embryos is the reason some people object to selection itself. The wider question of what present generations owe future ones is its own topic (what-we-owe-future-generations).

The why-now: on September 9, 2026, a Columbia University team led by Dieter Egli published in Nature a study of base editing in human embryos, a technique more precise than CRISPR. Using it to change single DNA letters, they altered the cholesterol gene PCSK9 and the hemoglobin genes HBG1 and HBG2 in single-cell embryos kept in the lab for six to seven days; in some experiments every embryo cell carried the intended change and development looked normal, but the method also produced mosaic embryos, edits at unintended sites and some large deletions, and Egli said “given our findings, it is currently not possible to do so safely” (Columbia University Irving Medical Center, https://www.cuimc.columbia.edu/news/study-shows-limits-precise-gene-editing-human-embryos). Three weeks later NPR profiled Origin Genomics, a New York startup launched in March 2026 by Cathy Tie, which tests editors on cells from donated embryos and hopes to persuade Congress to lift the ban that keeps the Food and Drug Administration from considering edited-embryo trials; it is one of several US startups, alongside Preventive in San Francisco, pursuing the idea (NPR via OPB, September 29, 2026, https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/29/origin-genomics-mission-to-make-healthy-gene-edited-babies/). On the selection side, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine concluded in December 2025 that polygenic embryo screening “should not be offered as a reproductive service at this time” (ASRM, December 8, 2025, https://www.asrm.org/news-and-events/asrm-news/press-releasesbulletins/asrm-ethics-and-practice-committees-release-new-report-concluding-polygenic-embryo-screening-is-not-ready-for-clinical-use), and Britain’s fertility regulator said in February 2026 that “PGT-P is not lawful in the UK,” even when the analysis is done abroad (HFEA, February 4, 2026, https://www.hfea.gov.uk/about-us/our-blog/pgt-p-is-not-lawful-in-the-uk-is-not-supported-by-evidence-and-may-reduce-the-chances-of-having-a-baby-overall). US companies kept expanding their menus regardless: Herasight launched a screening test for autism and schizophrenia risk on October 8, 2026 (Herasight, https://herasight.substack.com/archive), and Nucleus Genomics, whose November 2025 New York advertising campaign urged prospective parents to “Have your best baby” (Nucleus, https://mynucleus.com/have-your-best-baby-2025), was the listed affiliation of one co-author of the Columbia editing study, Nathan Treff (Columbia, same release); the Center for Genetics and Society cited that link when the work first appeared as a preprint, in calling for stronger prohibitions (June 6, 2026, https://www.geneticsandsociety.org/press-statement/center-genetics-and-society-comment-base-editing-embryo-genes-engineered-babies).

The debate is live because selection is already a consumer product in the United States, where federal law does not restrict which conditions or traits embryos may be tested for, while editing has moved from He Jiankui’s condemned 2018 experiment to venture-backed research with explicit plans for clinical use. The people in it divide four ways. Professional and scientific bodies such as the ASRM, the national academies and the gene therapy industry accept screening for serious inherited disease but want polygenic scores and editing kept out of the clinic until they are proven, and many of them would then permit editing only for serious disease. Companies such as Nucleus Genomics and Herasight and philosophers such as Julian Savulescu argue that parents should be free to choose among their own embryos, including for traits. Social-justice and disability-rights groups such as the Center for Genetics and Society want heritable editing and trait selection prohibited outright as a commercial revival of eugenics. Religious traditions that hold embryos to be human lives, among them the Catholic Church and the Southern Baptist Convention, object to creating and discarding embryos in order to choose among them at all.

### 1. Countries that prohibit heritable genome editing
- Label: Countries with a policy on heritable genome editing that prohibit it
- Value: 75
- Unit: countries, of 78 with a relevant policy (106 surveyed)
- Comparison rows (same source, same unit, policies as of 2020): prohibit outright 70; prohibit with possible exceptions 5; indeterminate 3; explicitly permit 0
- Data year: 2020
- Source label: Baylis, Darnovsky, Hasson & Krahn, “Human Germline and Heritable Genome Editing: The Global Policy Landscape,” The CRISPR Journal, October 2020 (authors’ summary)
- Source URL: https://www.geneticsandsociety.org/biopolitical-times/assessing-global-policy-landscape-human-germline-and-heritable-genome-editing
- Note: Of 78 countries with a policy on heritable genome editing, 75 prohibit it and none explicitly permits it.
- Context: The survey covered 106 countries. On the authors’ count, 78 had policies relevant to using an edited embryo to start a pregnancy; 70 ban it outright, 5 ban it with possible exceptions and 3 could not be classified. The published abstract states the same count against a different base, 75 of the 96 countries that have any relevant policy document (https://geneticsandsociety.org/article/human-germ-line-and-heritable-genome-editing-global-policy-landscape), so the share depends on the denominator. Two of the authors work at the Center for Genetics and Society, which favors prohibition. In the United States there is no statute banning the practice; instead, a rider first attached to the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2016 (section 749) bars the FDA from even acknowledging an application for clinical research on edited embryos (JAMA, 2017, https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2601488). The House passed the language again for fiscal 2027 on June 4, 2026, as section 734, which covers research “in which a human embryo is intentionally created or modified to include a heritable genetic modification” (FDA Law Blog, https://www.thefdalawblog.com/2026/06/riders-on-the-storm-part-1-what-the-fy2027-appropriations-report-means-for-fda/). China’s July 2024 ethics guidelines say “any clinical research involving germline genome editing is irresponsible and not allowed” (Government of China, https://english.www.gov.cn/news/202407/09/content_WS668d34e4c6d0868f4e8e903e.html), and South Africa withdrew a 2024 research-ethics guideline section on heritable editing that critics had read as opening a path to it (South African Medical Journal, 2025, https://repository.up.ac.za/items/31a00564-c626-46b6-a21f-2a59edd3faf8).

### 2. Predicted gain from choosing the top-scoring embryo
- Label: Average gain in height and IQ from choosing the top-scoring of five embryos
- Value: 2.5
- Unit: centimeters of height, and IQ points (≈2.5 each), compared with the average embryo
- Data year: 2019 (published model using genetic data then available)
- Source label: Karavani et al., “Screening Human Embryos for Polygenic Traits Has Limited Utility,” Cell, 2019
- Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6957074/
- Note: Picking the top-scoring of five embryos was predicted to gain about 2.5 cm of height and 2.5 IQ points over an average embryo.
- Context: The authors simulated embryos from real genomes and also checked predictions in large families: the child with the highest height score was the tallest child in only 7 of 28 families. They noted that polygenic scores “lose substantial power, or may even be invalid, when applied across different populations.” Prediction has improved since. Herasight, a company that sells embryo IQ prediction, reported in April 2026 that its cognitive-ability score implies an expected spread of about 10 IQ points between the highest- and lowest-scoring of five embryos for parents of European ancestry, about 13 with ten embryos, and less for other ancestries (6 points with five embryos for African ancestry); it calls these “expected values across many selections, not guarantees for any individual case” (Herasight, https://herasight.substack.com/p/cogpgtpub). The two figures are on different bases: Karavani measures the best embryo against the average one, Herasight the best against the worst, which roughly doubles the number, and Herasight’s is the seller’s own analysis.

### 3. Disease risk reduction from polygenic embryo selection
- Label: Reduction in schizophrenia risk from choosing the lowest-scoring of five embryos
- Value: 52
- Unit: percent relative risk reduction (about 0.5 percentage points absolute)
- Data year: 2021 (simulation using case-control genomes)
- Source label: Lencz et al., “Utility of polygenic embryo screening for disease depends on the selection strategy,” eLife, 2021
- Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510582/
- Note: Choosing the lowest-risk of five embryos cut predicted schizophrenia risk by about half in relative terms, about half a percentage point in absolute terms.
- Context: The study found that the benefit depends on the strategy. Excluding only embryos with very high scores achieves little, while choosing the embryo with the lowest score can give relative risk reductions of up to about 45 to 52 percent for schizophrenia and Crohn’s disease with five viable embryos. Because schizophrenia affects about 1 percent of people, a halving of relative risk moves the absolute risk by about half a point. Those who favor screening stress the relative figure across many diseases at once; those who doubt it stress the absolute figure, the small number of usable embryos most patients have (stat 6), and the risk that lowering one disease score raises another.

### 10. Unintended changes in CRISPR-edited human embryos
- Label: Edited embryo cells with large unintended genetic changes after CRISPR-Cas9 editing
- Value: 16
- Unit: percent (approximately) of embryo cells or samples analyzed
- Data year: 2021
- Source label: Alanis-Lobato et al., “Frequent loss of heterozygosity in CRISPR-Cas9–edited early human embryos,” PNAS, 2021
- Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8179174/
- Note: About 16 percent of cells analyzed from CRISPR-edited human embryos had large unintended changes at the target region.
- Context: The Francis Crick Institute team, led by Kathy Niakan, found that unintended changes spanning 4,000 to 20,000 DNA letters, including losses and gains of chromosome segments, were missed by standard checks; the paper states the share as cells analyzed, while the University of Cambridge summary describes it as about 16 percent of samples (https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/researchers-call-for-greater-awareness-of-unintended-consequences-of-crispr-gene-editing-0). A 2020 Cell study from Dieter Egli’s Columbia lab found that about half of the DNA breaks Cas9 made in embryos went unrepaired, often leading to loss of a chromosome arm (Zuccaro et al., https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33125898/). Newer base editing reduces such damage but does not eliminate it: the same Columbia group reported in Nature in September 2026 that base editing still produced mosaicism, edits at other sites and some large deletions, though far less often than CRISPR (Columbia, https://www.cuimc.columbia.edu/news/study-shows-limits-precise-gene-editing-human-embryos).

### 11. How much genetic predictions shrink within families
- Label: Shrinkage of genetic effect estimates when comparing siblings rather than unrelated people
- Value: 47
- Unit: percent decrease, for educational attainment
- Comparison rows (same source, same unit): number of children 67; age at first birth 52; depressive symptoms 50; educational attainment 47; cognitive ability 22; height 10
- Data year: 2022 (178,086 siblings from 19 cohorts)
- Source label: Howe et al., “Within-sibship genome-wide association analyses decrease bias in estimates of direct genetic effects,” Nature Genetics, 2022
- Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9110300/
- Note: Genetic associations with educational attainment were about half as strong when measured between siblings as across the population; for height, about a tenth weaker.
- Context: Embryos from the same parents are siblings, so what matters for selection is how well a score predicts differences within a family. Population studies mix direct genetic effects with family environment, ancestry and parenting that tracks parents’ genes; comparing siblings strips those out. The shrinkage was large for social and behavioral outcomes, moderate for cognitive ability and small for height and most biological measures such as cholesterol. Herasight reports that its cognitive score keeps most of its strength within families (effect 0.439 between siblings against 0.525 across the population in UK Biobank), which it presents as evidence the score captures direct effects (https://herasight.substack.com/p/cogpgtpub).

### 12. Polygenic score accuracy across ancestries
- Label: Accuracy of polygenic scores for people of European ancestry compared with others
- Value: 4.5
- Unit: times higher prediction accuracy than for people of African ancestry (2 times higher than for East Asian ancestry)
- Data year: 2019 (study participants as of 2016)
- Source label: Broad Institute summary of Martin et al., “Clinical use of current polygenic risk scores may exacerbate health disparities,” Nature Genetics, 2019
- Source URL: https://www.broadinstitute.org/news/study-highlights-need-increase-diversity-within-genetic-data-sets
- Note: Polygenic scores predicted about 4.5 times better for people of European ancestry than African ancestry, because most genetic studies enrolled Europeans.
- Context: As of 2016, 80 percent of participants in genetic studies were of European descent, so scores built from them work best for people of similar ancestry (Martin et al., https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6563838/). Data have since diversified somewhat, and companies say their newer scores travel better: Herasight reports its cognitive score keeps about 64 percent of its strength in African Americans and 59 percent in East Asian Americans in one US cohort, with wide uncertainty. The ASRM’s 2025 report cites the same limit as a reason polygenic screening is not ready for clinical use. Those who favor screening argue that the fix is more diverse data, not a ban; those who oppose it argue the gap means the product sold today benefits mostly white, wealthy parents.

- Label: Babies born after mitochondrial donation by pronuclear transfer at Newcastle
- Value: 8
- Unit: babies (born to 7 women)
- Data year: 2025
- Source label: Newcastle University press release on papers in the New England Journal of Medicine, July 16, 2025

- Value: 0.1
- Unit: percent lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia (from a baseline of 1)
- Comparison rows (same model, unit percent lifetime prevalence, baseline then after editing ten variants): coronary artery disease 6 to 0.1; type 2 diabetes 10 to 0.2; schizophrenia 1 to 0.1; Alzheimer’s disease 5 to under 0.6; major depression 15 to 9
- Data year: 2025 (theoretical model)
- Source label: Visscher, Gyngell, Yengo & Savulescu, “Heritable polygenic editing: the next frontier in genomic medicine?,” Nature, January 2025

- Note: A theoretical model predicted that editing ten variants in an embryo could cut lifetime risk of several common diseases more than tenfold, if those variants are truly causal.

The paper is the strongest statement of what heritable editing could eventually offer beyond selection, which can only choose among the variants parents already carry.

## Questions

### 1. How much can ranking embryos by polygenic scores actually change a child’s health or traits?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: meaningful gains / marginal and misleading
- Why contested: The scores are built from studies of adults and predict differences across a population reasonably well; what matters for selection is how well they predict differences among sibling embryos from the same parents, which is weaker for many outcomes (stat 11), and there are no long-term outcome data for children chosen this way. Published models give a few IQ points or centimeters of height (Karavani 2019) and relative disease-risk reductions of up to about half for some conditions but small absolute changes (Lencz 2021); companies report much larger gains from newer scores (Herasight 2026). The ASRM (2025), the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (2024) and the HFEA (2026) all conclude the evidence does not yet support clinical use.
- Meaningful gains: Those who see real value argue that the right comparison is across a lifetime of risks, not one disease: lowering scores for type 1 and type 2 diabetes, heart disease, several cancers and schizophrenia at once adds up, and a halving of relative risk matters greatly to a family with a history of the disease. Scores keep getting better as genetic datasets grow and diversify, and within-family tests of the newest scores show most of their predictive power survives between siblings for traits such as height and cognitive ability. Parents already choose among embryos on crude grounds such as appearance under a microscope; better information can only improve that choice, and as Herasight’s lab director put it to NPR, “For people that are risk averse and don’t want to simply roll the dice, they come to us.”
- Marginal and misleading: Those who doubt the value argue that the expected gains are small, apply on average across many selections rather than to any one child, and depend on having several healthy embryos, which many IVF patients do not have (stat 6). Scores are least accurate for people of non-European ancestry, overstate prediction for social outcomes because population studies capture family environment, and may raise one risk while lowering another, since many variants affect several conditions. As medical geneticist Susan Klugman told NPR, “we don’t know if you are also selecting for a certain disease or disorder,” and bioethicist James Tabery called it “this illusion of control that doesn’t actually exist.” Selling such products, on this view, gives false reassurance at high cost and may reduce the chance of a pregnancy by discarding usable embryos.

### 2. Should parents be free to choose embryos for traits that are not diseases, such as height or intelligence?
- Tag: value
- Poles: parents’ choice / medicine only
- Why contested: The same test that ranks embryos for diabetes risk can rank them for predicted height or educational attainment, so the line between medical and non-medical selection is a policy choice rather than a technical limit. US law draws no such line and companies sell trait prediction; UK law permits embryo testing only for listed medical purposes. Americans approve of screening for health conditions by large majorities but of screening for traits only by about a third (Furrer 2024), while 38 percent said they would likely use screening to raise a child’s educational prospects if it were free and safe (Meyer 2023).
- Parents’ choice: Those who favor freedom argue that parents already shape their children’s prospects in countless ways, through whom they marry, where they live, schooling and tutoring, and that choosing among their own embryos harms no existing person. Philosopher Julian Savulescu argues that prospective parents have strong reason to choose the child expected to have the best life, and that “we have a moral duty to pursue polygenic test scoring if it means ensuring a healthier life for future humans.” Traits such as intelligence are linked to health, income and longevity, so the medical line is blurry anyway. The state has no business ranking which reasons for choosing a child are acceptable, and Nucleus Genomics frames the matter as information: “Not every parent will want to use them, and that’s okay. But everyone deserves to know.”
- Medicine only: Those who would confine selection to medicine argue that treating children as products to be optimized changes the parent-child relationship from acceptance to specification, and that selecting for traits valued by markets, such as height or test scores, encodes social prejudice into who gets born. Unlike tutoring, the choice is made for a person who cannot consent and is irreversible. Widespread trait selection could become an arms race that pressures parents who would prefer not to take part: 73 percent of Americans expect that parents would feel pressure if gene editing for disease became widespread (Pew 2022). Medical uses rest on a shared understanding of serious disease; trait selection has no such limit, which is why most regulated systems refuse it.

### 3. Can editing embryos ever be made safe enough to start a pregnancy?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: provable with research / never safe enough
- Why contested: Early CRISPR experiments in human embryos produced frequent large unintended changes (Alanis-Lobato 2021; Zuccaro 2020); newer base editing is far more precise but still produced mosaic embryos and unintended edits in the September 2026 Columbia study. A biopsy samples only a few cells, so an embryo cannot be fully checked before transfer, and the only full test of safety is a child followed over a lifetime.
- Provable with research: Those who think it can be done argue that each generation of editors has been more precise than the last, that base and prime editing avoid the double-strand breaks that caused the worst damage, and that the remaining problems, such as mosaicism and off-target edits, are engineering problems that can be measured and reduced through laboratory work on donated embryos and stem cells. Medicine has accepted first-in-human risks before, from IVF itself to mitochondrial donation, after careful preclinical work. Origin Genomics CEO Cathy Tie argues that the barriers are “being overcome through advances in precision genome editing” and that “it is unethical to not pursue this research” if it could spare families devastating disease.
- Never safe enough: Those who doubt it argue that the bar for an elective procedure on a healthy future child, whose every cell and descendants would carry any error, is higher than for treating a sick patient, and that no laboratory test can establish long-term safety in advance. Mosaicism makes outcomes unpredictable even when the target edit works; Egli’s Columbia team said “Mosaicism creates a range of possibilities, making it impossible to predict outcomes.” The Center for Genetics and Society holds that editing embryos for reproduction “can likely never be fully safe.” The gene therapy industry’s own groups called in 2025 for a ten-year moratorium, saying “We simply lack the tools to make it safe now and for at least the next 10 years.”

### 4. Is a genetic change that passes to future generations different in kind from other medical choices?
- Tag: value
- Poles: a line not to cross / medicine like any other
- Why contested: Heritable editing is the only intervention discussed here whose effects are deliberately passed to people who are not yet conceived. The Council of Europe’s Oviedo Convention forbids genome modification aimed at descendants, and most countries with a policy agree (stat 1), but some ethicists argue that every reproductive choice, including choosing a partner or selecting an embryo, shapes descendants’ genes too. Pew found 52 percent of Americans choosing the statement that editing babies’ genes to reduce disease “is meddling with nature and crosses a line we should not cross.”
- A line not to cross: Those who see a categorical difference argue that heritable editing alters the shared human genome without the consent of any of the people affected, that its errors would multiply across generations, and that it puts one generation in the position of designing the next. Even medical uses would normalize the idea that people can be engineered, and history shows that judgments about which traits are defects shift with prejudice. The Catholic Church, the Center for Genetics and Society and many disability-rights advocates hold, for different reasons, that this is a boundary rather than a matter of risk.
- Medicine like any other: Those who see no fundamental difference argue that removing a mutation that causes Huntington’s disease or Tay-Sachs from a family line is a benefit to descendants, not a harm, and that future people cannot consent to any reproductive decision, including leaving a known mutation in place. Natural reproduction reshuffles genomes every generation, and selection already changes which variants are passed on. The International Commission on the Clinical Use of Heritable Human Genome Editing (2020) treated heritable editing as a medical intervention to be judged by safety and need, setting out a pathway “should a country decide to permit such uses.”

### 5. Does editing offer anything that embryo selection cannot?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: real unmet need / selection suffices
- Why contested: For almost all couples at risk of passing on a single-gene disease, some of their embryos are unaffected and can be found by existing testing, so the medical case for editing rests on rare situations and on future possibilities. A fertility-clinic analysis found the need for germline editing to prevent inherited disease “is typically grossly overestimated” because of the role of preimplantation testing (Viotti et al., 2019). Editing could, in principle, add variants neither parent carries, which selection cannot do (Visscher et al., 2025), but that use is speculative.
- Real unmet need: Those who see a need point to couples in which every embryo would be affected, for example when both parents have the same recessive disease or one parent carries two copies of a dominant mutation, and to patients, often older, who produce few embryos and cannot afford to discard affected ones. Correcting a mutation would rescue embryos rather than discard them, which matters to people who object to discarding embryos. Looking further ahead, editing a handful of protective variants could cut risk of common diseases far more than choosing among the few embryos a couple has (stat 17). The International Commission limited initial uses to couples who “have no option or extremely poor options for having a biologically related child who is not genetically affected.”
- Selection suffices: Those who see little need argue that the cases where selection cannot help are extremely rare and that donor eggs or sperm, adoption and future somatic therapies serve those families without the risks of editing. David Barrett, chief executive of the American Society of Gene & Cell Therapy, said “There is no compelling medical need to use HHGE.” Egli, whose lab does the editing research, said “When other technologies without this risk are available to prevent disease, gene editing is not the method of choice.” On this view the push for editing is driven by the commercial and enhancement uses that selection cannot deliver, which is why gene-editing pioneer Fyodor Urnov said of the startups, “I just don’t think they’re being honest with us about why they’re really doing this.”

### 6. Would widely available embryo selection entrench inequality or spread its benefits?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: entrenches advantage / spreads as costs fall
- Why contested: Today the services cost thousands to tens of thousands of dollars on top of IVF (stat 13), are paid out of pocket, and work best for people of European ancestry (stat 12). Whether that stays true depends on future prices, insurance coverage and public policy that no one can yet observe; the Visscher et al. (2025) model found that individually beneficial editing could deepen health inequalities.
- Entrenches advantage: Those who expect entrenched inequality argue that the people who can afford IVF and screening are already advantaged, that any real gains in health or educational outcomes would compound existing privilege across generations, and that scores built mostly from European data deliver the least benefit to the groups already worst served by medicine. The Center for Genetics and Society warns of a world of genetic “haves” and “have nots,” and Savulescu himself acknowledged concern that “already worsening inequality might become cemented.” Over time, groups that select could diverge from groups that do not, turning social inequality into biological inequality.
- Spreads as costs fall: Those who expect spread argue that new technologies typically start expensive and become cheap: genome sequencing costs fell by orders of magnitude, and IVF went from experimental to routine, now accounting for about 2.6 percent of US births (stat 4). Inequality is a reason to fund access, not to forbid the technology, and selection could even reduce inequality by giving everyone a shot at genes that today are distributed by luck. Banning it in one country simply sends the wealthy abroad, as UK couples sending embryo data to US companies already do.

### 7. Does screening embryos for conditions express that people living with them are worth less?
- Tag: value
- Poles: devalues disabled lives / prevents suffering
- Why contested: Disability-rights scholars and advocates argue that choosing against embryos with a condition sends a message about people who have it and can reduce the support and community available to them; others answer that preferring a child without a painful disease is like preferring health in any other choice. The US National Council on Disability’s 2024 report warns that commercial heritable editing could lead to “even greater health disparities, if not the complete eradication of their populations” for people with disabilities (https://www.ncd.gov/assets/uploads/reports/2024/ncd-fetal-surgery-genetic-editing.pdf). The argument now extends to autism, which Herasight began screening for in October 2026.
- Devalues disabled lives: Those who see devaluation argue that many conditions screened for are compatible with good lives, that the suffering associated with disability comes largely from social barriers rather than the condition itself, and that marketing embryo selection as a path to a “healthy baby” trades on stereotypes and fear. Each screened-out condition becomes rarer, research and services for it shrink, and remaining families face the implied question of why they did not prevent it. The National Council on Disability describes many disabled people as valuing disability as a meaningful part of their identity and a source of community and pride, and recommends that people with disabilities have a decision-making role in how these technologies are governed.
- Prevents suffering: Those who see prevention argue that choosing an embryo without Tay-Sachs or Huntington’s disease is an act of care for the future child, not a judgment on anyone now living, just as taking folic acid to prevent spina bifida is not. Parents choosing among embryos are choosing which child to have, not ending an existing life, and they bear the consequences. Many families with inherited disease, such as those who campaigned for mitochondrial donation in the UK, seek these tools precisely because they know the conditions firsthand. Respect for disabled people is owed to people, and is compatible with wanting one’s own child to avoid a serious disease.

### 8. Who should set the limits: binding law, professional bodies, or parents and their doctors?
- Tag: value
- Poles: binding law / parents and doctors
- Why contested: The models differ sharply. The UK licenses each permitted test through a statutory regulator; much of Europe bans heritable editing by treaty; the United States relies on professional guidance, a spending rider and the market, and a 2025 court ruling left laboratory-developed tests outside FDA review. Professional statements against polygenic screening (ASRM, ACMG) have not stopped companies from selling it in the US.
- Binding law: Those who want binding law argue that professional self-regulation failed to stop He Jiankui and is not stopping commercial trait prediction, and that decisions about which children may be engineered or selected affect everyone and should be made democratically, not by companies or individual clinics. The ten-year moratorium proposed by gene therapy groups calls for strengthening national and international legal prohibitions, and the Center for Genetics and Society says “Stronger prohibitions in the US and internationally are needed.” A clear statutory line, like the UK’s, also protects patients from paying for unproven tests.
- Parents and doctors: Those who prefer leaving decisions to parents and their doctors argue that reproduction is the most private of choices and that legislatures are poorly placed to judge fast-moving science or to rank parents’ reasons. Rigid statutes freeze yesterday’s science: the UK regulator’s finding that polygenic testing is unlawful rests on a statute last substantially amended in 2008, before such testing existed. Truth-in-advertising rules and professional standards can police false claims without banning a lawful choice, and bans push patients abroad to less careful providers, the “genome-editing tourism” that bioethicist R. Alta Charo told NPR she is “very concerned about.”

### 9. Does the moral status of embryos make creating and discarding them to choose among them wrong?
- Tag: value
- Poles: embryos are persons / embryos are not yet persons
- Why contested: Every form of selection, and most editing research, creates more embryos than will be transferred and sets some aside or destroys them. Whether that is acceptable depends on when a human being acquires moral status, a question that divides religious traditions, philosophers and the public, and that an Alabama Supreme Court ruling treating frozen embryos as having the rights of people brought into US politics shortly before the Southern Baptist Convention’s 2024 resolution (PBS NewsHour, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-southern-baptists-resolution-on-ivf).
- Embryos are persons: Those who hold that embryos are human beings from fertilization argue that ranking them by predicted health or traits and discarding the rest treats some human lives as unworthy of continuing. The Catholic Church teaches that preimplantation diagnosis “is therefore the expression of a eugenic mentality” (Dignitas Personae, 2008), and the Southern Baptist Convention’s 2024 resolution says IVF “increasingly engages in dehumanizing methods for determining suitability for life and genetic sorting, based on notions of genetic fitness and parental preferences.” On this view the question of which embryos to choose never arises, because no embryo should be created in order to be judged.
- Embryos are not yet persons: Those who hold that early embryos are not yet persons argue that a five-day blastocyst of a hundred or so cells, with no nervous system, has a different moral status from a fetus or a baby, and that many embryos fail naturally. On this view what matters morally is the welfare of the child who will be born and of the parents, and choosing the embryo most likely to become a healthy child is responsible, not dehumanizing. IVF as practiced in the United States and most other countries rests on this view, and laws such as the UK’s allow embryos to be tested, selected and discarded within set limits.

## Positions

### 1. Allow only proven uses against serious disease
- Adherents: American Society for Reproductive Medicine (institution; the main US professional society for fertility medicine, Ethics and Practice Committees report, December 8, 2025), https://www.asrm.org/news-and-events/asrm-news/press-releasesbulletins/asrm-ethics-and-practice-committees-release-new-report-concluding-polygenic-embryo-screening-is-not-ready-for-clinical-use ; International Commission on the Clinical Use of Heritable Human Genome Editing (institution; convened by the US National Academy of Medicine, US National Academy of Sciences and the UK Royal Society, report of September 2020), https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2020/09/heritable-genome-editing-not-yet-ready-to-be-tried-safely-and-effectively-in-humans-initial-clinical-uses-if-permitted-should-be-limited-to-serious-single-gene-diseases ; Paul Knoepfler (stem cell biologist, University of California, Davis; STAT opinion, June 24, 2026), https://www.statnews.com/2026/06/24/embryo-editing-dieter-egli-ethical-boundaries-moratorium/ . Also the organizers of the Third International Summit on Human Genome Editing (2023), the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, and Columbia embryologist Dieter Egli.
- Case in the camp’s voice: Embryo testing is one of the great successes of reproductive medicine. Couples who carry the mutations for cystic fibrosis, Tay-Sachs or Huntington’s disease can now have children free of those diseases by choosing among their own embryos, and that should remain available and be better supported. The test for any new genetic choice is the same: is it aimed at serious disease, and has it been shown to work and to be safe? Polygenic screening fails the second test today. It predicts small average effects, works poorly for many ancestries and can cost patients usable embryos, which is why the ASRM concluded it is “not ready for clinical practice” and that “Any use of PGT-P should occur only in research settings under Institutional Review Board oversight.” Heritable editing fails it too, for now: the organizers of the 2023 international summit said “Heritable human genome editing remains unacceptable at this time,” and the newest, most precise embryo edits still produce mosaic embryos and unintended changes. That is a reason for rigorous laboratory research, not for prohibition forever. If editing can one day be shown to be precise and safe, initial uses should be limited, as the International Commission recommended, to serious single-gene diseases and to couples who “have no option or extremely poor options for having a biologically related child who is not genetically affected,” with long-term follow-up of every child. Choosing embryos for height, intelligence or eye color is not medicine and should not be offered as if it were.
- Variation inside the camp: The camp differs on timing and on how open the door should stay. The Alliance for Regenerative Medicine, the International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy and the American Society of Gene & Cell Therapy want a ten-year global moratorium on heritable editing and stronger legal prohibitions in the meantime, and some of their leaders doubt it will ever be needed; Knoepfler supports a temporary moratorium but not a ban on laboratory embryo research. At the other end, startups such as Origin Genomics and Preventive accept the limit to severe inherited disease but argue the safety research should move now and that the US rider should eventually be lifted so the FDA can review trials; Origin’s founder Cathy Tie says “it is unethical to not pursue this research.” Critics of those startups, including gene-editing pioneer Fyodor Urnov, doubt that their stated limit to disease will hold. On selection, the camp includes both those who would allow polygenic screening once validated for serious disease and those who favor a legal list of permitted purposes on the UK model.
- Best evidence: The record of PGT-M in preventing serious single-gene disease; the professional consensus against clinical polygenic screening (ASRM 2025, ACMG 2024, HFEA 2026); the modest and uncertain gains in published models (Karavani 2019, Lencz 2021); the unintended changes found in edited human embryos (Alanis-Lobato 2021, Zuccaro 2020, Columbia 2026); the UK’s licensed mitochondrial donation program, with eight babies born, as a model of a cautious, regulated pathway; and public opinion that strongly favors screening for health conditions but not for traits (Furrer 2024).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that “serious disease” and “proven” are moving lines that will give way to commercial pressure, or alternatively that they paternalistically block parents’ choices): Lines drawn by law and licensing do hold where they exist. The UK has allowed embryo testing only for listed serious conditions for decades, and its regulator has now refused polygenic testing even when done abroad. To the other side, a requirement of evidence is not paternalism but the ordinary standard of medicine, which protects patients from paying for tests that do not do what they promise.

### 2. Let parents choose their children’s traits
- Adherents: Nucleus Genomics (institution; New York company selling embryo analysis for health risks and traits; “Have Your Best Baby” campaign, November 2025), https://mynucleus.com/have-your-best-baby-2025 ; Julian Savulescu (philosopher, originator of the principle of procreative beneficence; IAI News essay, September 28, 2021), https://iai.tv/articles/the-moral-case-for-eugenics-auid-1916 . Also Herasight, which sells embryo predictions of IQ, height and disease risk (https://herasight.substack.com/p/cogpgtpub).
- Case in the camp’s voice: Every parent wants the best for their child, and nobody objects when parents pay for tutoring, braces or a better school district. Choosing, among the embryos a couple has already made, the one with the best predicted health and prospects is no different in kind, and it harms no one: the alternative is not a different life for the same child but a different child chosen by chance. The science is real and improving. Polygenic scores already identify embryos at lower risk of diabetes, heart disease and schizophrenia, and the newest scores for cognitive ability hold up within families, where it counts. Savulescu argues that “we have a moral duty to pursue polygenic test scoring if it means ensuring a healthier life for future humans.” What eugenics got wrong was state coercion; the opposite of eugenics is parents freely choosing for their own families. Nucleus’s founder Kian Sadeghi puts the goal simply: “We help people have their best babies.” People should be told what the technology can and cannot do, and then left to decide: “Not every parent will want to use them, and that’s okay. But everyone deserves to know.”
- Variation inside the camp: The camp divides on how far and how fast. Herasight presents its predictions with explicit caveats, calling them “expected values across many selections, not guarantees for any individual case”; Nucleus markets more broadly, including eye color and other cosmetic traits. Some, including Savulescu, who co-authored a 2022 Human Reproduction paper titled “Polygenic risk score for embryo selection: not ready for prime time” (https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1689405-polygenic-risk-score-for-embryo-selection-not-ready-for-prime-time), accept the principle while wanting better evidence and regulation, and argue the technology should be treated like other universal health measures so that it does not entrench inequality. Some in the camp extend the principle to future embryo editing for disease resistance or traits; others limit it to choosing among existing embryos.
- Best evidence: Within-family validation of newer polygenic scores (Herasight 2026; Howe et al. 2022 showing little shrinkage for height); model estimates of meaningful relative risk reductions when choosing the lowest-risk embryo (Lencz 2021); public willingness to use screening (82 percent of a representative sample interested if already doing IVF, Furrer 2024; 38 percent likely to use it for educational prospects, Meyer 2023); and non-medical sex selection, which most US clinics offer (Capelouto 2018) and which the camp cites as a choice the United States has long left to parents.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that trait selection is eugenics that will entrench inequality and devalue people with disabilities): Eugenics was the state deciding who could reproduce; this is individual parents deciding which of their own embryos to carry, with no one forced to take part. Inequality is a reason to make these tools cheaper and more widely available, not to forbid them to everyone. And choosing a healthier embryo says nothing about the worth of people now living, any more than vaccinating a child insults people who had polio.

### 3. Ban heritable editing and trait selection outright
- Adherents: Center for Genetics and Society (institution; Berkeley-based public-interest group on human genetics and assisted reproduction; statement of June 6, 2026), https://www.geneticsandsociety.org/press-statement/center-genetics-and-society-comment-base-editing-embryo-genes-engineered-babies ; Autistic Self Advocacy Network (institution; organization run by and for autistic people; submission to the International Commission, 2019), https://autisticadvocacy.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/ASAN-response-to-the-public-call-for-evidence.pdf ; Katie Hasson (executive director, Center for Genetics and Society), quoted at https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/29/origin-genomics-mission-to-make-healthy-gene-edited-babies/ . Also the Gender Justice and Disability Rights Coalition on Heritable Genome Editing, and, on editing, Senator John Cornyn.
- Case in the camp’s voice: The question is not whether genetic engineering of children can be made a little safer but whether a society that believes in equal human worth should permit it at all. Heritable editing would change people who cannot consent and all their descendants, and in the Center for Genetics and Society’s words it “remains unacceptably risky and can likely never be fully safe.” It is also unnecessary: embryo screening already lets nearly every at-risk couple have an unaffected child. What is driving the push is not unmet medical need but a market in “optimized” babies backed by technology fortunes, the same market that already sells IQ and height predictions for embryos. That is a commercial revival of eugenics, one that would sort children by traits rich parents value, deepen inequality between genetic haves and have-nots, and tell people with disabilities that their lives were a mistake to be prevented. The Autistic Self Advocacy Network envisions “a world in which all lives - including the lives of people with disabilities - have equal value” and holds that “Such a world is simply not compatible with the use of technology to prevent the births of people with disabilities.” Soft guidance and professional statements have not stopped the companies; “Stronger prohibitions in the US and internationally are needed,” including a binding international agreement and a US statute rather than a rider renewed each year.
- Variation inside the camp: The camp agrees on prohibiting heritable editing for good and on stopping commercial trait selection, but differs on embryo screening for disease. The Center for Genetics and Society treats screening for serious inherited disease as a safe alternative to editing and focuses its objection on enhancement and commercialization; the Autistic Self Advocacy Network and many disability-rights advocates also object to selecting against disabilities such as autism or Down syndrome, which they see as identities rather than defects. The camp spans the political spectrum on editing: progressive social-justice groups stress inequality and disability rights, while some conservatives, such as Cornyn, who wrote to the health secretary in December 2025 that “Scientific advancement cannot devolve into evil and immoral eugenic practices,” stress human dignity. The Center for Genetics and Society frames its work within reproductive justice and does not oppose IVF, which sets the camp’s core apart from the fourth camp.
- Best evidence: The rarity of cases where screening cannot avoid a single-gene disease (Viotti et al. 2019); the persistent unintended changes in edited embryos, including with base editing (Columbia 2026); the global legal consensus, with 75 of 78 countries that have a relevant policy prohibiting heritable editing (Baylis et al. 2020); the concentration of benefits among wealthy parents of European ancestry (Martin et al. 2019; stat 13); and the history of twentieth-century eugenics, which also presented itself as science serving better children.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that a ban denies families a chance to spare their children serious disease and pushes the work abroad): Families at risk already have safe options in embryo screening, donor gametes and, increasingly, somatic therapies that treat the person without altering descendants. Bans do not prevent all abuse, but they define what is legitimate, deny rogue actors funding and respectability, and make clear that a He Jiankui will be prosecuted, not celebrated. An international agreement, which most countries already approximate in law, is the answer to editing tourism.

### 4. Stop discarding embryos to choose among them
- Adherents: The Catholic Church (institution; Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, instruction Dignitas Personae, 2008), https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20081208_dignitas-personae_en.html ; Southern Baptist Convention (institution; resolution “On the Ethical Realities of Reproductive Technologies and the Dignity of the Human Embryo,” June 2024), https://www.sbc.net/resource-library/resolutions/on-the-ethical-realities-of-reproductive-technologies-and-the-dignity-of-the-human-embryo/ . Also the resolution’s drafters, Albert Mohler and Andrew Walker of Southern Baptist Theological Seminary.
- Case in the camp’s voice: The debate between screening for disease and screening for traits misses the prior question. Every embryo is a human life from fertilization, with the same dignity as any child, whether it is in a womb or a laboratory dish. Embryo selection works by creating more lives than will be carried, ranking them by their genes and discarding or freezing indefinitely those judged less fit. The Catholic Church teaches that preimplantation diagnosis “is therefore the expression of a eugenic mentality,” because it makes the right to be born depend on health and normality, and that “germ line cell therapy in all its forms is morally illicit.” The Southern Baptist Convention warns that IVF “increasingly engages in dehumanizing methods for determining suitability for life and genetic sorting, based on notions of genetic fitness and parental preferences,” and calls on believers “to only utilize reproductive technologies consistent with that affirmation,” limiting the number of embryos created. Polygenic ranking and editing are simply the latest steps down a road that began when human beginnings were moved into the laboratory and made subject to quality control. Compassion for infertile couples and for families facing inherited disease is owed through care, support and adoption, including of frozen embryos, not through choosing which of their children may live.
- Variation inside the camp: Catholic teaching rejects IVF itself, as well as preimplantation diagnosis and germline modification. The Southern Baptist resolution does not forbid IVF; it urges couples to create only as many embryos as they will transfer, which in practice rules out selection among many embryos, and some members accept IVF on that basis while others avoid it entirely. Catholic teaching judges germline therapy illicit because its risks to offspring are not controllable in the present state of research, and separately condemns genetic engineering for non-medical purposes as promoting a eugenic mentality.
- Best evidence: The structure of the techniques themselves, which depend on creating surplus embryos (the gains in stats 2 and 3 assume five or more to choose from); the large numbers of embryos held in frozen storage, which the Southern Baptist resolution calls stockpiling; the expansion of selection from serious disease to sex, cosmetic traits and intelligence, which the camp sees as the predictable result of accepting selection at all; and the ease with which selection against disability became routine in prenatal testing.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that early embryos are not persons and that the position denies help to suffering families): The moral status of a human life does not depend on its size or stage of development; each of us was once an embryo, and a principle that allows discarding embryos for being less fit has no stopping point. The camp does not deny help to families: it supports medical care, research into treatments for the living, somatic therapies, and adoption, and it asks only that help not come by deciding which human beings are worth keeping.

## Crux

The question under the others: when, if ever, is choosing which genes a child will have an act of care for that child rather than a judgment about which people should exist, and how much can today’s tools actually deliver?

The part evidence can settle is what the tools do. How much does choosing among a couple’s embryos by polygenic score change the health and traits of the children actually born, measured within families and across ancestries, and how often does it cost a couple their only usable embryo? Can embryo editing be made precise enough that every cell carries the intended change and nothing else, and what happens to the health of children followed over decades? How many couples have no way to an unaffected child except editing? Do the services stay with wealthy parents or spread as prices fall, and does selecting against a condition shrink the support available to people who live with it? Studies under way, from within-family validation of scores to base-editing research on donated embryos and follow-up of the UK’s mitochondrial donation children, can narrow each of these. The first camp’s position is explicitly conditional on these answers, and much of the second camp’s case rests on them: proof of large, safe benefits would meet the conditions cautious professional bodies have set for disease uses, and evidence of small or harmful effects would undercut the case for selling trait prediction now.

The part no data can close is the weighing. Even with perfect prediction and safe editing, people disagree about whether choosing a child’s height or intelligence is like choosing a school or like ranking human worth; whether a line between preventing serious disease and choosing traits is principled or arbitrary; whether people not yet born, and their descendants, are wronged by changes they could not consent to; whether parents’ freedom or society’s commitment to the equal value of disabled lives should prevail when they conflict; and, for some, whether an early embryo is already a person whose creation and discard for the sake of choosing is itself the wrong. A person who thinks polygenic scores work well can still oppose trait selection on principle, and a person who thinks they barely work can still defend parents’ right to use them.
