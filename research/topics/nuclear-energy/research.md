# Nuclear Energy — research

## Overview

Nuclear energy here means fission reactors used to generate electricity: the roughly 420 commercial power reactors operating in 31 countries, the plants being built or restarted, the designs being licensed, the fuel chain that supplies them and the waste they leave behind. The question the topic turns on is how large a role fission should play in electricity systems that are trying to cut carbon emissions, keep the lights on and hold costs down at the same time. The scope deliberately excludes nuclear weapons, weapons proliferation as a security question in its own right, naval and space reactors, and fusion, which is a different physical process with a different cost and safety profile. Proliferation appears only where it bears on civilian reactor and fuel-cycle choices, which is how the argument itself uses it.

Why now: on 8 September 2026 the US Department of Energy closed a loan of up to $1.9 billion to NextEra Energy to restart the Duane Arnold Energy Center, a 615 MW reactor in Linn County, Iowa that shut in 2020, with Google signing a 25-year power purchase agreement for the output (US Department of Energy, https://www.energy.gov/articles/energy-department-closes-19-billion-loan-restart-duane-arnold-nuclear-plant). It is the third shuttered US reactor to get federal restart financing, after Holtec’s Palisades in Michigan and Constellation’s Crane Clean Energy Center — the former Three Mile Island Unit 1 — which is contracted to Microsoft. No US reactor in decommissioning has ever been brought back; Palisades would be the first, and as of mid-September 2026 it had loaded fuel and passed its major restart milestones but had not yet returned to the grid, having missed targets of late 2025 and February 2026. [Correction 2026-10-05: as of September 2026 Palisades had not run and had paused fuel loading when a fuel assembly tilted; it had not completed fuel load. Source as given on the page: US Department of Energy, https://www.energy.gov/articles/energy-department-closes-19-billion-loan-restart-duane-arnold-nuclear-plant.]

The debate is live because three things changed at once. Electricity demand started growing again in rich countries after two decades of flat load, driven substantially by data centers; the IEA projects global data center consumption roughly doubling to about 945 TWh by 2030. Governments reversed long-standing positions: 38 countries have now signed the COP28 Declaration to Triple Nuclear Energy by 2050, the World Bank ended its ban on financing nuclear projects in June 2025, and the US set a goal of 400 GW of nuclear capacity by 2050 in executive orders signed 23 May 2025. At the same time the two large Western reactor projects completed or nearing completion in this cycle — Vogtle 3 and 4 in Georgia and Hinkley Point C in England — came in at roughly two to three times their original budgets, which is the central fact the skeptical side points at. The participants are national governments and regulators, utilities and reactor vendors, hyperscale technology companies buying firm power, environmental organizations on both sides of the question, and the energy-system modelers whose cost assumptions largely determine what the models recommend.

## Questions

Question: Can the cost of building a new reactor in a Western country come down far enough to matter?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Learning curve works / Structurally expensive
Why contested: Everyone agrees Vogtle at $35 billion and Hinkley Point C at £35 billion in 2015 prices are too expensive to replicate. The disagreement is whether that price is a property of the technology under Western conditions or a property of having built nothing for thirty years. No country outside Asia has built enough units in sequence recently to settle it.
Case for the learning curve: Nuclear construction costs are dominated by labor productivity, rework and financing time, all of which improve with repetition. Vogtle 4 cost roughly 30 percent less than Vogtle 3 on the same site with the same crews; France built 56 reactors in about 15 years by standardizing designs and keeping one workforce continuously employed; South Korea delivered Barakah’s four units in the UAE on a recognizable schedule; China builds in about six years at $2,000 to $3,000 per kW. The pattern in every case is the same: the nth unit of a standard design costs a fraction of the first. What the West has, on this reading, is not an expensive technology but an atrophied supply chain, a regulator geared to one-off review, and no order book long enough to justify investment in either. Fix the order book and the cost follows.
Case for structural expense: Nuclear is the only large energy technology whose real costs have risen over time in most countries that have built it, including France, whose later reactors cost more than its earlier ones. The reasons are not obviously fixable: reactors are large, safety-critical, site-built objects that cannot be factory-produced the way solar modules or turbine nacelles are, so they capture very little of the learning that mass manufacturing delivers. Regulatory ratchets follow each accident, and liberalized electricity markets will not finance a fifteen-year payback without a guaranteed price. China and Korea build cheaply under state financing, state land assembly and state liability, which is a description of a political system as much as an industry. The honest conclusion from the last twenty years is that the learning curve is a hypothesis that has been tested and has not held outside command economies.

Question: Does a decarbonized grid need firm low-carbon generation, or can renewables plus storage do it alone?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Firm power needed / Renewables suffice
Why contested: Every energy-system model answers this, and the answers differ mainly because of assumptions about long-duration storage costs, transmission buildout and how bad the worst wind-and-solar drought is. No large grid has yet run at 100 percent variable renewables for a year, so the question remains partly unsettled by evidence.
Case for firm power: Wind and solar output varies by week and season, not just by hour. Northern Europe has multi-day wind lulls in winter under high pressure, when demand peaks and solar contributes almost nothing; covering those with batteries means storing energy for days, and lithium-ion economics degrade sharply past about eight hours. Models that force the last 10 to 20 percent of emissions out of a system consistently find that adding some clean firm resource — nuclear, geothermal, hydrogen or gas with carbon capture — lowers total system cost substantially, because it removes the need to build three or four times peak renewable capacity plus vast storage for rare events. Nuclear is the only clean firm option deployed at scale anywhere today.
Case for renewables sufficing: The cost of the alternatives keeps falling faster than the models assume. Solar fell about 90 percent in a decade and added a record 647 GW globally in 2025; battery costs have fallen comparably, and long-duration chemistries, demand response, interconnection across weather regions, and overbuilding with curtailment all attack the same problem from different directions. Several detailed studies find fully renewable systems feasible at costs comparable to mixed systems, and the empirical trend is that each year’s study finds the renewable path cheaper than last year’s did. Building a fifteen-year nuclear project to insure against a gap that cheaper technologies may close in the meantime is an expensive bet on today’s cost curves being wrong.

Question: Should public money underwrite new nuclear construction?
Kind: value
Poles: Public backing justified / Let markets decide
Why contested: Almost no new reactor anywhere has been financed on purely commercial terms. Whether that is a market failure worth correcting or a verdict worth accepting is a judgment about what governments are for, not a fact about reactors.
Case for public backing: Electricity systems are public infrastructure and always have been; governments already shape them through carbon prices, renewable mandates, capacity markets, transmission planning and liability caps for every technology. Nuclear’s particular problem is that its costs are almost all upfront and its benefits — reliability, emissions avoided, fuel security, a domestic industrial base — are spread across decades and partly not captured by the builder. That is a textbook case for public risk-sharing, of exactly the kind that created the shale industry, the offshore wind industry and the solar manufacturing base. The World Bank reached this conclusion in June 2025 when it ended its ban on nuclear lending.
Case for letting markets decide: Subsidy shifts risk from investors to taxpayers and ratepayers, and the record of nuclear risk transfer is bad: South Carolina customers are still paying for the $9 billion VC Summer project that produced nothing. Nuclear has received public support continuously since the 1950s — research funding, loan guarantees, the Price-Anderson liability cap, waste management, and now production tax credits — and still cannot raise private capital on its own terms after seventy years. At some point continued support stops being an infant-industry argument. If the technology is genuinely the cheapest way to deliver clean firm power, a technology-neutral carbon price or clean firm procurement should reveal that without singling nuclear out.

Question: How should the risk of a severe accident be weighed against the routine harms of the alternatives?
Kind: value
Poles: Catastrophe is different / Count the bodies
Why contested: The statistics are not seriously in dispute — nuclear kills far fewer people per unit of energy than fossil fuels and is comparable to wind and solar. What is disputed is whether a small probability of an event that displaces a hundred thousand people and contaminates a region for decades is the same kind of harm as a steady toll of air pollution deaths, and whether it is legitimate for a society to weight it more heavily.
Case that catastrophe is different: Expected-value arithmetic flattens distinctions that people care about. A reactor accident is involuntary, concentrated, irreversible on a human timescale, and imposed on a specific place rather than distributed thinly. Fukushima produced almost no radiation deaths but forced about 160,000 people from their homes, and the evacuation itself killed more than 2,300; Chernobyl’s exclusion zone is still closed forty years later. Populations reasonably pay a premium to avoid tail risks in insurance, finance and medicine, and it is not irrational to do the same here — particularly since the harm falls on the host community while the benefits are spread across a grid.
Case for counting the bodies: Air pollution from coal and gas kills millions of people a year, quietly, and nobody evacuates. If the standard is lives lost, nuclear at about 0.03 deaths per TWh is among the safest sources ever measured, and every reactor not built is in practice replaced by something that kills more. The dread premium is not free: it is paid in cancers and heart disease in the towns downwind of coal plants, which are usually poorer than the towns near reactors. Worse, the premium may be self-inflicted. The Fukushima evacuation was driven by radiation-protection standards built on linear-no-threshold assumptions that UNSCEAR itself warns against extrapolating to low doses; a proportionate response would have killed far fewer people than the evacuation did.

Question: Are small modular and advanced reactors a genuine break from the cost problem, or the same problem in smaller units?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Factory build changes it / Same economics, less scale
Why contested: No SMR is operating commercially in a Western country, so the entire case rests on projections. The first real data points — Darlington in Ontario, Kemmerer in Wyoming — arrive around 2030 and 2031.
Case that factory build changes it: The thing that makes reactors expensive is building an enormous one-of-a-kind object outdoors, on site, under weather and labor-market conditions nobody controls. Shrink the unit until it fits on a truck and the work moves into a factory, where the aviation and shipbuilding learning rates apply, quality is controlled, and unit 20 is genuinely cheaper than unit 1. Smaller units also cut the financing problem: a $1 billion commitment with a four-year build is a decision a utility board can actually make, where a $15 billion commitment with a twelve-year build is not. Passive safety in these designs removes whole classes of active systems and, with them, cost. Ontario is building four BWRX-300 units; TerraPower has an NRC construction permit for Kemmerer Unit 1 and a January 2026 agreement with Meta for eight plants.
Case for the same economics: Economies of scale are real and run the other way — per kilowatt, a small reactor needs more containment, more staff, more security and more regulatory attention per unit of output than a large one. Every published cost estimate, including those from SMR vendors, shows higher capital cost per installed kilowatt than a large reactor; the bet is entirely that serial manufacturing will beat that penalty, and serial manufacturing requires hundreds of orders that do not exist. NuScale’s Carbon Free Power Project, the furthest-advanced US SMR, was cancelled in 2023 after its target price rose over 50 percent with no units built. Ontario’s four-unit Darlington project is budgeted at CAD 20.9 billion, roughly CAD 17,000 per kW, which is not an obviously cheap number. Small reactors have been built before, in the 1950s and 1960s, and the industry moved to large units precisely because they were cheaper per kilowatt.

Question: Does building civilian nuclear power meaningfully increase the risk of weapons proliferation?
Kind: empirical
Poles: Real proliferation channel / Separable problem
Why contested: Historically, several weapons programs grew out of civilian infrastructure, and several did not need it. The question is whether the next wave of newcomer countries, and the enrichment and reprocessing capacity that serves them, changes the odds.
Case that it is a real channel: Enrichment and reprocessing are the same technologies whether the output is fuel or weapons material, and a country with a civilian program acquires the expertise, the materials handling and the political cover. India’s first device used plutonium from a research reactor supplied for peaceful use; Iran’s enrichment program is nominally civilian; North Korea’s reactor was built on a research design. As nuclear power spreads to states with weaker institutions and unresolved regional rivalries, and as advanced designs create demand for high-assay fuel enriched closer to weapons grade, the safeguards burden grows. Rosatom’s practice of bundling reactor exports with fuel and financing also gives one state leverage over a widening set of clients.
Case that it is separable: Most states that pursued weapons did so through dedicated military programs, not diverted power reactors, because power reactors are conspicuous, safeguarded and produce poor-quality plutonium. Light-water reactors fueled with low-enriched uranium supplied and taken back under IAEA safeguards offer almost no practical route to a weapon; the UAE built a large program under a legally binding commitment not to enrich or reprocess, and the model worked. The binding constraint on proliferation is political will and access to enrichment, which the non-proliferation regime addresses directly through safeguards, export controls and fuel-supply guarantees. Refusing to build civilian reactors in countries that want clean power does not prevent a determined state from seeking weapons; it only removes the leverage that comes with being the supplier.

Question: Should aging reactors have their licenses extended to 60 or 80 years?
Kind: value
Poles: Extend while safe / Retire on schedule
Why contested: The original 40-year license terms were not engineering limits, and components that cannot be replaced — chiefly the reactor pressure vessel, embrittled by decades of neutron flux, and buried concrete and cabling — are the subjects of genuine technical uncertainty rather than settled knowledge. The judgment about how much residual uncertainty is acceptable is not purely technical.
Case for extending: Life extension is the cheapest low-carbon electricity available anywhere: a fully depreciated reactor runs at $26 to $36 per MWh and emits nothing. Retiring a functioning reactor almost always means burning more gas, which is what happened in Germany, Japan and New York. Regulators do not extend licenses casually; the US process requires aging-management programs, inspections and component replacement, and 67 percent of world nuclear capacity is already over 30 years old and operating with excellent load factors. Refusing to extend is choosing higher emissions and higher prices in exchange for a risk reduction nobody has quantified.
Case for retiring on schedule: Every component in a reactor was qualified against a design life, and running to 80 years means operating machinery far outside the envelope in which its materials behavior was characterized. Embrittlement, concrete degradation, cable insulation and corrosion under insulation are known aging mechanisms with limited operating experience at these durations, and the economic pressure on regulators to approve extensions is enormous and growing as demand rises. The Palisades restart has already drawn scrutiny over steam generator weld documentation. A fleet whose average age keeps climbing is also a fleet whose failure probability is rising against a baseline nobody can validate, and the cost of being wrong is not symmetric.

Question: Who should decide whether a reactor gets built — the host community or the national government?
Kind: value
Poles: Local consent required / National interest governs
Why contested: Nuclear polls well nationally and worse locally, and the costs and benefits fall on different populations. Every country that builds successfully has had to resolve this, and they have resolved it differently.
Case for local consent: The community hosts the risk, the construction, the waste and the emergency planning zone, while the electricity and the climate benefit go to everyone. Consent-based siting is not only fairer, it works better: Finland’s Onkalo repository was built because Eurajoki agreed to host it, while Yucca Mountain has consumed nearly $15 billion and never opened because Nevada never did. Imposing facilities produces litigation, delay and cost, which is the opposite of what a fast buildout needs.
Case for national interest: Grid reliability and emissions are national problems, and a veto held by every potential host is a veto on the whole program — the same dynamic that has stalled transmission lines, wind farms and housing. Local opposition is systematically unrepresentative, drawing on the minority with time and money to object, and it is often informed by risk perceptions that regulators have examined and rejected. France built its fleet, and Ontario is building its SMRs, on the strength of national decisions with local consultation rather than local consent. A country that grants a veto to every neighborhood has decided, in effect, not to build anything.

## Positions

Camp: Build new reactors with public backing
Adherents: US Department of Energy — https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/9-key-takeaways-president-trumps-executive-orders-nuclear-energy ; World Nuclear Association — https://world-nuclear.org/our-association/publications/world-nuclear-outlook-report/global-energy-developments ; also the governments of France, Canada, South Korea, Poland and the Czech Republic, Ontario Power Generation, Électricité de France, and the 38 signatories of the COP28 tripling declaration.
The case in the camp’s own voice: We already know how to decarbonize a large grid with fission, because France did it in fifteen years and is still running on it. What we lost was not the technology but the industrial capacity to deliver it — the welders, the forgings, the project managers, the regulator geared to serial rather than one-off review. Vogtle is what it costs to rebuild all of that from a standing start with a single order; it is not what the tenth unit costs, and Vogtle 4 already came in about 30 percent below Vogtle 3. The answer is to order proven designs in fleets, on the same sites, with the same crews, backed by the kind of long-horizon public financing that every piece of national infrastructure has always required. Electricity demand is rising again after twenty flat years, and it is rising in a form — always-on data centers and electrified industry — that wants firm, carbon-free, price-stable power on twenty-year terms. Nuclear is the only clean source that supplies that today at scale, on a few hundred acres, at a 91 percent capacity factor, with a fuel cost so small that gas crises pass it by. Tripling world capacity by 2050 is a construction problem, and construction problems are solved by ordering.
Best evidence: France went from almost no nuclear to roughly two-thirds of its electricity in about fifteen years, and still has one of the lowest-carbon grids in the industrialized world. The UAE built four APR-1400 units at Barakah between 2012 and 2024, now supplying about a quarter of national electricity. China connected 38 reactors in the decade to 2024 at roughly $2,000 to $3,000 per kW and around six years each. Existing reactors run at $26 to $36 per MWh, the cheapest firm low-carbon power available. The IEA reports nuclear investment above $80 billion in 2026, up about 50 percent in five years, and the World Bank ended its financing ban in June 2025.
Answer to the strongest objection — that the West cannot build on budget: We do not dispute the record; we dispute the diagnosis. The projects that blew up were first-of-a-kind units built by consortia that had not built a reactor in decades, under regulators reviewing a novel design in parallel with construction, with design changes landing after concrete was poured. Every one of those is a fixable condition and every one of them is absent by the fourth unit of a standard design, which is why the same AP1000 is built in China on schedule. The alternative is not a cheaper clean firm resource; it is gas, which is what actually gets built when a reactor is cancelled.

Camp: Run the existing fleet and stop there
Adherents: Union of Concerned Scientists — https://www.ucs.org/resources/nuclear-power-dilemma ; Natural Resources Defense Council — https://www.nrdc.org/bio/matthew-mckinzie/nrdc-analysis-nuclear-energy-and-safer-climate-future-0 ; also a range of energy economists and the state programs in Illinois, New York and New Jersey that pay existing reactors to stay open.
The case in the camp’s own voice: Closing a working reactor is almost always a mistake, and building a new one is almost always a bad investment. Those two sentences are not in tension, because they are about different things. An existing reactor is a paid-for asset producing carbon-free electricity at $26 to $36 per MWh; when one closes early, gas fills the gap, emissions rise and prices rise, which is what happened in Germany, in Japan after 2011, and at Indian Point. So we support license renewals where the safety case holds, we support payments that reflect the low-carbon value these plants provide, and we have supported specific restarts where the grid case is real. What we will not do is hand tens of billions in construction risk to ratepayers and taxpayers for new plants, because the industry has had seventy years of public support and still cannot deliver a project on budget. The right policy is not a nuclear policy at all. Price carbon, or set a low-carbon electricity standard, make the support temporary and conditional, require companies seeking it to open their books, and let the existing fleet earn what its carbon-free output is worth.
Best evidence: The cost gap between running a reactor and building one is roughly sevenfold on Lazard’s 2026 numbers. Germany’s phase-out completed in April 2023 and the shortfall was met substantially by fossil generation in the near term. The US restart projects pencil out at an illustrative $71 per MWh precisely because they reuse an existing site, license and grid connection. Against that, Vogtle cost $35 billion against a $14 billion estimate, VC Summer consumed about $9 billion and was abandoned with nothing built, and South Carolina customers are still paying for it.
Answer to the strongest objection — that this is a policy with no future, since the fleet ages out: It is a policy with a horizon, which is not the same thing. Eighty-year licenses take the current fleet most of the way to mid-century, which is the period that matters for the carbon budget, and they cost a fraction of new construction. If in that time someone demonstrates a new reactor delivered on time and on budget without a public guarantee, we will update. We are asking for evidence before capital, which is the normal order.

Camp: Phase out nuclear and build renewables instead
Adherents: Greenpeace — https://www.greenpeace.org/usa/climate/issues/nuclear/ ; Mycle Schneider and the World Nuclear Industry Status Report — https://www.worldnuclearreport.org/ ; also the governments of Austria and, until recently, Germany, the Sierra Club, Beyond Nuclear, Friends of the Earth and International Physicians for the Prevention of Nuclear War.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The climate problem is a race against a clock, and nuclear loses that race on every metric that matters. A reactor takes nearly ten years from first concrete to grid connection, and that is for the ones that finish; solar and wind arrive in one to three years, at a third of the cost per unit of energy delivered, and they got cheaper every year while nuclear got more expensive. Money spent on a reactor is money not spent on the technologies that would cut more carbon sooner, which makes nuclear not merely unhelpful but actively counterproductive. And nuclear carries costs that do not appear on a balance sheet: a class of accident that renders territory uninhabitable and displaces whole populations, a waste stream that stays dangerous for longer than any institution has ever survived, a fuel chain that concentrates strategic dependence on a handful of states, and a permanent overlap with the technology of weapons. We are not asked to accept those costs for a source that is winning. Nuclear is at 8.8 percent of world electricity and falling, and solar and wind together already generate far more. This is a declining industry asking to be rescued.
Best evidence: Nuclear’s share of world electricity has fallen from about 17.5 percent in 1996 to 8.8 percent in 2025, while global capacity has been effectively flat for a decade. Solar added a record 647 GW in 2025 and overtook wind; both are projected to overtake nuclear in output in 2026. The seven reactors that started up worldwide in 2024 averaged 9.6 years of construction. Solar investment is running at $365 billion a year against $80 billion for nuclear. Fukushima’s evacuation displaced about 160,000 people and killed more than 2,300; Chernobyl’s zone remains closed forty years on; and no country except Finland has yet opened a permanent repository for the waste.
Answer to the strongest objection — that a fully renewable grid needs firm power nuclear alone can supply: The gap that argument depends on keeps shrinking, and it is being closed by things that can be deployed in the time nuclear takes to permit. Batteries, demand flexibility, long-duration storage, geothermal and continental-scale interconnection all address the same hours, and all of them have cost curves pointing down rather than up. Meanwhile the system nuclear is supposed to firm does not exist yet either: we are being asked to commit to a fifteen-year project to solve a problem in a grid we have not built, with a technology whose price rose while every competitor’s fell.

Camp: Rebuild the industry around new reactor designs
Adherents: TerraPower — https://www.terrapower.com/natrium/ ; Ontario Power Generation — https://www.opg.com/projects-services/projects/nuclear/smr/darlington-smr/ ; also X-energy, Kairos Power, Rolls-Royce SMR, GE Vernova Hitachi, the Nuclear Innovation Alliance, and Google, Amazon and Meta as offtakers.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The problem with nuclear is not fission; it is the way we have been building it. A gigawatt-scale reactor is a bespoke megaproject assembled outdoors over a decade, which is the single worst way to build anything, and ordering more of them in fleets only spreads that failure mode across more sites. Make the unit small enough to be manufactured in a factory and shipped, and the economics change category: you get real serial learning, controlled quality, a four-year build instead of twelve, and a capital commitment a utility board can approve without betting the company. Passive safety designs remove whole classes of active systems, pumps and backup power, which removes both cost and the accident sequences that frightened people in the first place. Some of these designs run hot enough to serve industrial heat, and some can run on used fuel. The customers exist already and are signing twenty-year contracts. Ontario has a construction licence and four BWRX-300 units underway; TerraPower has an NRC construction permit for Kemmerer. This is the generation of reactors that gets built, and it is not the generation that got cancelled.
Best evidence: Ontario Power Generation obtained a construction licence for the first Darlington BWRX-300 in April 2025, began construction, and applied for an operating licence in 2026, targeting grid connection by the end of 2030 — the first SMR in a G7 country. The NRC issued a construction permit for TerraPower’s Kemmerer Unit 1 on 4 March 2026, with grid connection targeted for 2031. Meta and TerraPower agreed in January 2026 to develop up to eight 345 MW Natrium plants, up to 2.8 GW, with early work starting on two units and rights held to six more; Google, Amazon and Microsoft have each signed comparable advanced-nuclear or restart agreements. The NRC is reorganizing toward an 18-month licensing target under the 2024 ADVANCE Act and the May 2025 executive orders.
Answer to the strongest objection — that small reactors cost more per kilowatt and no serial production exists: Both are true today and neither is a verdict. Per-kilowatt capital cost is the wrong metric for a product whose thesis is learning rate rather than unit scale; the comparison that matters is the twentieth unit, and there has never been a twentieth unit because there has never been an order book. That is what the current offtake agreements are for. We accept that NuScale’s Carbon Free Power Project failed in 2023 when its target price rose past what its subscribers would pay, and we accept that the first units will be expensive. The test is whether the second and third come in lower, and that test runs from about 2030.

Camp: Set clean firm targets and let technologies compete
Adherents: Princeton ZERO Lab and Jesse Jenkins — https://zero.lab.princeton.edu/ ; Clean Air Task Force — https://www.catf.us/resource/clean-firm-electricity-technologies-why-what-how/ ; also a substantial share of the academic energy-modeling community and system operators procuring by attribute rather than by resource type.
The case in the camp’s own voice: The question “how much nuclear should we build” is the wrong question, and asking it is why this argument has gone in circles for twenty years. What a deeply decarbonized grid actually needs is a quantity of generation that is clean, dispatchable and not dependent on the weather — clean firm capacity — because the last 10 to 20 percent of emissions is far more expensive to remove without it. That requirement is technology-agnostic. Nuclear can fill it. So can enhanced geothermal, gas with carbon capture, hydrogen or ammonia combustion, and long-duration storage, and which of them wins should be settled by procurement and cost, not by ideology inherited from the 1970s. The right policy is to write the requirement as an attribute, set the standard, and let the technologies bid. Doing it this way has a second advantage: it stops the debate from being a referendum on nuclear’s symbolism and turns it into an auction with a number at the end. If nuclear hits its cost targets it will win a large share. If it does not, something else will, and we will not have spent a decade arguing about it.
Best evidence: System models across many research groups converge on the finding that adding a clean firm resource to a wind, solar and storage system lowers total system cost substantially, and that the identity of that resource matters much less than its availability. The same models show nuclear’s optimal share swinging from near zero to very large depending on its assumed capital cost — which is an argument for competitive procurement, since nobody knows that cost in advance. Corporate buyers have already begun procuring this way, signing clean firm offtake agreements across next-generation geothermal, nuclear and gas with carbon capture rather than committing to a single technology.
Answer to the strongest objection — that this is a way of avoiding the decision while the clock runs: We are not avoiding a decision; we are avoiding the wrong decision, which is picking a winner before the prices are known. A clean firm standard is a commitment, with a quantity and a date, and it forces exactly the hard tradeoffs that a debate about nuclear in the abstract lets people dodge. The objection from the nuclear side is really that nuclear needs a guaranteed order book to get cheap, which may be true — but then the argument is about industrial policy for one technology, and it should be made in those terms and defended on its own, not smuggled in as a claim about physics.

## Crux

The disagreement under the disagreements is whether the price of a new reactor in a democratic, market-financed country is a fact about fission or a fact about how we have chosen to build and license it. Almost every camp’s position resolves to an answer to that question. If the $175 to $255 per MWh that Lazard derives from Vogtle is roughly what the tenth unit of a standard design would also cost, then nuclear is an expensive way to buy clean electricity and the sensible policy is to run the plants that exist, retire them when they are done, and spend the money on things that got cheaper. If that price is mostly the cost of restarting a dormant industry — a first-of-a-kind design, a supply chain that had to be rebuilt, a regulator reviewing as it went, financing at rates that punish a twelve-year build — then the number is an artifact of a cold start and the policy conclusion inverts entirely: the expensive thing is stopping and starting, and the cheapest reactor is the one after the last one. Everything else in this debate, including the safety argument and the waste argument, tends to follow rather than lead. When nuclear is cheap, the risks look manageable; when it is expensive, they look decisive.

What evidence could settle: Whether serial construction of a standard design brings Western costs down is an empirical claim with a test running now. The data will come from the next cohort — the four BWRX-300 units at Darlington, whose first unit targets grid connection by the end of 2030; TerraPower’s Kemmerer Unit 1, permitted in March 2026 and targeting 2031; any second and third tranche of AP1000s ordered on existing sites; and the three US restarts at Palisades, Crane and Duane Arnold, which test how much of the cost is the site and licence rather than the machine. The specific observable is the slope: whether unit two costs materially less than unit one, whether unit four costs less again, and whether schedules converge on the six-to-seven years achieved in China and the UAE or stay near the 9.6-year global average. A second empirical thread runs alongside it: how much clean firm capacity a deeply decarbonized grid actually needs, which is a modelling question that will be answered by the grids of the 2030s as storage, geothermal and interconnection either close the gap or do not. Neither thread is settled today, and both will look different in five years.

What no data can close: How much a society should pay for firmness, and who should carry the risk of being wrong. Suppose the numbers come in ambiguous — nuclear somewhat more expensive than a renewables-plus-storage portfolio, but available regardless of weather, on a small footprint, with a fuel supply that does not move with gas markets. Whether that premium is worth paying depends on how much weight you give to security of supply against price, to the interests of a host community against those of a national grid, and to a small chance of a concentrated, irreversible harm against a certainty of diffuse ones. It also depends on a distributional judgment that is not technical at all: new nuclear will not be built without someone underwriting the construction risk, and the choice of whether that someone is taxpayers, ratepayers, a data-center offtaker or nobody is a choice about who a society thinks should bear the cost of insurance it may never need. No cost curve decides that.
