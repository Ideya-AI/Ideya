# Smartphones & Childhood — research

## Overview

This topic asks whether smartphones and social media are a major cause of the decline in adolescent mental health that began in the early 2010s in the United States and several other rich countries, and what should follow for the way childhood is organized. It covers depression, anxiety, self-harm and suicide among roughly 10- to 19-year-olds; the research linking those trends to smartphones and social media; the policy responses now spreading, from phone-free schools and minimum-age laws for social media to design rules and lawsuits against platforms; and the parallel argument that childhood has become more supervised and adult-structured, with less unsupervised play and independence, and that this shift is part of the cause or the larger part of it. It deliberately excludes screen time for children under school age, video games as a subject of their own, online child sexual exploitation except as one of the harms in the debate, content moderation and speech law in general (covered in the content moderation topic), and whether mental illness is overdiagnosed except where that bears on whether the decline in youth mental health is real (covered in the overdiagnosis topic).

Why now: on 26 August 2026 Meta agreed to pay at least $12.1 billion, rising to $17.1 billion if other major social media companies reach similar settlements, to resolve claims by a bipartisan coalition of 51 attorneys general that it designed Instagram and Facebook to be addictive for children and teens while knowing the features were harmful (New York Attorney General, https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-secures-171-billion-and-groundbreaking-reforms-meta). Connecticut’s announcement puts the guaranteed portion at $12.19 billion over ten years and lists the required changes: a combined two-hour daily limit for minors across Instagram and Facebook, no access between midnight and 6 a.m., no notifications during school hours on weekdays, age verification, and parental controls to switch off algorithmic feeds and autoplay (Connecticut Attorney General, https://portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2026-press-releases/settlement-with-meta). The settlement is subject to court approval, and neither announcement describes an admission of wrongdoing by Meta. It came five months after the first jury verdict of its kind: on 25 March 2026 a Los Angeles jury found Meta and YouTube liable for negligently designing addictive products that harmed a young woman identified as K.G.M. and awarded $3 million in compensatory and $3 million in punitive damages, with fault split 70 percent Meta and 30 percent YouTube (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K.G.M._v._Meta_et_al.). Governments moved at the same time. Australia’s ban on social media accounts for under-16s has been in force since 10 December 2025, and on 15 June 2026 the UK government announced that under-16s would be barred from platforms “like Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and X” from spring 2027, after a consultation that drew more than 116,000 responses (GOV.UK, https://www.gov.uk/government/news/social-media-to-be-banned-for-under-16s-in-landmark-government-move-to-givekids-their-childhood-back). And the underlying data turned: on 21 September 2026 the CDC reported that the share of US high school students with persistent sadness or hopelessness fell from 42 percent in 2021 to 33 percent in 2025 (CDC, https://www.cdc.gov/media/releases/2026/cdc-data-show-encouraging-progress-in-youth-health.html), echoing a July 2026 federal survey that found past-year major depression among 12- to 17-year-olds down from 20.8 percent in 2021 to 15.1 percent in 2025, while teens’ use of phones and social media stayed high.

The debate is live because the trend it set out to explain is both real and now reversing, and because nobody can run the experiment that would settle it. Jonathan Haidt’s 2024 book The Anxious Generation gave the smartphone explanation its public form and its program: no smartphones before high school, no social media before 16, phone-free schools and more independent play. Researchers such as Candice Odgers, Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski argue that the evidence shows small average associations and does not establish that phones caused the decline, and a 2023 National Academies committee concluded that the literature “did not support the conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level.” Peter Gray, a co-founder of the independence movement Haidt helped launch, published a book in September 2026 arguing that school pressure and the loss of free play, not phones, are the main cause. Around them sit parents’ groups, legislatures and attorneys general pushing age limits and design rules; child-safety charities that want platforms fixed rather than children banned; civil-liberties groups warning about age verification and the loss of online community for isolated and LGBTQ teens; and platforms contesting liability in court. The public has largely made up its mind: in a Pew survey in May 2026, 56 percent of US adults supported banning social media for under-16s and 21 percent opposed it.

Label: Share of variation in adolescent well-being associated with digital technology use
Value: 0.4
Unit: percent of variation in well-being (maximum)
Viz: bar
Data year: 2007 to 2016 survey data
Source label: Orben & Przybylski, “The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use,” Nature Human Behaviour, 2019
Source URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1
Note: Across three large datasets covering 355,358 adolescents, digital technology use was negatively associated with well-being but explained at most 0.4 percent of the variation in it.
Context: The paper ran every defensible combination of variables and controls (a “specification curve”) on three large US and UK surveys, and found an association that was consistently negative but small, of a size the authors compared with the association between well-being and eating potatoes. It became the reference point for researchers who argue the smartphone thesis is overstated. Haidt, Jean Twenge and Zach Rausch reply that the analysis averages across all kinds of screen use, both sexes and many outcomes, and that when the question is narrowed to social media, girls and depression, correlations rise to a range they compare with other accepted public-health risks. They also argue that a small average can conceal large harm to a subset of heavy users, and that population-level effects can be larger than individual correlations suggest when a technology changes a whole peer group at once. Both readings are defended in the peer-reviewed literature.

Data year: studies published through 2023
Source label: Thrul et al., “Social Media Reduction or Abstinence Interventions Are Providing Mental Health Benefits — Reanalysis of a Published Meta-Analysis,” Psychology of Popular Media, 2025
Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12125955/
Note: Pooled across 27 experiments, reducing social media use improved mental health by a statistically insignificant 0.088 standard deviations; a reanalysis found a significant improvement of 0.156 when reductions lasted a week or more.
Context: Randomized experiments are the strongest test of cause and effect, and they are the most contested evidence in the debate. Christopher Ferguson’s 2024 meta-analysis (https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000541) pooled 27 studies that manipulated social media use and found an average effect whose confidence interval included zero; he concluded the experiments do not show that social media harms mental health. Johannes Thrul and colleagues reanalyzed the same data and found that very short reductions made people feel slightly worse, consistent with withdrawal, while reductions of a week or more produced a small, significant improvement. Haidt and Rausch, in the 2026 World Happiness Report, put the improvement in well-designed reduction experiments at 0.19 to 0.28 standard deviations. Most of these experiments were run on college students and adults for days or weeks, not on children over years, so all sides agree they cannot directly answer what a childhood with or without social media does. The pooled value is as described by the meta-analysis’s critics at After Babel (https://www.afterbabel.com/p/the-case-for-causality-part-1).

The study measured learning and behavior, not mental health. The main study of mental health, the SMART Schools study of 1,227 students aged 12 to 15 in 30 English secondary schools (Goodyear et al., Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40213498/), found that students in schools restricting recreational phone use spent less time on phones during the school day but no less overall, and found no difference in mental wellbeing (adjusted mean difference −0.48 points on a scale with a mean of 47, confidence interval −2.05 to 1.06). It did find that more phone and social media time overall was associated with worse wellbeing. Supporters of bans criticize the study as cross-sectional and its restrictive schools as not strict enough.

A settlement is not a finding of fact about causation, and Meta has said it disagrees with the verdict and will appeal; but the product changes it agreed to are the design remedies one camp in this debate has sought.

## Questions

Question: Did smartphones and social media cause a large share of the rise in adolescent depression and anxiety after 2012?
Type: empirical
Poles: Major cause / Minor factor
Why contested: The rise and the spread of smartphones happened at the same time, and no one can randomly assign a generation to grow up without them, so each side reads the same imperfect studies differently. Individual-level correlations are small on average, experiments are short and mostly on adults, and natural experiments are few.
Case for “Major cause”: The timing is sharp and shared: depression, self-harm and suicide among young adolescents, especially girls, turned upward around 2012 in the US, Canada, the UK and parts of northern Europe, just as front-facing cameras, Instagram and always-on smartphones became normal in early adolescence. Heavy users, roughly those on social media three or more hours a day, have about double the risk of depression and anxiety symptoms, and half of US teens are heavy users. Natural experiments point the same way: when Facebook arrived at US colleges one campus at a time, students’ mental health worsened, and the arrival of high-speed internet in regions of several countries has been followed by worse mental health among girls. Experiments that cut social media use for a week or more produce small improvements. Company documents disclosed in litigation show platforms knew their products harmed some teens. Taken together, Jonathan Haidt and Zach Rausch argue in the 2026 World Happiness Report, seven independent lines of evidence converge, and a technology that changes a whole peer group’s social life at once can do population-level harm that individual correlations understate.
Case for “Minor factor”: The best large analyses find the average association between screen or social media use and well-being is negative but very small, explaining well under one percent of the variation. Longitudinal studies find that distress predicts later use about as well as use predicts later distress. Pooled experiments show no significant average effect, and the ones that do show effects are brief studies of college students and adults. The US National Academies reviewed the literature in 2023 and concluded it did not show that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level. Other explanations fit the timing too: academic pressure, the aftermath of the 2008 recession, the opioid epidemic, rising firearm access in youth suicide, changes in how distress is screened and labeled, and a long decline in children’s independence. And the trend does not track smartphones everywhere: countries with similar phone adoption show different mental-health paths, and since 2021 US teen depression has fallen while use stayed high.

Question: How much certainty should be required before restricting children’s access to a technology?
Type: value
Poles: Act on precaution / Wait for proof
Why contested: Both sides accept that the evidence is incomplete; they disagree about which error is worse, restricting children needlessly or failing to protect them while studies accumulate.
Case for “Act on precaution”: Children are not able to consent to the risks of products designed to capture their attention, and societies routinely set age limits for alcohol, tobacco, driving and gambling without randomized trials proving harm at the population level. The costs of waiting are borne by a generation passing through a sensitive developmental window, while the costs of delay (a few years without accounts or a smartphone) are modest and reversible. Parents cannot act alone, because a child without a phone is excluded from the group chat; a shared rule solves a collective-action problem. Precaution is what the public, most parents and many teens themselves now favor.
Case for “Wait for proof”: Policy built on a contested causal story can do harm of its own: it spends political attention and money that could go to mental-health care, pushes teens to less regulated corners of the internet, cuts off isolated young people from support, and normalizes age verification for everyone. Past panics over comics, television, video games and rock music produced restrictions that later looked misguided. If phones are not the main cause, restrictions will not bring the rates down and the real causes will go unaddressed. Measures should be proportionate to what is known, targeted at specific demonstrated harms, and evaluated as they roll out.

Question: Should the law set a minimum age for social media, or should parents decide?
Type: value
Poles: Legal age limit / Parents decide
Why contested: The question is about who holds authority over a child’s social life, and it cuts across party lines; courts in the US and France have also questioned whether a blanket age ban respects young people’s own rights to expression.
Case for “Legal age limit”: Parents already face a collective-action trap: each family that holds out makes its child the odd one out, so individual choice drifts toward the earliest age any family allows. A legal minimum age moves the burden to the companies, which have the data to enforce it, and gives every parent the same backing. Existing US rules set the de facto minimum at 13 only because of a 1998 children’s privacy law that was never meant as a judgment about development. Australia, Brazil, Indonesia and Malaysia now enforce a minimum of 16 in some form, and the UK plans to follow.
Case for “Parents decide”: Children differ, families differ, and parents are better placed than legislators to judge when a particular child is ready. A ban overrides parents who want their teenager on a platform for family contact, creative work or community, and it forces age checks on every user, adults included. The better role for law is to give parents working tools, such as consent requirements and controls that platforms must provide, and leave the decision with the family. In the US, the Constitution protects minors’ access to speech, and courts have blocked several state laws on those grounds; France’s Constitutional Council struck down that country’s under-15 ban in August 2026.

Question: Should the remedy target children’s access or the platforms’ design?
Type: value
Poles: Restrict access / Fix design
Why contested: Both camps agree platforms can harm children; they disagree about whether keeping children off is realistic and sufficient, or whether it lets companies off the hook for products that also harm older teens and adults.
Case for “Restrict access”: Design rules are slow, complex and easy to evade, and companies have strong incentives to maximize engagement. A clear age line is simple to state and to enforce, and it protects the most vulnerable years of early puberty whatever the platforms do. Age limits and design rules are not exclusive; delay buys time while better design is worked out.
Case for “Fix design”: Features such as infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks, engagement-driven recommendations, beauty filters and late-night notifications are what harm young users, and they harm 16- and 17-year-olds and adults as well. A ban removes the pressure on companies to change, pushes children to platforms outside the rules, and makes the day a teen turns 16 a cliff edge. Design duties such as the UK’s Age Appropriate Design Code, and the product changes Meta accepted in its 2026 settlement with state attorneys general, target the mechanism itself.

Question: Is the loss of unsupervised play and independence a larger cause of youth distress than screens?
Type: empirical
Poles: Lost independence / Screens first
Why contested: Children’s independence declined for decades before smartphones, and both trends point toward less face-to-face, self-directed time with peers, so they are hard to separate; the leading voices on each side founded an organization together.
Case for “Lost independence”: Peter Gray argues that the long decline of free play and independent activity, together with rising academic pressure, explains youth anxiety and depression better than phones do. Children who organize their own play learn to solve problems, manage fear and feel competent, and anxiety rises when those experiences are removed. Youth suicide and depression also rose in earlier decades before the internet, as children’s freedom shrank, and Gray points to psychiatric emergencies that fall during summer vacations as evidence that school pressure is a direct driver. On this view, online spaces became one of the last places teens could be unsupervised, and taking them away without restoring freedom offline makes things worse.
Case for “Screens first”: The decline in independence is real but gradual, running from the 1970s onward, while the mental-health break is sudden and concentrated after 2012, which fits a fast technological change better. Haidt calls it two mistakes, overprotection in the real world and underprotection online, and argues that the phone-based childhood accelerated and deepened the loss of play by replacing unstructured time with screen time. Restoring independence matters, but it will not work while children have a more attractive option in their pockets.

Question: Is the rise in reported distress mainly a real increase in illness, or partly a change in how young people describe and report distress?
Type: empirical
Poles: Real rise / Partly relabeling
Why contested: Most trend data come from self-reports and diagnoses, which shift with awareness, screening and language; the harder measures, such as suicide and hospital visits, rose too but are also affected by other factors.
Case for “Real rise”: Behavioral measures that cannot be explained by a new vocabulary also rose, including emergency visits for self-harm among young adolescent girls and suicide among 10- to 14-year-olds, which tripled between 2007 and 2018 in the US. Survey questions such as the Youth Risk Behavior Survey’s item on persistent sadness have been asked in the same way for decades, and the rise appears across countries with different awareness campaigns.
Case for “Partly relabeling”: Lucy Foulkes and Jack Andrews’s “prevalence inflation” hypothesis holds that mental-health awareness efforts help some young people recognize real problems but lead others to interpret ordinary distress as disorder, which can raise reported rates and, through expectation and avoidance, make some symptoms worse. Diagnoses and screening expanded over the same years. Self-harm figures are influenced by changes in hospital coding and help-seeking. None of this denies suffering, but it means the size of the rise may be overstated and its causes include how the culture talks about mental health.

Question: Does the improvement since 2021 show that restrictions are working, or that phones were never the main driver?
Type: empirical
Poles: Early restrictions working / Pandemic recovery
Why contested: US measures of teen depression and hopelessness fell sharply between 2021 and 2025 while teens’ use of phones and social media stayed high; each side has a ready explanation and none has been tested.
Case for “Early restrictions working”: The fall coincides with a wave of phone-free school laws, growing parental caution, platform changes such as teen accounts with default limits, and a cultural shift in which teens themselves say social media harms people their age. Girls, the group most exposed to social comparison online, improved most. On this view the improvement is a sign that changing how children use technology helps, and the rates remain well above where they were before 2012.
Case for “Pandemic recovery”: The sharpest movements, the peak in 2021 and the fall after, line up with school closures and reopening, not with any change in phone ownership. Almost constant internet use among teens is higher now than a decade ago, yet depression is falling, which is hard to square with phones as the primary cause. Phone-free school laws mostly took effect in 2024 and 2025, too late and too varied to explain a trend that began earlier.

Question: Should age limits be enforced if they cut isolated and minority teens off from the communities they find online?
Type: value
Poles: Protect the many / Preserve lifelines
Why contested: The same platforms that expose some teens to harm give others, including LGBTQ youth, rural teens and those with rare conditions, their main source of peers and support; a rule for everyone trades one group’s safety against another’s.
Case for “Protect the many”: Policy should be judged by its effect on the whole population of children, and the harms of compulsive use, sleep loss, harassment, sextortion and exposure to self-harm content reach far more young people than the benefits of specific online communities. Vulnerable teens are also among those most exposed to predators and harmful content. Messaging, dedicated support services and moderated communities can remain available without open social media accounts; the UK plan exempts messaging apps.
Case for “Preserve lifelines”: For many teens who are isolated at home or at school, online spaces are where they first find people like themselves and information about their health. The Trevor Project and other LGBTQ organizations report that a large share of LGBTQ youth rely on social media for peer support. Cutting them off in the name of the majority removes a demonstrated benefit to answer a contested harm, and the young people who most need protection are the ones who most need access.

Question: Is mandatory age verification worth its cost to privacy and anonymous speech?
Type: value
Poles: Verify ages / Protect anonymity
Why contested: Any enforceable minimum age requires checking ages, and every method, from ID uploads to facial age estimation, touches every user, not only children.
Case for “Verify ages”: Age checks are routine offline for alcohol, cinema and gambling, and privacy-preserving methods, such as estimation that discards the image or checks done once on the device or in the app store, can confirm an age range without revealing identity. Without verification, age limits are empty, as the long-ignored minimum of 13 shows.
Case for “Protect anonymity”: Requiring age checks creates databases of IDs or biometric data that can leak and be abused, chills anonymous speech for adults and adolescents alike, and falls hardest on people without documents. Estimation technologies are least accurate for the very ages the laws target. The Electronic Frontier Foundation argues that courts have repeatedly found such requirements incompatible with free-speech rights, and France’s Constitutional Council struck down that country’s ban partly because it lacked privacy safeguards for the age checks it required.

## Positions

Camp: Delay smartphones and social media until mid-adolescence
Adherents: Jonathan Haidt, social psychologist at New York University and author of The Anxious Generation, whose four “new norms” are no smartphones before high school, no social media before 16, phone-free schools and more independence, free play and responsibility in the real world (https://www.anxiousgeneration.com/pdfs/four-norms.pdf; with Zach Rausch, World Happiness Report 2026, chapter concluding that “social media platforms are not safe for children and adolescents,” https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2026/social-media-is-harming-adolescents-at-a-scale-large-enough-to-cause-changes-at-the-population-level/). The UK government, which announced on 15 June 2026 that under-16s would be barred from Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and X from spring 2027, with Technology Secretary Liz Kendall describing it as “taking power away from the tech giants and putting it back in parents’ hands” (https://www.gov.uk/government/news/social-media-to-be-banned-for-under-16s-in-landmark-government-move-to-givekids-their-childhood-back). Also: the Australian government, whose under-16 ban took effect on 10 December 2025; parent movements such as Smartphone Free Childhood in the UK and Wait Until 8th in the US; and a majority of the US public (56 percent support in Pew’s 2026 survey).
Case: Around 2012 childhood moved onto the phone, and within a few years depression, anxiety, self-harm and suicide among young adolescents rose steeply across much of the English-speaking world and northern Europe, most of all among girls. The products that moved in were designed to maximize engagement, not to support development, and they arrived in early puberty, the most sensitive period for social comparison, sleep and identity. The harm is not only to heavy users: when a whole peer group moves online, even children who hold out lose the in-person social life that used to be the default. No single family can fix this, because a child without a phone is left out; shared norms and a legal floor solve the collective-action problem. The ask is modest and reversible: basic phones before about 14, social media accounts from 16, phones locked away during the school day, and more real-world freedom to replace what the phone displaced.
Evidence: The timing of the rise across several countries; the roughly doubled risk of depression symptoms among heavy users; longitudinal studies in which social media use predicts later depression; natural experiments such as the staggered arrival of Facebook at US colleges, which worsened students’ mental health (Braghieri, Levy and Makarin, American Economic Review, 2022, summary at https://www.aeaweb.org/research/charts/social-media-mental-health-college); reduction experiments lasting a week or more, which Haidt and Rausch put at effect sizes of g = 0.19 for depression and 0.28 for anxiety; internal company documents disclosed in litigation; and the 2025 JAMA finding that rising addictive use of social media in early adolescence was linked to about twice the risk of suicidal behavior.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that the evidence is only correlational and small, the camp answers that no product is held to the standard of proving population-level harm by randomized trial before a child safety rule is set, that seven independent lines of evidence point the same way, and that the small average correlations in studies like Orben and Przybylski’s mix together all screens, both sexes and all outcomes, masking the larger association between social media and depression among girls. To the point that teen depression has fallen since 2021 while use stayed high, it answers that phone-free schools, parental caution and platform changes spread in those years and that rates remain far above their pre-2012 level.
Variation: Adherents differ on instrument. Haidt emphasizes coordinated parental norms and school policy as much as law; Australia and the UK use national bans enforced against platforms; many US states act only through school phone laws and parental-consent rules. Many in this camp, including the UK government and Australia’s eSafety Commissioner, pair age limits with design duties on platforms, which is the hybrid position in this debate; it differs from the next camp in treating an age floor as necessary rather than as a risk.

Camp: Hold platforms to safe design instead of banning young users
Adherents: The Molly Rose Foundation, set up after the suicide of 14-year-old Molly Russell, which in a joint statement with the NSPCC, 5Rights Foundation and 42 child-protection and online safety organizations opposed a blanket under-16 ban and called instead for a stronger Online Safety Act holding companies accountable for harmful design and algorithms; its chair Ian Russell said “Bans are the wrong answer to a vital question. They risk unintended consequences that could leave children at greater risk” (https://mollyrosefoundation.org/childrens-and-online-safety-campaigners-issue-joint-statement-on-social-media-ban-for-under-16s/). UNICEF, which warned in December 2025 that “social media bans come with their own risks, and they may even backfire,” and that age restrictions “must be part of a broader approach” with platforms investing in safety, design and moderation (https://news.un.org/feed/view/en/story/2025/12/1166557). Also: Fairplay, which campaigns for the US Kids Online Safety Act with a “duty of care” on platform design (https://fairplayforkids.org/fairplay-statement-on-the-kids-act-passing/); the bipartisan attorneys general whose 2026 settlement with Meta imposed design changes such as time limits, overnight blocks and non-algorithmic feeds.
Case: The problem is not that teenagers are online; it is that the products they use are engineered for engagement, with infinite feeds, autoplay, streaks, notifications, beauty filters and recommendation systems that push distressing and self-harm content to vulnerable users. Those features harm 16- and 17-year-olds and adults too, so a ban at 16 leaves the machinery untouched and simply moves the cliff edge. Bans are easy to evade, push children toward less regulated apps and gaming chats, cut off the vulnerable teens who find support online, and let companies claim the problem is solved once children are formally excluded. The durable fix is a legal duty on companies to design safely for the young people who will use their products regardless: age-appropriate defaults, algorithmic feeds off by default, no nighttime notifications, strong enforcement and real penalties.
Evidence: Coroner’s findings in the Molly Russell case in 2022 that online content contributed to her death; company documents disclosed in US litigation showing awareness of harms from specific features; the 2025 JAMA finding that addictive patterns of use, not total screen time, were linked to suicidal behavior; the K.G.M. verdict of March 2026, in which a jury found Meta and YouTube negligent in product design; and the product changes Meta accepted in its August 2026 settlement with 51 attorneys general.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that design rules are slow and companies will evade them, the camp answers that bans also depend on company enforcement and age checks that teens get around, and that litigation and regulators have now shown that design duties can be imposed and enforced, as the Meta settlement shows. To the charge that it is soft on platforms, it answers that it asks more of them than a ban does: a ban lets them keep harmful designs for everyone over 16.
Variation: UNICEF and some members accept age limits as one element of a broader approach; the Molly Rose Foundation coalition opposes blanket bans outright. In the US, adherents concentrate on KOSA’s duty of care and state age-appropriate design codes.

Camp: Look past phones to the wider causes of youth distress
Adherents: Candice Odgers, psychologist at the University of California, Irvine, who wrote in her Nature review of The Anxious Generation that its claim that digital technology is causing an epidemic of mental illness “is not supported by science” and that the focus on social media “might distract us from effectively responding to the real causes of the current mental-health crisis in young people” (https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00902-2; open copy https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-11/Odgers_Nature_2024.pdf). The US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, whose 2023 committee found that its “review of published literature did not support the conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level,” while recommending platform standards, research access and media literacy (https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/27396/Highlights_for_Social_Media_and_Adolescent_Health.pdf). Also: Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski, whose 2019 analysis found digital technology use explained at most 0.4 percent of the variation in adolescent well-being (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1); Christopher Ferguson, whose 2024 meta-analysis found no significant pooled effect of reduction experiments.
Case: Youth distress is real and deserves a serious response, which is exactly why the explanation matters. The research linking social media to mental health finds small average associations, runs in both directions, and does not show that phones caused a population-wide decline. A single, vivid culprit is appealing, but it risks spending public attention on restricting teenagers’ devices while leaving the known drivers untouched: poverty and economic insecurity, academic pressure, family stress, discrimination, adverse childhood experiences, firearm access in youth suicide, and a mental-health system that cannot meet demand. Some harms online are real and should be regulated, but the priority is better data, including researcher access to platform data, and investment in care.
Evidence: The Orben and Przybylski specification-curve analysis; the National Academies’ 2023 review; Ferguson’s 2024 meta-analysis of 27 reduction experiments, with a pooled effect of 0.088 standard deviations whose confidence interval included zero; the 2025 SMART Schools study, which found no difference in wellbeing between English schools with and without restrictive phone policies; the mismatch between phone adoption and mental-health trends across countries; and the fall in US teen depression since 2021 while phone and social media use stayed high.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that waiting for proof leaves children exposed, the camp answers that it does not oppose protecting children from specific harms, only building national policy on a causal claim the evidence has not established; a policy aimed at the wrong cause does not protect anyone and uses up the political will needed for measures that would help. To the natural experiments and longitudinal studies the other side cites, it answers that their effects are small, their samples are mostly college students or adults, and they do not account for the size or the timing of the rise among children.
Variation: A related line of argument, the “prevalence inflation” hypothesis of Lucy Foulkes and Jack Andrews (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0732118X2300003X), partly rejects the question as posed: it holds that some of the measured rise reflects mental-health awareness efforts leading young people to interpret ordinary distress as disorder, so the decline itself may be smaller than the surveys suggest. Researchers in this camp differ on whether any restriction is warranted now; most support school phone rules for attention and learning while doubting they will move mental-health rates.

Camp: Give children back independence and free play
Adherents: Peter Gray, research professor of psychology at Boston College and co-founder of Let Grow, whose book Restoring Childhood (September 2026) argues that school pressure and the loss of free play, not phones, drive teen distress: “I have looked broadly and deeply into the voluminous research on [technology and teen well-being] and have found no convincing evidence that social media or other uses of digital technology are responsible for much if any of the steep rise in teen suffering” (review by Elizabeth Nolan Brown, Reason, 15 September 2026, https://reason.com/2026/09/15/restoring-childhood-says-its-not-smartphones-making-teens-miserable-its-school/; Gray’s own announcement, https://petergray.substack.com/p/116-my-new-book-restoring-childhood). Let Grow, the nonprofit led by Lenore Skenazy that campaigns for childhood independence and state “Reasonable Childhood Independence” laws, which argues that children’s screen use is their “DEFAULT not their DESIRE” and that “if parents want their kids to put down their phones, they need to start opening the front door” (https://letgrow.org/let-grow-in-the-atlantic-the-surprising-reason-kids-are-glued-to-their-phones/). Also: Elizabeth Nolan Brown, “What If Tech Isn’t Destroying Childhood, We Are?” (https://enb.substack.com/p/what-if-tech-isnt-destroying-childhood); Rep. Jennifer McClellan and Reps. Blake Moore and Virginia Foxx, sponsors of the federal Promoting Childhood Independence and Resilience Act (https://mcclellan.house.gov/2026/05/12/mcclellan-introduces-bill-to-promote-childhood-independence/).
Case: Children have been losing their freedom for fifty years. They no longer walk to school, roam the neighborhood or play without adults; their days are filled with school, homework, tests and adult-run activities, and parents can be investigated for letting them out alone. Children who never solve their own problems, take small risks or manage their own conflicts do not develop the sense of control that protects against anxiety and depression. Phones are the symptom, not the cause: the online world became the one place teenagers could be with friends without adults watching. Taking phones away without giving freedom back removes the last unsupervised space and adds one more restriction to an already overcontrolled childhood. The remedy is to let children play, roam and take responsibility, to ease academic pressure, and to change the laws and norms that punish parents for allowing it.
Evidence: Long declines in children’s independent mobility, such as the fall from 48 to 13 percent of US children in kindergarten to grade 8 walking or biking to school between 1969 and 2009 and from 86 to 25 percent of English primary children allowed home from school alone between 1971 and 2010; Gray, Lancy and Bjorklund’s 2023 review in the Journal of Pediatrics linking the decline of independent activity to declining child mental health (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36841510/); the rise in youth suicide and depression in earlier decades as childhood freedom shrank, and their improvement in the 1990s as the internet spread; seasonal patterns in which youth psychiatric emergencies fall during summer vacation; and a 2025 Harris Poll for Let Grow in which children aged 8 to 12 said they preferred unstructured in-person play to online time.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the objection that the loss of independence was gradual while the mental-health break after 2012 was sudden, the camp answers that academic pressure intensified sharply around the same time, with the spread of high-stakes testing and new national standards in the US, and that the “sudden break” partly reflects a return to pre-1990s levels after an unusual improvement. To the charge that it ignores real online harms, it answers that specific harms can be addressed without treating the whole technology as the cause, and that freedom offline is what makes children less dependent on screens.
Variation: Let Grow itself spans two views: Gray rejects phones as a major cause, while co-founder Jonathan Haidt treats lost independence and the phone-based childhood as two halves of one problem and supports both restrictions and freedom. Adherents who share Gray’s view of causes differ on whether phone limits do any good; most support letting families decide.

Camp: Keep the internet open to young people without age checks
Adherents: The Electronic Frontier Foundation, which argues that “mandatory age verification measures are not the right way to protect young people” (Paige Collings, 5 June 2026, https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/06/internet-age-gates-are-growing-global-threat) and that the UK’s under-16 ban “will cause more harm than it prevents” because “there remains no reliable, privacy-preserving method of verifying the age of every internet user” (Collings and Jillian C. York, 19 June 2026, https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/06/uks-new-under-16-social-media-ban-will-cause-more-harm-it-prevents). The American Civil Liberties Union, whose senior policy counsel Jenna Leventoff said on 29 June 2026 that “censorship and invasive age verification measures will not keep children safe” (https://www.aclu.org/press-releases/aclu-urges-congress-to-vote-against-online-censorship-proposed-in-the-kids-act). Also: The Trevor Project, the LGBTQ youth suicide-prevention organization, which told Rhode Island legislators on 7 April 2026 that a bill barring under-18s from social media accounts “will prevent LGBTQ+ young people from accessing life-saving online support systems,” while agreeing that social media “has the potential to cause harm” (https://www.rilegislature.gov/Special/comdoc/House%20Innovation%20Internet%20and%20technology%202026/04-08-2026--H7953--Gabby_Doyle(The%20Trevor%20Project)--IT.pdf), and which has joined LGBT Tech, NBJC and the Center for Democracy and Technology in a joint letter opposing age-minimum bans (https://cdt.org/insights/cdt-signs-onto-joint-letter-led-by-lgbttech-nbjc-and-the-trevor-project-to-oppose-age-minimum-social-media-bans/); NetChoice, the tech-industry trade group, which opposes bans and age verification laws and argues that “parents and guardians are best positioned to address” children’s needs (https://netchoice.org/protecting-respecting-our-youth/).
Case: Young people have rights to speak, learn and find community, and for many of them, especially LGBTQ teens, teens in unsupportive homes, rural teens and those with disabilities, online spaces are where they find information and people like themselves. An age ban cuts them off from all of it on the strength of a contested causal claim. Enforcing it requires checking the age of everyone, which means collecting IDs or face scans from adults and children alike, creating databases that can leak and ending anonymous speech online. Bans are also ineffective, since determined teens get around them and move to less safe corners of the internet. The better answers are comprehensive privacy law that stops platforms from harvesting young users’ data, education in digital literacy, tools that let parents choose, and enforcement against actual abusers.
Evidence: US court rulings that have blocked state age-verification and parental-consent laws on First Amendment grounds; the French Constitutional Council’s decision of August 2026 striking down France’s under-15 ban as a disproportionate infringement of freedom of expression, lacking safeguards for the age checks it required (reported by France 24, https://www.france24.com/en/france/20260814-france-s-constitutional-authority-strikes-down-social-media-ban-for-under-15s); the Trevor Project’s report that LGBTQ young people with access to affirming online spaces have significantly lower odds of attempting suicide; documented inaccuracy of facial age estimation around the ages the laws target; and early reports of Australian teens circumventing the ban.
Answer to the strongest objection: To the charge that it puts abstract rights ahead of children’s safety, the camp answers that the rights at stake belong to children too, that safety measures that expose everyone’s identity create new dangers, and that it supports targeted action against specific harms, such as strong privacy law and prosecution of predators. To the collective-action argument for bans, it answers that parents can coordinate without the state requiring the whole internet to check IDs.
Variation: Civil-liberties groups oppose age verification on principle; NetChoice, whose members include major platforms, opposes bans and state laws but stresses parental tools. Meta itself takes a different line: it supports federal legislation requiring app stores to verify age and obtain parents’ approval before under-16s download apps, which puts the age check at the device rather than the platform (The Hill, https://thehill.com/policy/technology/4311311-meta-urges-lawmakers-to-require-parental-approval-for-app-store-downloads-by-teens/).

## Crux

Crux: Does heavy smartphone and social media use in early adolescence cause a meaningful share of the rise in youth depression, anxiety and self-harm, and, given that the answer will stay uncertain for years, how much uncertainty should society accept before changing how children use these products?

Evidence could settle: Whether the link is causal and how large it is. The deciding evidence would be long-running trials or natural experiments on children rather than adults, in which some groups start social media or smartphones later than others for reasons unrelated to their mental health, and whose outcomes are then followed for years; independent researcher access to platform data on what young users actually see and do; and careful evaluation of the policies now in force, such as Australia’s under-16 ban, the UK’s coming ban and state school phone laws, compared against places without them. The same evidence would show whether particular design features, such as recommendation feeds or nighttime notifications, carry most of the harm, and whether the post-2021 improvement follows the policies or the end of the pandemic. It would also help to settle how much of the measured rise reflects changes in how distress is reported. Every camp in this debate says it would update on this evidence.

Value choice: Who carries the burden of proof and whose losses count most while the evidence is incomplete. One side weighs most heavily the risk of harm to a generation passing through a sensitive period and the difficulty any one family has in holding out alone, and so accepts restrictions on a probable rather than proven cause. The other weighs most heavily the costs of acting on a wrong diagnosis: lost access to information and community for teens who depend on it, age checks and data collection imposed on everyone, attention diverted from other causes, and the state taking over decisions that belonged to families and to young people themselves. How to trade the safety of the many against the freedom and lifelines of some, and whether a child’s social life should be governed by parents, platforms or the law, are choices no dataset can make.
