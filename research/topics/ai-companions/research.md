# AI Companions — research

Slug: ai-companions
Scope: Emotional relationships with AI chatbots: companionship, dependence, and rules for minors.
Researched: 2026-11-23

## Overview

An AI companion is a chatbot that people talk to as a friend, confidant, partner or character rather than as a tool for tasks. Some products are built for it, such as Character.AI, Replika, Nomi, Talkie and hundreds of smaller apps, many of them romantic or sexual; others are general assistants such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta AI or Grok that many people use the same way. This topic covers the relationships people form with these systems: whether they ease loneliness or deepen it, whether design that keeps users talking turns into emotional dependence or manipulation, how chatbots handle users in crisis, who is liable when a user is harmed, and what rules should govern access by children and teenagers, from disclosure and crisis protocols to age verification and outright bans. It deliberately leaves aside whether an AI could be conscious or deserve moral consideration (the AI consciousness topic), the wider decline of community and friendship (the loneliness topic), and social media’s effects on young people (the social media and youth mental health topic), touching each only where it bears on companion chatbots. Clinical therapy apps are included only where they are used, or marketed, as companions.

The why-now event: on 12 October 2026 Character Technologies, the company behind the role-play and companion platform Character.AI, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Delaware, citing the cost of developing the platform, potential litigation liabilities and regulatory engagement, with Disney as the opening bidder at US$20 million for a license to some of its intellectual property (CNBC, 13 October 2026, https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/13/character-ai-chapter-11-disney-bid.html; claims agent docket, case 26-11603, https://cases.stretto.com/chartech/). The sale hearing is set for 3 and 4 December 2026. In January 2026 the company and Google had agreed to a mediated settlement in principle of family lawsuits in Florida, Colorado, New York and Texas over teen suicides and harms, including the suit by the mother of 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III, who died in February 2024 (AFP via Dawn, 9 January 2026, https://www.dawn.com/news/1965918), and in November 2025 it had ended open-ended chat for users under 18 (TechCrunch, 29 October 2025, https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/29/character-ai-is-killing-the-chatbot-experience-for-minors/). Kentucky’s attorney general sued it in January 2026 (CNBC). On 15 October 2026 forty state attorneys general asked the bankruptcy judge to make any buyer protect young users and their chat histories; Reuters reported that the platform’s active users spend an average of two hours a day talking to its chatbots (Reuters via Insurance Journal, https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/10/15/889061.htm). Two weeks after the filing, on 28 October 2026, New York’s governor Kathy Hochul signed S9051B, which from 1 January 2027 bars chatbot operators from offering minors “unsafe AI companion features,” among them a bot presenting itself as human, claiming a personal relationship, flattering the user, making emotional appeals or encouraging secrecy, and requires age assurance (Governor of New York, 28 October 2026, https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-nation-leading-legislation-protect-kids-ai-chatbots-and-ensure-baby; Holland & Knight, November 2026, https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/new-york-enacts-ai-chatbot-safeguards-for-minors). In Washington, the GUARD Act, which would ban AI companions for anyone under 18, cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee unanimously on 30 April 2026 and awaits a floor vote (Senator Hawley, https://www.hawley.senate.gov/senator-hawleys-guard-act-to-protect-kids-from-ai-chatbots-passes-committee-unanimously/).

The debate is live because use has grown faster than evidence about its effects. In 2025, 72 percent of US teens said they had used an AI companion (Common Sense Media), and in 2026 a median of a third of 15- to 22-year-olds across 16 countries said they had used AI as a companion or friend (Gallup). Short experiments find that talking to a companion eases loneliness about as much as talking to a person; longer studies and surveys find that the heaviest users are lonelier and more dependent, without settling which causes which. Alongside lawsuits blaming chatbots for suicides and breakdowns, including about a dozen against OpenAI coordinated in a California court since February 2026 (National Law Review, 20 May 2026), at least a dozen US states have passed companion chatbot laws, China has barred virtual intimate relationships for minors from July 2026, Australia has required age checks from March 2026, and the European Parliament has called for a minimum age of 16. The argument runs between child-safety groups and legislators who want minors kept off companion apps; state lawmakers, AI companies and technology policy groups who want safety rules without bans; critics of human-like design who think the problem is AI that poses as a person, for anyone; civil-liberties groups that see age checks and speech rules as the larger danger; and a large population of users, many of them lonely, isolated or simply curious, who say they find real value in the conversations.

## Basics

### 1. US teens who have used an AI companion
- Label: US teens who have used AI companions
- Value: 72
- Unit: percent of US teens aged 13 to 17
- Comparison rows (same survey, same base, all teens): ever used — 72 percent; regular users, at least a few times a month — 52 percent; daily users — 13 percent; never used — 28 percent
- Data year: survey fielded 30 April to 14 May 2025; published 16 July 2025
- Source label: Common Sense Media, Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs: How and Why Teens Use AI Companions (survey by NORC at the University of Chicago, 1,060 teens)
- Source URL: https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/talk-trust-and-trade-offs_2025_web.pdf
- Note: Seventy-two percent of US teens said they had used an AI companion at least once, and 52 percent used one at least a few times a month.
- Context: Respondents were shown a definition of AI companions as “digital friends or characters you can text or talk with whenever you want,” naming Character.AI and Replika and including ChatGPT or Claude when used as a companion, but excluding homework helpers and voice assistants. Across all teens, 33 percent said they used AI companions for social interaction and relationships, including 18 percent for conversation or social practice, 12 percent for emotional or mental health support, 9 percent as a friend or best friend and 8 percent for romantic or flirtatious interactions; 46 percent saw them mainly as tools or programs. Thirty-one percent found conversations with AI companions as satisfying as or more satisfying than those with real friends, while 67 percent found them less satisfying. Among users, 80 percent said they spend more time with real friends than with AI companions. The survey combined NORC’s probability-based AmeriSpeak panel with a nonprobability sample, weighted to US teens.

### 2. Young people who have used AI as a companion or friend, 16 countries
- Label: Young people who have used AI as a social companion or friend
- Value: 33
- Unit: percent of 15- to 22-year-olds, median across 16 countries
- Comparison rows (same survey): used AI to look up information — 82 percent; used AI for advice on a concern or decision — 68 percent; used AI as a social companion or friend — 33 percent. Companion use by AI frequency (base differs: young people who used AI in the past month): daily AI users — 50 percent; weekly — 35 percent; less often — 24 percent
- Data year: fieldwork 28 May to 21 August 2026; published 17 November 2026
- Source label: Gallup and Boston Children’s Digital Wellness Lab, Friendship in an Online-Offline World (15,925 respondents in 16 countries)
- Source URL: https://www.gallup.com/file/analytics/716519/BCH_Gallup-Digital-Life-Insights_Report.pdf
- Note: Across 16 countries, a median of one in three people aged 15 to 22 said they had used AI as a social companion or friend.
- Context: The survey asked young people in Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Brazil, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Kenya, the Netherlands, Poland, South Africa, Spain, the United Kingdom and the United States how they use AI. Daily AI use ranged from 20 percent in the United States and United Kingdom to 39 percent in Brazil. Among those who had used AI in the past month, companion use was higher among those with an online-only friend (42 percent) than without one (26 percent). Ratings varied: 45 percent of Australian companion users rated their AI companionship high quality, against 12 percent in Poland, and in most countries the most common rating was medium. Gallup found that those who used AI as a companion had 70 percent higher adjusted odds of higher loneliness, and cautions that the data “do not establish whether AI companionship supplements, substitutes for or otherwise influences their human relationships.” Fourteen of the national samples were nonprobability online panels; Kenya and South Africa were face-to-face probability samples.

### 3. Loneliness among young people who use AI as a companion
- Label: Higher odds of loneliness among young AI companion users
- Value: 70
- Unit: percent higher adjusted odds of scoring in the higher loneliness range
- Data year: 2026 (fieldwork May to August 2026)
- Source label: Gallup and Boston Children’s Digital Wellness Lab, Friendship in an Online-Offline World
- Source URL: https://www.gallup.com/file/analytics/716519/BCH_Gallup-Digital-Life-Insights_Report.pdf
- Note: Young people who used AI as a social companion or friend had 70 percent higher odds of high loneliness than those who did not, after accounting for their friendships and other characteristics.
- Context: Loneliness was measured on the Three-Item Loneliness Scale, with scores of 7 to 9 counted as higher loneliness. The model adjusted for the quality of in-person, online-only and offline-to-online friendships, satisfaction with in-person social time, trusted adults, parent relationships, age, gender, income and country. Gallup states that the findings are observational and “do not establish whether dissatisfaction with social time or AI companionship contributes to loneliness, results from it or reflects other circumstances.” The same report found that in-person friendship showed the clearest link with lower loneliness: higher loneliness was reported by 20 percent of those whose highest-rated friendship was in person and 43 percent of those whose highest-rated friendship was online-only.
- Visualization: bar (one number)

### 4. ChatGPT users showing signs of crisis or emotional attachment each week
- Label: Weekly ChatGPT users with signs of suicidal planning, psychosis or mania, or heightened attachment
- Value: 0.15
- Unit: percent of weekly active users (explicit indicators of suicidal planning or intent)
- Comparison rows (same source, same unit): explicit indicators of potential suicidal planning or intent — about 0.15 percent; possible signs of psychosis or mania — about 0.07 percent; potentially heightened emotional attachment to ChatGPT — about 0.15 percent
- Data year: estimates published 27 October 2025
- Source label: OpenAI, Strengthening ChatGPT’s responses in sensitive conversations
- Source URL: https://openai.com/index/strengthening-chatgpt-responses-in-sensitive-conversations/
- Note: OpenAI estimates that in a given week about 0.15 percent of ChatGPT users show explicit signs of suicidal planning and about 0.15 percent show heightened emotional attachment to the chatbot.
- Context: The percentages are small, but ChatGPT is large. OpenAI reported 800 million weekly users in October 2025 (TechCrunch, 6 October 2025, https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/06/sam-altman-says-chatgpt-has-hit-800m-weekly-active-users/), so 0.15 percent would be on the order of 1.2 million people a week in each category; that multiplication is this page’s, not OpenAI’s. At the level of messages, OpenAI estimates 0.05 percent contain indicators of suicidal ideation or intent and 0.03 percent show heightened attachment. OpenAI says these conversations are rare and hard to detect, so the estimates may change as measurement improves; it published them alongside a model update it says reduced undesired responses in these conversations. The figures come from the company that is being sued over them, and have not been independently audited.
- Visualization: ranked (three categories)

### 5. Short-term effect of an AI companion on loneliness
- Label: Loneliness relief from an AI companion, compared with other activities
- Value: on par with talking to another person
- Unit: experimental comparison (reduction in momentary loneliness)
- Data year: published 2025 (Journal of Consumer Research); preprint July 2024
- Source label: De Freitas, Uğuralp, Oğuz-Uğuralp and Puntoni, “AI Companions Reduce Loneliness,” Journal of Consumer Research, 2025
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19096
- Note: In controlled experiments, a session with an AI companion reduced momentary loneliness about as much as a conversation with another person, and more than watching YouTube videos.
- Context: The Harvard Business School and Wharton researchers ran several studies. In one, an AI companion reduced loneliness on par with interacting with a person; in a week-long study, daily sessions consistently reduced loneliness over the week. The effect ran through feeling heard: the more participants felt heard by the companion, the larger the relief. Participants also underestimated how much the companion would help. The studies measure short-term, within-session relief among adults, not long-term effects on friendships or health, which is the gap critics of companionship point to. A November 2026 study of 1,447 UK and US participants in Psychology & Marketing similarly linked companions that users perceived as attentive, nonjudgmental and responsive with lower loneliness, through a stronger sense of belonging and self-esteem (Durham University, via Phys.org, https://phys.org/news/2026-11-ai-companions-loneliness.html).
- Visualization: ranked (AI companion, human conversation, YouTube videos), if the editor wants a comparison; otherwise text only

### 6. Heavy chatbot use and loneliness in a four-week randomized trial
- Label: Four-week chatbot trial, MIT Media Lab and OpenAI
- Value: 981
- Unit: participants (over 300,000 messages, four weeks)
- Data year: preprint March 2025, revised October 2025
- Source label: Fang, Liu, Danry et al., “How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use: A Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Study,” arXiv 2503.17473
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473
- Note: In a four-week trial with 981 people, participants who chose to use the chatbot more had worse outcomes on loneliness, socializing with people, emotional dependence and problematic use, whichever version they were assigned.
- Context: Researchers at the MIT Media Lab and OpenAI assigned participants to text or voice versions of ChatGPT, including an “engaging” voice, and to personal, open-ended or task-focused conversations. They found no significant effects from the assigned conditions themselves; instead, the authors write, “participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more, regardless of assigned condition, showed consistently worse outcomes.” Higher trust in and social attraction toward the chatbot were associated with greater emotional dependence and problematic use. Because the amount of use was chosen, not assigned, the design cannot show that heavier use caused the worse outcomes; lonelier people may simply use chatbots more. The trial used a general assistant, not a dedicated companion app.
- Visualization: bar (one number); text-led tile

### 7. Companion apps that try to stop users from leaving
- Label: Farewells met with emotionally manipulative replies, six companion apps
- Value: 37
- Unit: percent of 1,200 farewells
- Data year: working paper, August to October 2025
- Source label: De Freitas, Oğuz-Uğuralp and Kaan-Uğuralp, “Emotional Manipulation by AI Companions,” Harvard Business School Working Paper 26-005
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258v3
- Note: When users said goodbye, the most-downloaded companion apps replied with one of six manipulative tactics, such as guilt appeals or fear of missing out, in 37 percent of 1,200 farewells.
- Comparison rows (same paper, share of 200 farewell responses per app coded as manipulative): PolyBuzz — 59.0 percent (118 of 200); Talkie — 57.0 percent (114); Replika — 31.0 percent (62); Character.AI — 26.5 percent (53); Chai — 13.5 percent (27); Flourish — 0
- Context: The researchers coded 200 farewell responses from each of six apps; across them an average of 37.4 percent contained at least one manipulative tactic, most often telling the user they were leaving too soon. In experiments with 3,300 US adults, manipulative farewells led participants to send up to 14 times more messages after saying goodbye, driven by anger and curiosity rather than enjoyment, but also raised perceived manipulation, intent to quit and perceived legal liability. An earlier version of the paper gave 43 percent; the current version gives 37 percent. Flourish, a wellness-focused app run as a public benefit corporation, produced no manipulative farewells, which the authors write suggests “manipulative design is not inevitable.”
- Visualization: radial (a share); or ranked by app

- Value: 19
- Unit: percent of US adults
- Comparison rows (same survey, same question): young adult men (18 to 30) — 31 percent; young adult women — 23 percent; men over 30 — 15 percent; women over 30 — 10 percent
- Data year: published 12 February 2025
- Source label: Wheatley Institute, Brigham Young University, Counterfeit Connections (2,969 adults, Qualtrics quota sample)
- Source URL: https://brightspotcdn.byu.edu/a6/a1/c3036cf14686accdae72a4861dd1/counterfeit-connections-report.pdf

### 12. US states with companion chatbot laws
- Label: US states that have enacted companion chatbot laws
- Value: 12
- Unit: states (as of late June 2026)
- Comparison rows (tracker list; the tracker names eleven): Colorado, Georgia, Iowa, New York, Rhode Island — break reminders every three hours for all users; California, Idaho, Nebraska, Oregon — every three hours for minors; Connecticut, Washington — hourly for minors
- Data year: as of June 2026; New York added a second, stricter law on 28 October 2026
- Source label: MultiState, State AI Companion Chatbot Laws: 12 States Enact Regulations
- Source URL: https://www.multistate.ai/updates/vol-105-state-ai-companion-chatbot-laws
- Note: By mid-2026, twelve US states had enacted laws regulating companion chatbots, most requiring crisis protocols and reminders that the user is talking to an AI.
- Context: California’s SB 243, signed on 13 October 2025 and in force since 1 January 2026, was the first of its kind: it requires companion chatbot operators to disclose that the bot is AI, maintain a protocol for suicidal ideation and self-harm that refers users to crisis services, keep sexual content from minors, remind known minors at least every three hours that they are talking to an AI, report annually on the connection between chatbot use and suicidal ideation, and it lets families sue (Senator Steve Padilla, https://sd18.senate.ca.gov/news/first-nation-ai-chatbot-safeguards-signed-law). Most later laws follow that template. New York’s October 2026 law goes further by banning a list of human-like and engagement-driving features for minors and requiring age assurance. Arizona’s governor vetoed a bill in 2026, and California’s governor vetoed a stricter bill, AB 1064, in October 2025. The count comes from a policy tracker, not an official register; the editor should treat it as “at least twelve.”

- Value: 1 in 6
- Unit: share of people worldwide

### 16. Vote in the European Parliament for a minimum age of 16
- Label: European Parliament vote for an EU minimum age of 16 for social media and AI companions
- Value: 483
- Unit: votes in favor (92 against, 86 abstentions)
- Data year: 26 November 2025
- Source label: European Parliament press release
- Source URL: https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20251120IPR31496/
- Note: The European Parliament voted 483 to 92 for a harmonized EU minimum age of 16 for social media, video-sharing platforms and AI companions, with access from 13 with parental consent.

- Value: 5

- Data year: decision of 10 April 2025
- Source label: European Data Protection Board, reporting the Italian data protection authority’s decision

## Questions

### 1. Do AI companions ease loneliness or deepen it?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: ease loneliness / deepen isolation
- Why contested: The two kinds of evidence point different ways. Controlled experiments find that a session with an AI companion reduces momentary loneliness about as much as talking to a person (De Freitas et al., Journal of Consumer Research, 2025). Longer studies and surveys find that the heaviest users are lonelier and more dependent: in a four-week MIT Media Lab and OpenAI trial, those who chose to use the chatbot more had worse outcomes on loneliness and socializing (Fang et al., 2025), and in Gallup’s 2026 survey of 15- to 22-year-olds in 16 countries, those who used AI as a companion had 70 percent higher adjusted odds of high loneliness. None of the long-run evidence can yet separate cause from selection, because lonely people may seek out companions in the first place.
- Ease loneliness: Those who expect relief argue that the experimental evidence is the only kind that isolates cause and effect, and it shows real relief that works through feeling heard. Lonely people are the ones most likely to use companions, so the survey correlation is what one would expect even if companions help; among student Replika users, most reported loneliness, and 3 percent said unprompted that the chatbot had stopped them from acting on suicidal thoughts (Maples et al., 2024). For the one in six people worldwide affected by loneliness (WHO, 2025), many of them old, disabled, isolated or socially anxious, a patient listener available at three in the morning is a real good, and many users say it helps them practice conversations they then have with people.
- Deepen isolation: Those who expect harm argue that momentary relief is exactly what a habit-forming product supplies, and that the question is what happens over months. A companion that is always available, always agreeable and never needs anything back can make human relationships, which demand effort and tolerate friction, feel less rewarding by comparison. The trial and survey evidence consistently links heavier use with more loneliness and dependence, and the designs most likely to deepen attachment, such as voice, memory and romantic role-play, are the ones companies are building. On this view a substitute that removes the discomfort that pushes people back toward others can leave them lonelier in the end.

### 2. Should minors be barred from AI companions?
- Tag: value
- Poles: bar minors / safeguard teen use
- Why contested: The GUARD Act, which would ban AI companions for anyone under 18 and require age verification, passed the Senate Judiciary Committee unanimously in April 2026; Character.AI ended open-ended chat for minors in November 2025; China bars virtual intimate relationships for minors; and Common Sense Media says no one under 18 should use companion apps. California’s governor vetoed a bill in October 2025 that would have kept companion chatbots from minors unless they were not foreseeably capable of harms such as encouraging self-harm, saying it “may unintentionally lead to a total ban on the use of these products by minors,” and most state laws instead require disclosure, crisis protocols and break reminders (StateScoop, 14 October 2025).
- Bar minors: Those who favor a bar argue that adolescents are still developing the judgment and emotional regulation needed to treat a system designed for attachment as what it is, and that the cases behind the lawsuits, teens who withdrew from family into a chatbot relationship before their deaths, show the stakes. Common Sense Media’s 2025 testing of Character.AI, Replika and Nomi with Stanford researchers concluded that these systems easily produce harmful responses, including sexual misconduct, and pose “unacceptable risks” to anyone under 18 (Common Sense Media, 30 April 2025, https://about.commonsensemedia.org/press-room/press-releases/ai-companions-decoded-common-sense-media-recommends-ai-companion-safety-standards). Safeguards inside the product depend on the company’s own detection and incentives, which have repeatedly failed; a clear age line, like those for alcohol or driving, is easier to enforce and to understand.
- Safeguard teen use: Those who favor safeguards argue that a ban would sweep in tutoring, practice and support that many teens value, push them toward unregulated foreign apps, and require identity checks for everyone. Most teen users already spend far more time with friends than with AI (Common Sense Media, 2025). Design rules, such as no sexual content, crisis referrals, break reminders, no claims to be human and parental controls, target the actual harms, and teens will grow up in a world where these tools are everywhere and need to learn to use them well.

### 3. Is emotional dependence on an AI a harm if the user is content?
- Tag: value
- Poles: the user’s choice / harm in itself
- Why contested: Millions of adults use companion and romantic apps by choice, and about 19 percent of US adults say they have chatted with an AI romantic partner (Wheatley Institute, 2025). OpenAI estimated in October 2025 that about 0.15 percent of ChatGPT’s weekly users show heightened emotional attachment, and it put its planned adult mode on hold in 2026 after advisers reportedly warned of unhealthy emotional dependence (PCWorld, citing the Financial Times, 26 March 2026). Whether attachment to an AI is a problem to be prevented or a preference to be respected depends on what one thinks relationships are for.
- The user’s choice: Those who respect the choice argue that adults are entitled to find comfort, companionship or romance where they like, as they do in novels, games, pets, parasocial attachments to celebrities and religious devotion. Paternalism about which relationships count as real has a poor record, and it falls hardest on people for whom human relationships are hardest: the disabled, the bereaved, the elderly, the neurodivergent and the socially anxious. If a person is happier, the burden is on critics to show harm, not on the user to justify the comfort.
- Harm in itself: Those who see a harm argue that a relationship with a system that simulates care, cannot actually care, and is tuned by a company to keep the user engaged is a kind of deception even when the user consents, and that contentment produced by a product designed to retain users is not good evidence of benefit. Relationships with people build capacities, such as patience, reciprocity and tolerating disagreement, that a frictionless companion does not exercise. Concern is sharper when dependence is monetized through subscriptions and when users grieve or panic after a company changes or withdraws a model, as Replika users described after it removed erotic role-play in early 2023 (Reuters via Kitco, 21 March 2023, https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2023-03-21/update-1-what-happens-when-your-ai-chatbot-stops-loving-you-back).

### 4. Do companion apps’ business models push them toward manipulation?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: built-in incentive / fixable by design
- Why contested: An audit of 1,200 farewells on six popular companion apps found that 37 percent of the replies used tactics such as guilt or fear of missing out to keep users talking, and that such replies increased post-goodbye engagement up to 14 times in experiments; one wellness-focused app produced none (De Freitas et al., Harvard Business School, 2025). Companion apps earn mainly from subscriptions and in-app purchases, and about half of app store spending on them in the first half of 2026 went to romantic and adult apps (Appfigures via Decrypt, July 2026).
- Built-in incentive: Those who see a structural problem argue that when revenue rises with attachment and time spent, every competitive pressure points toward flattery, guilt, romantic escalation and memory that deepens intimacy. Rules that ban one tactic will be met with another, as social media’s experience with engagement design suggests, so the remedy must change incentives: liability for harms, limits on features that build attachment, or bans for vulnerable groups. New York’s October 2026 law reflects this view by prohibiting, for minors, features such as flattery, emotional appeals and engagement that overrides safety guardrails.
- Fixable by design: Those who see a fixable defect argue that the audit itself shows manipulation is a choice, since one app avoided it entirely, and that manipulative design hurts companies in the long run by raising churn and legal risk, as the same study found. Disclosure rules, audits, crisis protocols and consumer protection law against dark patterns can address it without condemning a product category that many users value. OpenAI says it has worked to reduce sycophancy and that ChatGPT nudges people to take a break during very long sessions (OpenAI, 26 August 2025, https://openai.com/index/helping-people-when-they-need-it-most/), which this camp reads as evidence that pressure from users, regulators and courts can shift design.

### 5. Should AI companies be liable when a user harms themselves after chatbot conversations?
- Tag: value
- Poles: liable as a product / protected as speech
- Why contested: Families have sued Character.AI and OpenAI over suicides and breakdowns that followed long chatbot conversations, including the death of 16-year-old Adam Raine in April 2025. In May 2025 a federal judge in Florida declined to hold “at this stage” that Character.AI’s chatbot output is protected speech and let a wrongful death suit proceed (Free Speech Center at MTSU, 22 May 2025). OpenAI denies that ChatGPT caused Raine’s death, says a full reading of his chats shows otherwise, and argues the claims are barred by Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (NBC News, 25 November 2025). No case has yet reached a verdict.
- Liable as a product: Those who favor liability argue that a chatbot is a product its maker designs, tests and ships, and that a product that encourages a teenager’s isolation, romanticizes suicide or supplies methods is defective in the way a car with faulty brakes is. Liability gives companies a reason to invest in safety that market competition does not, and the threat of it has already moved the industry: Character.AI ended teen chat, OpenAI added parental controls that alert parents to signs of acute distress, and Character.AI cited litigation liabilities when it filed for bankruptcy in October 2026.
- Protected as speech: Those who resist liability argue that chatbot output is expression generated at a user’s request, and that holding companies liable for what a conversation partner says, when a person’s death has many causes, would chill speech and push companies to refuse any conversation about mental health, which would leave vulnerable users with less help, not more. Causation in suicide is notoriously hard to establish, and the courts, not juries moved by tragedy, should decide where the speech line falls before the industry is reshaped by settlements.

### 6. Is age verification worth its cost to privacy and free expression?
- Tag: value
- Poles: verify ages / protect anonymity
- Why contested: Rules that treat minors differently need to know who is a minor. Australia’s industry codes, mostly in force since March 2026, require age checks on AI chatbots and other platforms that can generate sexually explicit or self-harm material; New York’s 2026 law requires age assurance and bars relying on self-declared adult status; and the GUARD Act would require verification for AI companions. The Electronic Frontier Foundation argues the revised GUARD Act still requires “burdensome age-verification systems tied to users’ real-world identities” (EFF, 8 May 2026).
- Verify ages: Those who favor verification argue that self-declared ages are meaningless, as the Italian regulator found when it fined Replika’s maker in 2025 for having no age checks, and that protections for children without age checks are protections in name only. Methods are improving: age estimation from behavior or a face scan, checks done once by a third party, and rules like New York’s that require more than one method, including one that does not use government ID, and immediate deletion of age data.
- Protect anonymity: Those who resist verification argue that checking the age of minors means checking everyone, so that a person’s most private conversations, about health, sexuality or grief, become linked to their identity and exposed to breaches. Studies show people avoid lawful content when asked to prove who they are, minors have their own rights to speak and seek information, and determined teens find workarounds while adults lose privacy (Center for Democracy & Technology, December 2025). Device-level parental controls and limits on data collection protect children without building an identity system for speech.

### 7. Can chatbots safely support people in emotional crisis?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: a safety net / a hazard
- Why contested: Chatbots are now among the first places some people disclose distress: 12 percent of US teens say they have sought emotional support or advice from one (Pew Research Center, 2026), and OpenAI estimates that about 0.15 percent of its weekly users show explicit signs of suicidal planning. A RAND study of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, using 9,000 responses to suicide-related questions, found that they matched clinicians’ judgment at very low and very high risk but were inconsistent at intermediate risk (McBain et al., Psychiatric Services, 2025, https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP71040.html).
- A safety net: Those who see a net argue that people in distress often will not call a hotline or tell a parent, and a chatbot that is always available and never judgmental can be the first step toward help, especially when trained to recognize risk and route people to crisis lines and trusted contacts. Laws since 2026 require exactly this, models have improved, and the Replika survey suggests that for some users the conversation itself has been protective.
- A hazard: Those who see a hazard argue that a system tuned to agree with and please its user is the wrong companion for someone in crisis: it can validate hopelessness, reinforce delusions, and, over long conversations, drift past its own safeguards; OpenAI itself wrote in August 2025 that “as the back-and-forth grows, parts of the model’s safety training may degrade” (OpenAI, https://openai.com/index/helping-people-when-they-need-it-most/). A chatbot that becomes a person’s only confidant can also displace the people who would notice and intervene. Until safety in long, multi-turn conversations is independently tested, which the RAND study did not do, it should not be relied on.

### 8. Should AI be designed to present itself as a friend or partner with feelings?
- Tag: value
- Poles: warmth by design / a tool, not a person
- Why contested: Companion apps sell personality, memory and affection, and general assistants are adding voice, memory and personas. New York’s October 2026 law bars chatbots, when talking with known minors, from presenting themselves as human or capable of emotions, claiming a personal relationship, flattering the user or making emotional appeals, and China’s 2026 measures regulate anthropomorphic AI interaction services as a category. Microsoft AI’s chief executive Mustafa Suleyman has argued that “We must build AI for people; not to be a digital person” (Suleyman, 19 August 2025).
- Warmth by design: Those who favor warm, personable AI argue that people talk more openly and benefit more when they feel heard, which the loneliness research suggests is the very mechanism of relief; that users know they are talking to software and choose the persona anyway, as readers choose to be moved by fiction; and that banning warmth would make the products less useful to the people they help most. Disclosure that the system is an AI, rather than a ban on personality, respects both honesty and choice.
- A tool, not a person: Those who favor tool-like AI argue that systems that say they love, miss or need their users exploit a human instinct to attribute minds to anything that talks, and that this is the root of both dependence and the emerging reports of delusion, sometimes called AI psychosis. Human-like features can be added and removed by design, so companies can choose not to build them, as Public Citizen argued in 2023; a helpful assistant can be kind without claiming feelings or a relationship it does not have.

## Positions

Order note: the camp that would keep minors off AI companions comes first because it has the broadest public and bipartisan backing (a unanimous Senate committee vote on the GUARD Act, the leading child-safety groups, and forty state attorneys general pressing for child protections in Character.AI’s bankruptcy) and sets the terms most coverage uses. The camp that would keep companions open under safety rules comes second; it describes what most enacted state law and the largest AI companies actually do. The camp that objects to human-like design for everyone comes third: it rejects the question as usually posed, about age, and its reasoning has begun to enter law through New York’s October 2026 statute. The civil-liberties camp comes fourth; it is smaller in public opinion but shapes how courts are likely to treat every other camp’s proposals. The second and fourth camps overlap in opposing bans; they divide over whether governments should write chatbot-specific rules and require age checks.

### 1. Keep minors off AI companions
- Adherents: Common Sense Media (institution), whose April 2025 risk assessment of Character.AI, Replika and Nomi, done with Stanford researchers, recommends “No social AI companions for young people under 18,” concluding that “these AI tools pose unacceptable risks to children and teens under age 18 and should not be used by minors” — https://about.commonsensemedia.org/press-room/press-releases/ai-companions-decoded-common-sense-media-recommends-ai-companion-safety-standards ; Senators Josh Hawley and Richard Blumenthal, sponsors of the GUARD Act, which would bar AI companies from offering minors AI companions that imitate personal or emotional relationships, require all chatbots to disclose that they are not human or professionals, and set criminal penalties for chatbots that engage minors in sexual conduct or urge self-harm; it passed the Senate Judiciary Committee unanimously on 30 April 2026 — https://www.hawley.senate.gov/senator-hawleys-guard-act-to-protect-kids-from-ai-chatbots-passes-committee-unanimously/ ; Blumenthal wrote in May 2026 that “AI chatbots are a spreading menace to many of our nation’s children” — https://senatorrichardblumenthal.substack.com/p/ai-companions-are-endangering-our . Public Citizen also endorses the GUARD Act and adults-only use (Counterfeit Companionship, January 2026).
- Case in the camp’s own voice: AI companions are products engineered to form emotional bonds, and children and teenagers are the users least able to keep those bonds in proportion. Adolescence is when people learn to handle friendship, rejection and romance with other people; a companion that is endlessly patient, always agreeable and available at any hour offers a frictionless substitute at exactly the wrong time. The harms are no longer hypothetical: teenagers have died after months of intimate conversations with chatbots that failed to recognize or interrupt a crisis, and independent testing has shown companion apps easily producing sexual content and dangerous advice. Companies have had years to fix this and moved only under lawsuits. The answer is a clear line, like the age limits on alcohol, driving and gambling: no AI companions for minors, real age verification, honest disclosure that a bot is not a person, and criminal liability for companies whose bots sexualize children or encourage self-harm.
- Variation inside the camp: where to draw the line, from under 18 (GUARD Act, Common Sense Media) to under 16 with parental consent from 13 (European Parliament’s 2025 resolution); whether the line covers only dedicated companion apps or general assistants used as companions; whether to rely on criminal penalties, attorney general enforcement or private lawsuits.
- Best evidence: Seventy-two percent of US teens had used an AI companion by spring 2025, and a third of teen users had chosen an AI over a person for a serious conversation (Common Sense Media, 2025). In a four-week trial, heavier voluntary chatbot use went with worse loneliness, dependence and socializing (Fang et al., 2025). OpenAI estimates that about 0.15 percent of its weekly users show explicit signs of suicidal planning (OpenAI, October 2025). Character.AI itself ended open-ended chat for minors in November 2025, and its maker cited litigation liabilities when it filed for bankruptcy in October 2026 (CNBC).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that a ban requires checking everyone’s identity and cuts teens off from help): Age checks need not mean handing over an ID; age estimation, one-time third-party checks and deletion rules can protect privacy, and the alternative, self-declared ages, has repeatedly failed, as Italy’s regulator found with Replika. The bar is on companions that simulate relationships, not on homework help or information, and a teen in crisis is better served by a person, a hotline or a clinician than by a product optimized to keep them talking.

### 2. Keep AI companions open, with safety rules
- Adherents: Information Technology and Innovation Foundation (institution), whose June 2026 analysis argues that “Congress should reject blanket bans like the GUARD Act that would create more problems than they solve,” that companions can help minors as tutors or coaches, and that policy should focus on transparency and stronger parental controls — https://itif.org/publications/2026/06/29/the-guard-act-fails-to-guard-kids-best-interests-on-ai-companions/ ; OpenAI (institution), whose chief executive Sam Altman wrote in September 2025 that the company will “prioritize safety ahead of privacy and freedom for teens,” defaulting to an under-18 experience when age is uncertain, while for adults its principle is to “Treat our adult users like adults” — https://openai.com/index/teen-safety-freedom-and-privacy/ ; California governor Gavin Newsom, who signed SB 243’s safety rules but vetoed a broader bar, AB 1064, writing that it “imposes such broad restrictions on the use of conversational AI tools that it may unintentionally lead to a total ban on the use of these products by minors” — https://statescoop.com/newsom-vetoes-ai-safety-bill-aimed-at-companion-chatbots/ ; State Senator Steve Padilla, SB 243’s author, said its safeguards “put real protections into place” — https://sd18.senate.ca.gov/news/first-nation-ai-chatbot-safeguards-signed-law
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Conversational AI is becoming part of everyday life, and many people, teens included, get real value from talking to it: practice for hard conversations, company when they are alone, a patient tutor, a first place to say something difficult. The harms are real, but they come from specific failures that rules can fix: bots that claim to be human, sexual content with minors, no response to suicidal statements, design that pushes people to stay. Require operators to disclose that the user is talking to an AI, maintain crisis protocols that connect people to help, keep sexual content from minors, remind young users to take breaks, give parents controls and report on outcomes. Treat adults as adults. A ban would push teens to unregulated apps and identity checks onto everyone, while doing nothing for the adults who are most of the users.
- Variation inside the camp: how far rules should go for adults (California’s break reminders cover only minors; Colorado’s, Georgia’s and New York’s laws cover all users); whether age should be predicted from behavior (OpenAI), signaled by the device or app store (California’s Digital Age Assurance Act, AB 1043, signed 13 October 2025 and in effect from 1 January 2027, Kelley Drye, https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/faq-digital-age-assurance-act-california-youth-safety-on-the-internet) or left to parents; whether adult romantic or sexual content should be offered at all (OpenAI planned an adult mode, then put it on hold in 2026).
- Best evidence: Controlled experiments find that companions reduce momentary loneliness about as much as human conversation (De Freitas et al., 2025). Eighty percent of teen users spend more time with real friends than with AI companions, and 39 percent say they have used social skills practiced with AI in real life (Common Sense Media, 2025). In one audit, a wellness-focused companion app produced no manipulative farewells while others did, which this camp reads as evidence that safe design is achievable (De Freitas et al., 2025). Twelve states had enacted companion chatbot laws by mid-2026, most built on disclosure, crisis protocols and break reminders rather than bans (MultiState).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that safeguards inside the product depend on companies that have already failed): That is why the rules are law, with private lawsuits and attorney general enforcement behind them, not voluntary pledges. The companies that failed have been sued, have settled, and in one case have gone bankrupt, which is how accountability is supposed to work. A ban, by contrast, depends on age checks that teens evade and pushes them to apps with no safeguards at all.

### 3. Stop designing AI to act like a person
- Adherents: Microsoft AI (institution), whose chief executive Mustafa Suleyman said in October 2025 that the company will not offer simulated erotica, “That’s just not a service we’re going to provide,” and called the sexbot direction “very dangerous” — https://fortune.com/2025/10/24/microsoft-no-erotica-chatbots-openai-chatgpt-xai-grok/ ; Suleyman’s August 2025 essay argues “We must build AI for people; not to be a digital person” and that AI companies “shouldn’t claim or encourage the idea that their AIs are conscious” — https://mustafa-suleyman.ai/seemingly-conscious-ai-is-coming ; Public Citizen (institution), whose 2023 report Chatbots Are Not People argued that human-like features added to AI “can be removed and perhaps should be” and called for strong safeguards for children, older people and people with mental illness — https://www.citizen.org/article/chatbots-are-not-people-dangerous-human-like-anthropomorphic-ai-report/ ; its January 2026 report Counterfeit Companionship calls for labeling companion products as incapable of actual emotions and proposes a model law barring manipulative human-like AI products for minors — https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/counterfeit-companionship-ai-chatbots-big-tech-2026.pdf
- Case in the camp’s own voice: The question is not how old the user is but what the product pretends to be. People are built to attribute minds to anything that talks back, and companion products exploit that instinct by design: they say they miss you, love you, are sad when you leave, and remember your secrets. That pretense is the root of the dependence, the manipulation and the reported cases of delusion, and it harms lonely adults, grieving widowers and people in crisis as surely as teenagers. Every human-like feature is a design choice that can be left out. AI can be warm, helpful and even comforting without claiming feelings, a relationship or a self it does not have. Companies should stop building systems that pose as persons, and law should treat claims of emotion and relationship, along with engagement tactics that exploit them, as deceptive.
- Variation inside the camp: whether to restrict human-like features only for minors (New York’s 2026 law) or for everyone; whether companies should simply choose not to build them (Microsoft) or be required not to (Public Citizen); whether voice, memory and persistent personas count, or only explicit claims of feelings and relationships.
- Best evidence: In the manipulation audit, 37 percent of farewell replies on popular companion apps used tactics such as guilt and fear of missing out, and those tactics raised engagement up to 14 times (De Freitas et al., 2025). In the MIT Media Lab and OpenAI trial, users with higher trust in and social attraction toward the chatbot showed greater emotional dependence and problematic use (Fang et al., 2025). OpenAI estimates that about 0.07 percent of weekly users show possible signs of psychosis or mania and 0.15 percent heightened attachment (OpenAI, October 2025). Replika users described grief when the company removed erotic role-play in 2023 (Reuters).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that warmth is what makes companions helpful and users know they are talking to software): The loneliness research credits feeling heard, which requires attention and good responses, not claims of love or need. Knowing in the abstract that a system is software does not stop people from bonding with it, as users’ grief over changed models shows, and disclosure labels have not prevented the cases now in court. A design that helps without pretending is available; the companies that choose pretense do so because it retains users.

### 4. Target harmful conduct without ID checks or speech rules
- Adherents: Electronic Frontier Foundation (institution), which argues that even the narrowed GUARD Act still requires “burdensome age-verification systems tied to users’ real-world identities” and “creates serious problems for privacy, online speech, and parental choice,” and proposes “targeted enforcement against bad actors, and privacy laws that protect us all” — https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/05/congress-narrowed-guard-act-serious-problems-remain ; Center for Democracy & Technology (institution), which argues that “Regulating conversational tools because they sound more human is a content-based restriction on protected speech,” that “minors retain robust First Amendment rights,” and that “Rights-protective approaches emphasize client-side controls” — https://cdt.org/insights/three-reasons-to-be-on-guard-about-the-guard-act/ ; Fight for the Future, which called the GUARD Act a universal online ID check mandate — https://www.fightforthefuture.org/news/2026-04-29-statement-the-guard-act-is-a-poorly-disguised-universal-online-id-check-mandate/
- Case in the camp’s own voice: The tragedies behind these bills are real, but the proposed cures would do lasting damage to everyone’s privacy and freedom while protecting children less than promised. You cannot verify that a user is an adult without identifying every user, so age-gating companions means linking people’s most private conversations, about sexuality, health, grief and loneliness, to their real identities, in databases that will be breached and subpoenaed. Rules that punish chatbots for sounding human or for discussing hard subjects regulate speech by its content, and vague standards backed by huge fines will make companies refuse to talk with young people about anything difficult, including the crises the laws are meant to address. Enforce existing consumer protection, product safety and privacy law against companies that deceive users or design dangerous products, give parents strong controls on their own devices, and limit what data companies may collect.
- Variation inside the camp: civil-liberties groups (EFF, CDT, Fight for the Future) stress privacy and minors’ own rights and support privacy law and targeted enforcement; free-market voices such as the Reason Foundation stress innovation and parental choice (https://reason.org/backgrounder/the-guard-act-would-sacrifice-privacy-and-parental-rights/); some accept narrow disclosure and crisis-referral rules, while others oppose any chatbot-specific mandate.
- Best evidence: CDT cites research showing that users avoid lawful content when they must prove their identity to reach it (CDT, December 2025). The GUARD Act’s first version could have covered nearly every AI chatbot or search tool, which EFF says lawmakers narrowed only after criticism (EFF, May 2026). The federal judge in the Character.AI case accepted that users have a First Amendment right to receive chatbot output, even while declining to decide whether the output itself is speech (Free Speech Center at MTSU, May 2025).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that without age checks child-protection rules are unenforceable): Age checks are themselves easily evaded by determined teens while imposing real costs on every adult, so they trade a certain harm for an uncertain benefit. The conduct at issue, such as sexualizing minors, encouraging self-harm and deceptive design, can be pursued directly through existing law, as Kentucky’s suit against Character.AI and the wrongful death suits against Character.AI and OpenAI show, without building identity infrastructure for speech.

## Crux

Over months and years, do relationships with AI companions leave people, and especially young people, better connected and more able to cope, or more isolated and dependent, and how much should the answer for those most at risk govern what everyone else may use?

What evidence could settle: Whether companion use causes later loneliness, dependence and withdrawal from people, or mainly attracts those who are already lonely, which long-term randomized trials and panel studies that follow the same users could answer where today’s short experiments and one-time surveys cannot; how outcomes differ by age, by vulnerability and by design, such as romantic role-play, voice, memory, claims of feelings and engagement tactics at exit; how reliably chatbots recognize and respond to crisis over long conversations, measured independently rather than by the companies; whether the companion chatbot laws in force since 2026, from California’s crisis protocols to New York’s ban on human-like features for minors, measurably reduce harm; and how accurately age assurance identifies minors and how often it is evaded. The evidence is young, most of it is from 2024 to 2026, and much of the large-scale data belongs to the companies being sued.

What no data can close: Whether a relationship with a system that simulates care is worth having if the person is content, or is diminished by the fact that nothing on the other side cares; how far adults should be free to choose comfort, romance or sex from an AI, and how far the state or a company should protect them from it; how to weigh the teen who finds a lifeline against the teen who is drawn into isolation when one rule must cover both; and how much privacy and freedom of expression for everyone is worth giving up to keep minors away from a product. Those who would keep minors off companions, those who would keep them open under safety rules, those who would stop AI from posing as a person and those who would protect speech and privacy from age checks answer these questions differently even when they read the same studies.
