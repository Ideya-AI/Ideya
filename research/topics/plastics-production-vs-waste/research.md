# Plastics — research

## Overview

The world makes far more plastic than it can collect, recycle or safely dispose of, and the question this topic covers is where to act: by capping and reducing how much new plastic is made, or by managing what becomes waste better through collection, recycling, redesign and producer-funded cleanup. That choice now sits at the center of the United Nations negotiations for a legally binding treaty on plastic pollution, launched by the UN Environment Assembly in March 2022 (resolution 5/14) to cover the full life cycle of plastic, and it runs through national policy too: bans and taxes on single-use items, extended producer responsibility laws that make brands pay for packaging waste, recycled-content mandates, limits on waste exports, and fights over whether “advanced” or chemical recycling works. The topic covers production limits, product and chemical rules, waste management, recycling and the waste trade, and the health and climate arguments as they bear on that choice. It deliberately excludes the wider question of how fast to phase out fossil fuels (its own page, fossil-fuel-phaseout), water access (its own page), and personal shopping habits except where policy shapes them.

Why now: the treaty talks have entered what their chair calls the final phase. After the fifth negotiating session collapsed twice, in Busan in December 2024 and in Geneva in August 2025, the committee elected Chilean ambassador Julio Cordano as its new chair on 7 February 2026 (CIEL, https://www.ciel.org/news/inc-5-3-reaction/). In August 2026 he circulated an informal “Aid to Negotiations” text, which campaigners and the Scientists’ Coalition for an Effective Plastics Treaty criticized for bracketing or dropping provisions on production (Renewable Matter, https://www.renewablematter.eu/en/plastics-treaty-cordano-presents-aid-to-negotiations-but-disappoints-science). After closed-door talks among heads of delegation in Bangkok on 27 to 30 September 2026, Cordano told reporters: “My plan and my expectation is that we will be able to adopt the treaty in INC 5.4 (March meeting), if we follow the correct steps” (AFP via France 24, 1 October 2026, https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20261001-plastic-treaty-talks-chair-sees-deal-possible-in-march). That session is scheduled for 13 to 24 March 2027, venue to be announced (Geneva Environment Network tracker, https://www.genevaenvironmentnetwork.org/resources/updates/international-cooperation-on-plastic-pollution/). On the waste side, the European Union banned exports of plastic waste to non-OECD countries from 21 November 2026, under its Waste Shipment Regulation; in 2025 about half of the 1 to 1.5 million tonnes it exports each year went to non-OECD countries such as Malaysia, Indonesia and Vietnam (European Commission, 20 November 2026, https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/eu-bans-plastic-waste-exports-non-oecd-countries-2026-11-20_en).

The debate is live because the two diagnoses lead to different treaties. Most negotiating countries, small island states, environmental and health groups and many scientists argue that production is growing faster than any waste system can keep up with, so a treaty that ignores production cannot end pollution; 97 countries endorsed a June 2025 call for a global target to reduce production and consumption of primary plastic polymers (French Ministry of Ecological Transition, https://www.ecologie.gouv.fr/en/rendez-vous/nice-wake-up-call-for-an-ambitious-plastics-treaty). A smaller bloc of oil- and gas-producing states led by Saudi Arabia, joined since 2025 by the United States under the Trump administration, along with the petrochemical industry, argues that plastic itself is not the pollutant, that the problem is uncollected and mismanaged waste, and that limits on production would raise costs and lie outside the treaty’s mandate (AP via PBS, 15 August 2025, https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/plastic-pollution-treaty-talks-in-geneva-end-without-an-agreement; Washington Post via Anchorage Daily News, 24 August 2026, https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/08/24/leaked-diplomatic-memo-shows-how-the-us-aims-to-weaken-global-limits-on-plastic-production/). Between them, a coalition of more than 330 businesses, financial institutions and NGOs, convened by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation and WWF, wants harmonized global rules on product design, problem products, chemicals and producer responsibility, which it says would move the world toward sustainable levels of production and consumption, without calling for a cap on production (Business Coalition for a Global Plastics Treaty, 25 June 2025, https://www.businessforplasticstreaty.org/latest/the-business-coalition-for-a-global-plastics-treaty-calls-for-the-adoption-of-harmonised-regulations-to-end-plastic-pollution-at-inc-5-2, and 3 September 2026, https://www.businessforplasticstreaty.org/latest/our-statement-on-the-aid-to-negotiations). Because the treaty process has operated by consensus, a minority can block, and whether to keep it that way is itself part of the fight. Waste pickers are a further constituency: Pew’s 2025 assessment finds that more than three-quarters of all plastic recycled globally is collected and sorted by them, so their livelihoods depend on how any transition is designed (https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/reports/2025/12/breaking-the-plastic-wave-2025).

Viz: bar

Source label: Marfella et al., “Microplastics and Nanoplastics in Atheromas and Cardiovascular Events,” New England Journal of Medicine (March 2024), abstract on PubMed

Note: In an Italian study of 257 surgery patients, polyethylene was found in the artery plaque of 150, and they had about 4.5 times the risk of heart attack, stroke or death over about three years.
Context: The study was observational: it shows an association, not that microplastics caused the events, and people with more plastic in their plaque may differ in other ways. Its hazard ratio was 4.53 (95 percent confidence interval 2.00 to 10.27). It is the most-cited human evidence in the health argument for production limits, along with a 2025 Nature Medicine study reporting rising microplastic levels in human brains between 2016 and 2024 (Nihart et al.). The measurement method used in both, pyrolysis gas chromatography mass spectrometry, has been criticized for misreading fats as polyethylene; Germany’s Federal Institute for Risk Assessment wrote of the brain study that “the measured amounts appear implausibly high” and that “there is no reliable toxicological evidence of health risks from the intake of microplastics via food” (BfR, December 2025, https://www.bfr.bund.de/en/notification/microplastics-in-the-brain/). The Lancet Countdown on health and plastics, by contrast, states that plastics are responsible for health-related economic losses exceeding US$1.5 trillion a year, a figure built on a small number of cost studies (Landrigan et al., 2025).

## Questions

Question: Can waste collection and recycling grow fast enough to keep up with plastic production?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Waste systems can catch up / Production will outrun them
Why contested: Both sides accept that most plastic pollution today escapes from uncollected and mismanaged waste. They disagree about whether that gap is a temporary infrastructure deficit that money and policy can close, or a structural result of producing more plastic, much of it hard to recycle, than any system can absorb.
Case for “Waste systems can catch up”: High-income countries already show that near-universal collection and controlled disposal stop almost all leakage: the 2024 Nature inventory by Cottom and colleagues found high-income countries contribute about 0.3 percent of global macroplastic emissions, and China cut its emissions sharply by building incineration and sanitary landfill. The ICCA notes that about 2.7 billion people lack solid waste collection; extending it is a known engineering and financing problem, which the OECD costed at about EUR 25 billion a year for low- and middle-income countries. Producer responsibility fees, deposit systems and recycled-content mandates can pay for collection and create demand for recycled material. On this view the fastest way to cut pollution is to fund collection where it is missing, not to restrict a material whose production happens mostly in places that already manage their waste.
Case for “Production will outrun them”: Pew’s 2025 modeling finds that even with considerable investment, waste management capacity grows 26 percent between 2025 and 2040 while primary production grows 52 percent, so the absolute volume of unmanaged plastic rises. Recycling has stayed near 9 percent globally for years, because most plastic is mixed, multilayer, contaminated or additive-laden and loses value each cycle; landfill and incineration only move the problem into soil, air and emissions. Microplastics from tires, paint and textiles, a growing share of pollution, cannot be collected at all. Every tonne made eventually becomes waste, so the only way to make the problem smaller rather than chase it is to make less.

Question: Should a global treaty bind countries to reduce how much plastic they produce?
Tag: value
Poles: Global binding target / National choice
Why contested: Even people who agree production is too high disagree about whether a UN treaty should set a collective limit on an industrial output, which would fall unevenly on producer economies, or leave each country to set its own path, as the Paris Agreement does for emissions.
Case for “Global binding target”: Plastic and plastic pollution cross borders through trade, rivers and oceans, so national limits leak: a country that restricts production simply imports more. A shared target, like the Montreal Protocol’s phase-down of ozone-depleting chemicals, gives every producer the same rules and investors a clear signal, and it can be set as a reduction in primary polymers that leaves room for recycled and essential uses. Small island states, which produce almost no plastic but receive it on their shores, argue that voluntary national plans have not worked for any global pollutant and that only a binding target matches the scale of the problem.
Case for “National choice”: Plastic is embedded in food safety, medicine, housing, vehicles and energy, and its role differs between a rich country and one still building its economy. A global cap would be a production quota on a commodity, raising prices for the poorest consumers and locking in today’s producers; the US State Department has rejected proposals that would, in its words, “damage the American economy, drive inflation, and restrict consumer choice.” Producer states read the 2022 mandate as covering plastic pollution rather than plastic production itself, and treaties that countries can actually ratify and implement, such as nationally determined plans, have more effect than ambitious texts that key producers refuse to join.

Question: How much harm do microplastics and plastic chemicals do to human health?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Serious, growing harm / Unproven so far
Why contested: Plastic particles and plastic-associated chemicals have been measured in blood, placentas, arteries and brains, and some additives such as certain phthalates and bisphenols have established links to disease. But measurement methods are new and contested, and causal evidence for particles in humans is thin.
Case for “Serious, growing harm”: The Lancet Countdown on health and plastics concludes that plastics cause disease and death from infancy to old age, linking plastic chemicals to cancer, heart disease, reduced fertility and harm to children’s neurodevelopment, and estimating health-related losses above US$1.5 trillion a year. A 2024 New England Journal of Medicine study found that patients with microplastics in their artery plaque had about 4.5 times the risk of heart attack, stroke or death over three years. More than 4,200 chemicals used in plastics are of concern and hazard data are missing for about two-thirds of the 16,000 identified. When the evidence of exposure is universal and the plausible harms are severe, waiting for proof means waiting until the damage is done, which is the lesson of lead and tobacco.
Case for “Unproven so far”: Germany’s Federal Institute for Risk Assessment found “no reliable toxicological evidence of health risks from the intake of microplastics via food” and called reported brain concentrations “implausibly high,” citing analytical methods that can mistake fats for polyethylene. The artery study is observational, with 257 patients, and cannot separate plastic from other differences between patients. Specific harmful additives are best handled by chemical regulation targeting those substances, as with existing bans on certain phthalates, rather than by limiting all plastic. Policy built on alarming but fragile findings risks misdirecting money and losing public trust when results fail to replicate.

Question: Can recycling, including chemical recycling, scale up to close the loop?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Recycling can scale / Recycling is a dead end
Why contested: Recycling is the main promise of the waste-management approach, and industry has invested in chemical or “advanced” recycling to handle plastics that mechanical recycling cannot. Critics, including California’s attorney general in a 2024 lawsuit against ExxonMobil that is still being litigated, argue that the promise has been oversold.
Case for “Recycling can scale”: Mechanical recycling already supplies about 9.5 percent of world plastics production, according to Plastics Europe, and bottles reach around 29 percent recycling in the United States even without national mandates; in countries with deposit systems, bottle return rates are far higher. Recycling rates are low mainly because collection and sorting are underfunded and virgin plastic is cheap, both of which policy can change through producer responsibility fees, design-for-recycling rules and recycled-content mandates such as the EU’s. Chemical recycling can turn mixed and contaminated plastics back into virgin-quality feedstock, and it is early in its scale-up, as solar power once was.
Case for “Recycling is a dead end”: Global recycling has stayed near 9 percent for decades, and in the United States the EPA’s rate was 8.7 percent in 2018. Most plastics are not one material but thousands of polymer and additive combinations, so recycled output is degraded, often contaminated with chemicals of concern, and usually used once more before disposal. Chemically recycled output was about 0.1 percent of world production in 2024 on Plastics Europe’s figures, and much pyrolysis output is burned as fuel. A strategy that depends on recycling has been promised for 40 years; continued failure is evidence about the material, not just about funding.

Question: Would using less plastic raise emissions and costs through worse substitutes?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Substitutes do worse / Reduction does better
Why contested: Plastic is light and cheap, so swapping it for glass, metal or paper can raise transport emissions, food waste and costs. Whether that makes production limits counterproductive depends on whether less plastic means switching materials or using less material overall.
Case for “Substitutes do worse”: A 2022 McKinsey analysis of US applications found plastics had lower life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions than the alternatives used at scale in 13 of 14 cases, with savings of 10 to 90 percent. Plastic packaging extends food shelf life, and lightweight plastic parts cut fuel use in vehicles and aircraft. Paper bags and glass bottles have higher per-use footprints unless reused many times. A production cap would push users toward heavier, costlier or less hygienic materials, raising prices for the poorest households and emissions for everyone.
Case for “Reduction does better”: The relevant comparison is not plastic versus glass but plastic versus no single-use item at all: reuse systems, refill, concentrates and less packaging. Pew’s system-change scenario cuts primary production 44 percent below business as usual by 2040 while, Pew says, maintaining the same level of service, and reduces plastic-related emissions 38 percent; reuse accounts for about two-thirds of the packaging reduction. Primary plastic production alone emitted about 2.24 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent in 2019, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and life-cycle studies of plastic rarely count the costs of pollution, open burning and health damage.

Question: Is plastic pollution mainly a failure of waste systems in poorer countries or of overproduction by richer economies?
Tag: value
Poles: Uncollected waste in poorer countries / Overproduction and overconsumption
Why contested: The data show two different maps: where plastic leaks into the environment (mostly low- and middle-income countries with gaps in collection) and where plastic is made and consumed per person (mostly China, the United States, the Gulf, Europe and other richer economies). Which map defines responsibility shapes who should act and who should pay.
Case for “Uncollected waste in poorer countries”: The 2024 Nature inventory found 69 percent of macroplastic emissions come from 20 low- and middle-income countries, led by India, Nigeria and Indonesia, and that uncollected waste, not littering, drives them. High-income countries generate more plastic per person but leak almost none because they collect and manage it. The most direct way to stop plastic reaching rivers and oceans is to finance collection, sanitary landfill and recycling where it is missing, which also protects the health of people living near open dumps and burning.
Case for “Overproduction and overconsumption”: Upper-middle- and high-income economies make nearly 80 percent of the world’s virgin plastic, according to Pew, and the average American generates about 221 kilograms of plastic waste a year, around three times the Japanese and Korean average. Rich countries have exported millions of tonnes of their waste to poorer ones for decades, counting it as recycled. Producers and brands in rich economies decide what is made and how it is packaged; framing pollution as a Global South waste problem shifts responsibility to the countries with the least money and the least say over what they receive.

Question: Who should pay for plastic waste and pollution?
Tag: value
Poles: Producers pay / Shared public cost
Why contested: Collecting and managing plastic waste costs governments tens of billions of dollars a year, and the treaty talks have stalled over finance as well as production. The options include producer responsibility fees, a global fee on primary polymers, development aid from richer countries, and ordinary public budgets and user charges.
Case for “Producers pay”: Those who make and sell plastic profit from it, while cleanup falls on municipalities, taxpayers and the communities living with pollution. Extended producer responsibility, now law in the EU and seven US states, makes brands fund collection and gives them a reason to design packaging that is cheaper to recycle; a fee on primary polymers would raise money for the countries least able to pay and nudge production toward recycled material. The Business Coalition for a Global Plastics Treaty estimates that harmonized global rules could more than double cumulative producer responsibility revenues between 2026 and 2040, to $576 billion, compared with $279 billion under a treaty of voluntary national measures.
Case for “Shared public cost”: Waste management is a basic public service like water and sanitation, and every consumer generates waste. Fees on producers are passed through to prices, so they are in effect consumption taxes that fall hardest on the poor; they also add compliance costs that favor large firms. Development finance, public budgets and user charges are more transparent and can be targeted at the communities without collection, and a global polymer levy would hand a new taxing power to an international body without the accountability that national governments face.

Question: Should countries that want an ambitious treaty go ahead without the holdouts?
Tag: value
Poles: Wait for a universal deal / Proceed without holdouts
Why contested: The talks have operated by consensus, which has let a small group of producer states block production and chemical measures at Busan and Geneva. Proposals range from forcing votes under UN rules to concluding a separate treaty among willing countries, as was done for landmines.
Case for “Wait for a universal deal”: A plastics treaty without the main producers, including the United States, Saudi Arabia, Russia and possibly China and India, would cover little of the world’s production and invite trade disputes. Consensus is slow but produces agreements that countries implement; the chair, Julio Cordano, has said he aims for a “treaty that will be accepted by everyone,” and voting on substance would break the norm that has made environmental treaties durable. Patient negotiation already moved the United States and others in past rounds.
Case for “Proceed without holdouts”: Four years of consensus have produced no treaty, and a small minority has used the rule to veto measures backed by a large majority. A treaty among the many willing countries, including the large consumer markets of the EU and others, can still set product standards that exporters must meet to sell there, and later draw in holdouts, as the landmine and cluster munitions treaties did. CIEL argued after the February 2026 session that it showed governments can use other multilateral tools, including voting, when needed.

Question: Should rich countries be allowed to export plastic waste for recycling abroad?
Tag: value
Poles: Trade helps recycling / Exports shift the burden
Why contested: Waste exports let materials flow to where they can be processed most cheaply, but for decades much exported plastic was dumped or burned on arrival. China closed its market in 2018, the Basel Convention tightened rules on plastic waste in 2021, and the EU banned plastic waste exports to non-OECD countries from 21 November 2026.
Case for “Trade helps recycling”: Recycling, like any industry, benefits from scale and specialization, and some importing countries have built recycling sectors that employ many people and supply manufacturers with feedstock. Clean, sorted plastic waste is a commodity; banning its trade forces each country to recycle domestically even where that is costlier, which can mean more landfill or incineration at home. Strict prior-consent rules and inspection are a better answer to abuse than prohibition.
Case for “Exports shift the burden”: Exported plastic is counted as recycled in the exporting country while the residue, often a large share, is dumped or openly burned in poorer countries with weak enforcement, harming people who had no part in creating it. Export bans force rich countries to face the real cost of their waste, which creates pressure to produce and consume less and to design for domestic recycling. The EU ban reflects that logic: the Commission presents it as part of its push for an ambitious treaty covering the full life cycle of plastics.

## Positions

Camp: Cap and cut plastic production
Adherents:
- Greenpeace (institution): calls for a treaty that will “cut plastic production by at least 75% by 2040” and argues “you wouldn’t reach for a mop, you’d turn off the tap.” https://www.greenpeace.org/usa/why-greenpeace-is-calling-on-governments-to-cut-plastic-production-by-at-least-75-by-2040/
- The 97 governments of the Nice wake-up call, led by France with Costa Rica (institutions): ministers “call for the adoption of a global target to reduce the production and consumption of primary plastic polymers” to sustainable levels, plus binding phase-outs of the most problematic products and chemicals of concern. https://www.ecologie.gouv.fr/en/rendez-vous/nice-wake-up-call-for-an-ambitious-plastics-treaty
- Pacific Small Island Developing States, through Seinimili Nakora of Fiji’s Ministry of Environment and Climate Change, PSIDS chair, in Bangkok on 30 September 2026: “The full lifecycle approach must be protected and more clearly reflect sustainable production and consumption.” https://pina.com.fj/2026/09/30/pacific-calls-for-a-strong-implementable-global-plastics-treaty-in-bangkok/
- Center for International Environmental Law (David Azoulay) and the Scientists’ Coalition for an Effective Plastics Treaty (Trisia Farrelly, Massey University), which faulted the chair’s August 2026 text for omitting upstream provisions. https://www.renewablematter.eu/en/plastics-treaty-cordano-presents-aid-to-negotiations-but-disappoints-science
- The Lancet Countdown on health and plastics (Philip Landrigan and colleagues), which states: “The principal driver of this crisis is accelerating growth in plastic production.” https://www.econ.umd.edu/sites/www.econ.umd.edu/files/pubs/Lancet%20Plastic%20Countdown%20Publication.pdf
Case: Plastic pollution is a production problem before it is a waste problem. The world makes about 460 million tonnes of plastic a year, nearly two-thirds of plastic waste comes from items with lifetimes under five years, and production is on track to grow by half by 2040. Waste systems in the countries where consumption is growing fastest cannot be built quickly enough to absorb that growth, recycling has never exceeded about a tenth of plastic waste because most plastic is not designed to be remade, and a growing share of pollution is microplastic that no collection system can catch. Plastic also harms people before it is ever waste: production emits more than 2 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent a year and exposes fenceline communities to toxic chemicals, and thousands of plastic additives are of concern. The only lever that shrinks every one of these harms at once is making less virgin plastic, starting with avoidable single-use items, while keeping essential uses. A global target is needed because plastic and its pollution cross borders, because one country’s restraint is otherwise undercut by imports, and because voluntary national plans have not bent the global production curve. This is how the world tackled ozone-depleting chemicals under the Montreal Protocol: a binding phase-down, with time and money for developing countries. Within the camp, the size of the cut varies, from Greenpeace’s 75 percent by 2040 to the 40 percent from 2025 levels by 2040 that Rwanda and Peru proposed in 2024, and the instrument varies, from a cap to a fee on primary polymers to a schedule of product phase-outs. Many in the camp, including CIEL, would now have the majority adopt a treaty by vote or among willing countries rather than accept a weak consensus text. Waste pickers’ organizations largely support production reduction while insisting on a binding just transition for informal workers.
Best evidence: Pew’s 2025 modeling projects primary plastic production rising 52 percent between 2025 and 2040 against 26 percent growth in waste management capacity, so pollution more than doubles from 130 to 280 million tonnes a year, and finds that the deepest cut in pollution (83 percent) requires reducing primary production 44 percent below business as usual (https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/reports/2025/12/breaking-the-plastic-wave-2025). The OECD’s finding that only 9 percent of plastic waste is recycled, two decades after recycling became the industry’s main answer, and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s estimate of 2.24 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent from primary production in 2019, complete the case.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that a cap would raise prices and push users toward heavier substitutes with higher emissions, while doing nothing for the uncollected waste that actually leaks. The camp answers that reduction does not mean substitution: most of the cut in its scenarios comes from reuse, refill and eliminating unnecessary packaging, which lower both plastic and emissions, while essential uses such as medical plastics are exempt. It supports better waste management too; it denies that waste management alone can work while production keeps growing. And it notes that the price argument cuts both ways: virgin plastic is cheap because its pollution, health and cleanup costs are paid by others, often the poorest communities.

Camp: Fix waste systems instead of limiting production
Adherents:
- International Council of Chemical Associations (institution), with the Global Partners for Plastics Circularity: supports a global agreement to end plastic pollution built on a circular economy for plastics, identifies lack of waste collection as the primary driver of plastic pollution, notes that 2.7 billion people lack solid waste collection, and commissioned an Oxford Economics study of the unintended consequences of a production cap. https://icca-chem.org/focus/plastics/
- American Chemistry Council (institution): its chief executive Chris Jahn said in 2024 that a US “shift in position to support plastic production caps” would put American manufacturing jobs at risk, while ACC “still believes in the need for a strong global agreement to address plastic pollution.” https://www.americanchemistry.com/chemistry-in-america/news-trends/press-release/2024/acc-statement-on-us-position-change-on-un-plastics-agreement
- The US government under the Trump administration (institution): a diplomatic memo reported by the Washington Post in August 2026 opposes binding production limits and emphasizes waste management and private investment; the State Department said “The United States seeks to negotiate an agreement that all countries can join and implement” and rejected proposals that would “damage the American economy, drive inflation, and restrict consumer choice.” https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/08/24/leaked-diplomatic-memo-shows-how-the-us-aims-to-weaken-global-limits-on-plastic-production/
- Saudi Arabia and the Like-Minded Group of mostly oil-producing states, including Kuwait, Iran and Russia (institutions): consider plastic production outside the treaty’s scope and want it focused on waste, as reported by the Associated Press at the close of the Geneva session. https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/plastic-pollution-treaty-talks-in-geneva-end-without-an-agreement
Case: The problem is plastic in the environment, not plastic in the economy. Plastic keeps food safe and fresh, makes medicine sterile, insulates buildings and lightens vehicles, and in most uses it has a smaller carbon footprint than the materials that would replace it. Pollution happens where waste is not collected: about 2.7 billion people have no solid waste collection, and the countries that leak the most plastic are not the ones that produce it. High-income countries, which use the most plastic per person, leak almost none because they collect and manage it, which proves the solution works. The treaty should therefore help every country build collection, sorting and recycling, design products to be recyclable, invest in new recycling technologies, and mobilize private and public finance where systems are missing. A global cap on production is a quota on a commodity: it would raise prices for the poorest consumers, hand market share to whoever holds the quota, override national choices about development, and do nothing for the waste that already exists. Producer states add that the 2022 mandate was to end plastic pollution, and that each country should decide its own measures through national action plans, as under the Paris Agreement. A treaty that the largest producers and consumers can sign and implement, including the United States, which needs a two-thirds Senate vote to ratify, will achieve more than an ambitious text they cannot join.
Best evidence: The 2024 Nature inventory by Cottom, Cook and Velis found that 69 percent of global macroplastic emissions come from 20 low- and middle-income countries, that uncollected waste is the dominant source in the Global South, and that high-income countries, with near-full collection and controlled disposal, contribute 0.3 percent (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07758-6). McKinsey’s 2022 analysis found plastics had lower life-cycle emissions than alternatives used at scale in 13 of 14 US applications (https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/chemicals/our-insights/climate-impact-of-plastics). China’s lower emissions in the newest data, which the Nature authors attribute to its build-out of incineration and controlled landfill, show what waste investment can do.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that waste systems cannot grow as fast as production, so a waste-only treaty chases a growing problem. The camp answers that production growth is driven by rising incomes in developing countries, the same growth that pays for waste systems, and that collection coverage has risen alongside it in China and elsewhere; the binding constraint is finance and governance, not volume. It points out that capping supply would not cut demand for food packaging, pipes or medical goods, so the shortfall would be met by substitutes or by plastic from outside the treaty. And it notes that projections of runaway production assume no gains in recycling, design or reuse, all of which its approach supports.

Camp: Set global product rules without a production cap
Adherents:
- Business Coalition for a Global Plastics Treaty (institution), convened with the Ellen MacArthur Foundation and WWF and including consumer-goods companies, retailers and financial institutions: supports “harmonised regulation across the full lifecycle of plastics,” “global restrictions and phaseouts of certain plastic products and chemicals (e.g. listed in an annex),” global design rules and producer responsibility obligations, and in its September 2026 statement on the chair’s text makes no call for a production cap. https://www.businessforplasticstreaty.org/latest/our-statement-on-the-aid-to-negotiations
- Government of Japan, Ministry of the Environment (institution): after Busan, listed its priorities as “Clarification of common criteria for plastic products and chemical substances used in plastic products,” environmentally sound product design, plastic circularity in each country, and “Obligations of each country for sound waste management (including Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) system),” and did not list a production limit. https://www.env.go.jp/en/press/press_03515.html
Case: The useful question is not how many tonnes of plastic the world makes but which plastics, made how, and what happens to them. Global rules that ban the problem products most likely to leak, such as certain single-use items, restrict the chemicals of concern, require designs that can actually be reused or recycled, and make producers pay for collection everywhere would cut pollution and toxic exposure directly, and would reduce demand for virgin plastic as a consequence. Harmonized rules matter because companies sell into nearly 200 markets: a patchwork of national standards raises costs and slows change, while one set of global criteria lets firms redesign once and invest with confidence. Producer responsibility gives waste systems a dedicated, lasting source of money, which neither aid nor municipal budgets have provided. A numerical cap on polymer production, by contrast, is the one measure that unites producer states against the treaty, would be hard to allocate among countries and to verify, and is not needed if the rules on products and design are strong. Within the camp, emphasis varies: the business coalition wants binding global lists and criteria and says harmonized rules would support sustainable levels of production and consumption, while Japan has preferred common criteria with design guidelines and implementation set country by country.
Best evidence: The Business Coalition’s modeling estimates that harmonized global rules would more than double cumulative producer responsibility revenues between 2026 and 2040, to $576 billion against $279 billion under voluntary national measures, eliminate more than twice as many problematic and avoidable plastic products as a voluntary approach, and cut mismanaged plastic waste by 23 percent (https://www.businessforplasticstreaty.org/latest/the-business-coalition-for-a-global-plastics-treaty-calls-for-the-adoption-of-harmonised-regulations-to-end-plastic-pollution-at-inc-5-2). The OECD’s finding that existing national bans are limited to items like bags and do little to reduce overall pollution supports the case for common, broader product rules.
Answer to the strongest objection: Production-cap advocates object that product rules without a volume limit will be outpaced by growth, and the waste-first camp objects that global product lists override national choice. To the first, the camp answers that bans, design rules and producer fees reduce virgin demand where it is most avoidable, which is what a cap would do, without a quota fight that has blocked the treaty for two years; a production clause can be revisited once rules are working. To the second, it answers that products are traded globally, so national lists fragment markets and help no one, and that common criteria still leave countries to choose how to implement them.

## Crux

Can the world keep using more plastic every year and still keep it out of the environment and out of people’s bodies?

Everyone in the debate wants plastic pollution to end, and almost everyone accepts better waste management as part of the answer. The disagreement under the disagreements is whether waste management can be enough while production keeps growing, or whether the amount made has to come down for pollution to come down. If waste systems, redesign and recycling can contain a growing volume, the case for a production cap collapses into a costly symbolic fight; if they cannot, a waste-only treaty is a promise that cannot be kept.

What evidence could settle: whether collection, controlled disposal and recycling capacity can be built in the fast-growing economies of South Asia, Africa and Southeast Asia faster than their plastic use grows, as China’s recent record suggests to some and Pew’s projections deny; whether recycling, including chemical recycling, can move far beyond its current single-digit share of output once producers pay for it and design for it; how much of pollution is microplastic from tires, paint and textiles that no waste system can catch; whether using less plastic in practice means reuse and less material or a switch to heavier substitutes; and whether microplastics and plastic chemicals at today’s exposure levels cause meaningful disease, which current measurement methods cannot yet say with confidence.

What is a value choice no data can close: how much precaution to buy against harms that are plausible but unproven, at the price of a cheap, useful material and the industries and jobs built on it; whether responsibility lies with those who make and sell plastic or with the places where it escapes; and whether a global body should bind what sovereign countries produce, or only coordinate what each decides to do. Even a reader who accepted every projection in this debate would still have to decide how to weigh a petrochemical worker’s job in Texas or Jubail against a fisher’s catch in the Pacific and a waste picker’s lungs in Lagos.
