# Liberal Order vs. Multipolarity — research

## Overview

The “liberal international order,” also called the “rules-based order,” is the set of institutions, alliances and norms the United States and its allies built after 1945 and extended after the Cold War: the United Nations and its Charter ban on conquest, the IMF, World Bank and the trade rules now run by the World Trade Organization, a dollar-based financial system, US alliances in Europe and Asia, and, more contestedly, an expectation that democracy, human rights and open markets would spread. “Multipolarity” names the alternative its supporters and its critics both see arriving: a world with several centers of power (the United States, China, and to lesser degrees India, Russia, the European Union and regional powers) in which no single state writes or enforces the rules. This topic covers the contest between those two pictures of world order: whether the US-led order is ending or only under strain, whether it delivered peace and prosperity or mainly served its builders, what a multipolar world would be like to live in, and what the United States, its allies, China, Russia and the countries of the Global South want instead. It deliberately leaves several neighboring debates to their own pages and touches them only where they bear on the order as a whole: when outside powers may intervene inside a sovereign state (humanitarian intervention and sovereignty), nuclear weapons policy (nuclear deterrence versus disarmament), tariffs and trade policy as such (free trade versus economic nationalism), and the erosion of democracy inside countries (democratic backsliding and populism). It also treats individual wars, in Ukraine, Gaza and Iran, and the Taiwan question as evidence in the argument rather than as topics in themselves.

Why now: opening the UN General Assembly’s annual debate on 22 September 2026, Secretary-General António Guterres told world leaders, “We are clearly moving towards a multipolar world. And that movement is irreversible,” warning that it could become either “a world of fragmentation and transactionalism” or what he called “networked multipolarity” within “a common framework of international law and shared rules” (UN, https://www.un.org/unispal/document/sg-address-unga-81-22sep26/). Four days later India’s foreign minister, S. Jaishankar, told the same hall, “We are now well on our way towards a multi-polar world. The post-1945 order is giving way to new realities” (ThePrint, https://theprint.in/world/case-for-reformed-multilateralism-getting-stronger-day-by-day-jaishankar-at-unga/3054538/). Earlier that month, on 12–13 September 2026, BRICS leaders including Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin and Narendra Modi met in New Delhi and adopted a declaration raising concern over “unilateral tariff and non-tariff measures” and sanctions not authorized by the Security Council, without naming the United States; Modi called for the Global South to move from rule-taker to “rule-shaper” (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/13/brics-summit-2026-what-are-the-key-takeaways; declaration text, Prime Minister of India, https://www.pmindia.gov.in/en/news_updates/brics-new-delhi-declaration-building-for-resilience-innovation-cooperation-and-sustainability-september-12-2026/).

Those statements capped a year in which the United States itself stepped back from the order it built. In January 2026 alone, US forces seized Venezuela’s president Nicolás Maduro in Caracas on 3 January (CNN, https://www.cnn.com/2026/01/03/americas/venezuela-explosions-intl-hnk); President Trump ordered the United States out of 66 international organizations, 31 of them UN entities, on 7 January (White House, https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/01/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-withdraws-the-united-states-from-international-organizations-that-are-contrary-to-the-interests-of-the-united-states/); and he threatened tariffs on eight NATO allies over Greenland before backing off on 21 January (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/1/21/trump-nixes-european-tariff-threats-over-greenland-after-nato-chief-talks). At Davos on 20 January, Canada’s prime minister Mark Carney said, “We are in the midst of a rupture, not a transition,” and “The old order is not coming back” (Prime Minister of Canada, https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/speeches/2026/01/20/principled-and-pragmatic-canadas-path-prime-minister-carney-addresses). A war that began on 28 February 2026, when the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran, disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz for months, with a ceasefire from 8 April and a June agreement to extend it and reopen the strait (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/6/18/world-reacts-to-us-iran-deal-to-extend-ceasefire-begin-negotations); in his September address Guterres was still calling for the fighting in the region to stop and for navigational rights to be “fully restored in accordance with international law”, and in July 2026 Pew Research Center reported that, across 36 countries it surveyed, China was now viewed more favorably than the United States in 25 (Pew, https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/07/pg_2026.06.15_china-balance-power_report.pdf).

The debate is live because almost everyone now agrees the post-Cold War arrangement is over and almost no one agrees on what should follow. Liberal internationalists in the United States and Europe, such as Robert Kagan, argue that Washington has chosen to abandon an order that kept great-power peace for eighty years and should rebuild it. The Trump administration argues that the old order became “a weapon being used against us,” in Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s words, and that the United States should put its own interests first and stop “propping up the entire world order like Atlas.” China, Russia and much of the Global South, led diplomatically by China and India, welcome a multipolar world as fairer, while differing among themselves on what it should look like. Middle powers such as Canada, Finland and the European Union are trying to keep rules-based cooperation going in coalitions without depending on Washington. And realist scholars of restraint, such as John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt, argue that multipolarity is a structural fact the United States should adjust to rather than resist. The participants are governments, the UN and other international bodies, foreign-policy think tanks across the spectrum, and international-relations scholars.

Context: Purchasing power parity (PPP) converts each economy at the prices goods and services actually cost locally, rather than at market exchange rates, so it counts what an economy can produce and buy at home, including soldiers, steel and construction. The same database shows the advanced economies as a group falling from about 58 percent of world output in 2000 to about 39 percent in 2025, and emerging and developing economies rising from about 42 to about 61 percent. PPP is the measure people who see a multipolar world already here usually cite; it overstates what money can buy abroad, such as advanced chips, aircraft or foreign debt, which is why the market-exchange-rate measure in the next stat tells a different story. Context: This is the measure people who argue the United States remains the dominant power usually cite. Note: The Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded 65 armed conflicts with a state on at least one side in 2025, the highest number since its records begin in 1946; eight were between states.
Context: UCDP, at Uppsala University, is the most widely used source of conflict data. Note: The veto was used 320 times from 1946 through 2025, 155 times by the Soviet Union or Russia and 94 times by the United States.
Context: The five permanent members of the Security Council, the victors of 1945, can each block any binding Council decision, which is why the Council is the clearest case of the 1945 power structure written into law. The United States has used most of its vetoes to block resolutions it judged harmful to Israel, including two on Gaza in 2025. Some governments count the totals differently by one or two because of vetoes cast in private meetings; this stat follows the report. Rising vetoes are cited both by those who want the Council reformed to reflect today’s powers and by those who see a return of Cold War-style deadlock.

## Questions

Question: Is the world already multipolar, or is the United States still the dominant power?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Already multipolar / Still US-led
Why contested: The answer depends on what is counted. Measured at purchasing power parity, China’s economy has been larger than America’s since the mid-2010s and the BRICS outweigh the G7; measured at market exchange rates, the US share of world output is the same as in 1990, the United States spends almost three times as much on its military as China, and the dollar sits on one side of 89 percent of currency trades. Both sides can cite official data, and the disagreement is about which kinds of power decide outcomes.
Case for already multipolar: Power is the ability to get outcomes, and the record of the past decade shows the United States repeatedly unable to get them: it could not stop Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, could not deter or quickly end a war with Iran, and watched most of the world decline to join its sanctions on Russia. China is the larger trading partner for about 70 percent of economies, out-builds the US Navy in hulls, leads in many clean-energy and manufacturing technologies, and is now viewed more favorably than the United States across most of the countries Pew surveys. India, the Gulf states, Turkey and Brazil act independently and play the great powers off against each other. Guterres, Jaishankar and Carney, who agree on little else, all describe the shift as already under way. Insisting on unipolarity is reading old numbers while the world moves on.
Case for still US-led: No other state combines America’s economy, military reach, alliances, technology, universities, energy independence and financial system. At market prices the US economy is still more than half again as large as China’s ($30.8 trillion against $19.6 trillion in 2025, IMF), and its share of world output has not fallen in 35 years; the decline of “the West” is mostly Europe and Japan. The US military spends more than the next several countries combined and has bases and treaty allies on every continent, and China has no comparable allies. The dollar’s dominance in reserves, trade invoicing and debt has barely moved, which is why US sanctions still bite worldwide. China faces a shrinking, aging workforce, a property bust and heavy debt. On this reading the world is unipolar with a strong challenger, or at most bipolar, and the main thing that has changed is America’s choice about how to use its power.

Question: Did the US-led order deliver peace and prosperity for most of the world, or mainly serve those who built it?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Delivered for most / Served its makers
Why contested: The decades since 1945 saw no war between great powers, a sharp fall in deaths from interstate war, and the fastest reduction of poverty in history. They also saw US-led wars and interventions, from Vietnam to Iraq and Libya, trade and financial rules written mostly by rich countries, and a Security Council and IMF whose voting still reflects 1945. Whether the order caused the good outcomes, and how much the bad ones were its fault, is a question about cause and counterfactual that historians and economists still argue over.
Case for delivered for most: The order’s rules against conquest, its open markets and its security guarantees made possible the rise of postwar Germany and Japan, the East Asian miracles, and above all China’s and India’s growth, which pulled more than a billion people out of extreme poverty by selling into open Western markets. The share of states that lost territory to conquest fell sharply after 1945. American alliances ended the cycle of great-power war in Europe and Asia and kept nuclear proliferation far below what was predicted in the 1960s. Its hypocrisies were real, but countries everywhere chose to join its institutions, and the rising powers now criticizing it built their prosperity inside it.
Case for served its makers: For much of the world the “rules-based order” meant rules made in Washington and applied selectively. The United States and its allies invaded Iraq without Security Council approval, bombed Libya beyond their mandate, backed coups in Iran, Guatemala and Chile, and protected Israel from Council action with dozens of vetoes. Trade and intellectual-property rules favored rich exporters, IMF programs imposed austerity on borrowers, and sanctions cut whole countries off from the financial system. The long peace among great powers coexisted with dozens of proxy wars fought in the Global South. Carney’s admission at Davos that the story was “partially false,” and that “trade rules were enforced asymmetrically,” is what much of the world had been saying for decades.

Question: Will a world of several great powers be more dangerous than one led by the United States?
Tag: empirical
Poles: More dangerous / More stable
Why contested: Political scientists have argued since the 1960s over whether a world with one, two or several great powers is most prone to war. History offers examples for each answer: multipolar Europe produced long periods of peace after 1815 and two world wars after 1914, the bipolar Cold War never turned hot between the superpowers, and the unipolar decades after 1991 saw fewer interstate wars but many US-led ones. The current rise in interstate conflict is recent, and its causes are disputed.
Case for more dangerous: When no single power can enforce the peace, every state must look after itself, alliances shift, and miscalculation becomes more likely; that is how Europe stumbled into war in 1914. Several powers with nuclear weapons, unsettled borders and competing spheres make crises more frequent and harder to manage. The data already point this way: Uppsala counted eight wars between states in 2025, the most since its records began in 1946, and a record 65 conflicts involving states, while world military spending has risen eleven years in a row. Robert Kagan argues that a return to nineteenth-century great-power politics brings a return to its wars (The Atlantic, January 2026, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/2026/03/trump-national-security-greenland-spheres-of-interest/685673/).
Case for more stable: A single dominant power faces few checks, and the unipolar period was itself violent: the United States fought wars in Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya and elsewhere, and its expansion of NATO helped provoke the Russian confrontation. A balance among several powers forces each to bargain, respect others’ core interests and avoid overreach, as the Concert of Europe did for decades after 1815. Nuclear weapons make direct war between great powers far less likely than in 1914. Many governments in Asia, Africa and Latin America find that rivalry among several powers gives them more options and better terms than dependence on one.

Question: Should the United States keep paying to underwrite its allies’ security and the world’s institutions?
Tag: value
Poles: Keep underwriting / Shift the burden
Why contested: The United States has spent heavily for eighty years on alliances, forward-deployed forces, foreign aid and international organizations. Whether that spending has been a bargain that bought American security and prosperity, or a subsidy to wealthy allies paid for by American taxpayers, depends on how one weighs diffuse long-term benefits against concrete costs, and on who one thinks the US government should serve first.
Case for keep underwriting: The order is the best deal any great power ever had. It kept wars far from American shores, gave US firms open markets, let the dollar fund America’s deficits cheaply, and turned former enemies into allies who fight alongside it. Its costs are small relative to the US economy and far smaller than the cost of the wars it prevented. Pulling back invites others to fill the space: China’s influence grew fastest where American attention lapsed. Allies who cannot rely on Washington will hedge, rearm, seek nuclear weapons or make their own deals with Beijing and Moscow, and the United States will end up less secure and poorer.
Case for shift the burden: Wealthy allies in Europe and Asia have for decades spent far less on defense than Americans while relying on US protection, and the ordinary Americans who paid for that through taxes, debt and military service saw little of the benefit. The open-market side of the bargain hollowed out American industry. The 2025 National Security Strategy states this view plainly: “The days of the United States propping up the entire world order like Atlas are over.” Allies have already responded to pressure, with NATO members pledging in 2025 to raise defense spending toward 5 percent of GDP and Europe’s military spending rising 14 percent in 2025. A United States that focuses on its own hemisphere and its own economy is stronger, not weaker.

Question: Can international rules hold without one dominant power to enforce them?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Rules can hold / Need an enforcer
Why contested: Realists hold that rules last only as long as a powerful state backs them; institutionalists hold that once built, institutions keep working because members find them useful, even after their founder weakens. The test is happening now, as the United States withdraws from some bodies and other countries decide whether to carry on without it.
Case for rules can hold: Most international rules work because they are useful to those who follow them, not because anyone enforces them by force: shipping, aviation, postal and telecommunications standards, most trade rules, and the bulk of UN work rest on reciprocity. When the United States left the Trans-Pacific Partnership in 2017, the other eleven members completed it anyway, and the European Union is now building trade ties with that group. A coalition of WTO members created a workaround appeals system after the United States blocked appointments to the Appellate Body. G. John Ikenberry argues that the liberal order is distinct from American hegemony and can outlast it (Asia Experts Forum, February 2026, https://asiaexpertsforum.org/john-ikenberry-whether-liberal-international-order-can-survive-trump-2-0/). Most of the world wants predictable rules, and middle powers can supply them together.
Case for need an enforcer: Rules without enforcement are suggestions. The Security Council is paralyzed by vetoes, the WTO’s appeals body has not had a quorum since December 2019, and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine showed that the UN Charter’s ban on conquest means little when a great power ignores it. Coalitions of middle powers cannot stop a determined great power, and without US naval power and financial reach no one guarantees the sea lanes or punishes violators. Every past international order rested on a dominant state or a concert of strong ones; when the dominant state stepped back, as Britain did after 1918, the rules decayed and war followed.

Question: Should the international order promote democracy and human rights, or rest on sovereignty and non-interference?
Tag: value
Poles: Promote liberal values / Respect sovereignty
Why contested: The order’s founding documents contain both: the UN Charter’s sovereign equality and non-intervention, and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Liberal internationalists hold that an order of free societies is more peaceful and just; China, Russia, much of the Global South and now the US administration hold that each nation should choose its own system and that promoting values has been a pretext for interference. It is a disagreement about what an international order is for.
Case for promote liberal values: Rights are universal, not Western: the people of Hong Kong, Iran, Venezuela and Belarus who protested for them were not acting on foreign instructions. Democracies rarely fight each other, honor their commitments more reliably and make better partners, so an order of democracies is a safer order. An order built only on sovereignty protects governments, not people, and gives cover to those who jail opponents, crush minorities or start wars. Abandoning values also abandons one of the strongest attractions the Western order had, and V-Dem’s finding that three quarters of humanity now lives under autocracy is a reason to defend democracy, not to give up on it.
Case for respect sovereignty: Every society has the right to choose its own path, and the record of outsiders imposing democracy, from Iraq to Libya to Afghanistan, is one of chaos and death. “Human rights” and “democracy” have been applied selectively, against adversaries and rarely against allies, which is why much of the world sees them as tools of power. A stable order among countries with different systems requires agreeing to disagree about domestic politics. China’s Global Governance Initiative and the BRICS declarations put sovereign equality first, and the US National Security Strategy now also promises good relations “without imposing on them democratic or other social change.”

Question: Does China want a bigger place in the existing order or a different order?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Larger seat / Different order
Why contested: China presents itself as the defender of the UN-centered system and multilateralism against US unilateralism, and it has joined most international institutions and now pays the second-largest share of the UN budget. It has also built parallel institutions, rejected an international tribunal’s ruling on the South China Sea, expanded its nuclear forces, and aligned with Russia. Its intentions are inferred from behavior that can be read both ways.
Case for larger seat: China’s prosperity was built inside the existing order, and it has every reason to keep it working. It is the second-largest payer to the UN, a large contributor of UN peacekeepers, and an active member of the WTO, IMF and World Bank, where it seeks larger voting shares rather than an exit. Its new institutions, such as the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, follow familiar multilateral rules and include many US allies. Its official doctrine calls for an “equal and orderly multipolar world” based on the UN Charter, and its main complaint, that institutions underrepresent the countries where most people live, is shared by India, Brazil and South Africa. Treating China as a revolutionary power risks making it one.
Case for different order: China’s actions say more than its documents. It rejected the 2016 arbitral ruling on the South China Sea, militarized artificial islands, threatens Taiwan, uses trade bans to punish countries such as Australia and Lithuania, and has backed Russia’s war economy. Its concept of “sovereign equality” puts the authority of governments above individual rights and would remove the liberal content from the order while keeping its shell. It is building alternative payment systems and standards, and its push to “reform” global governance aims to make institutions defer to state power. On this reading China wants an order in which its own power and system are secure from outside judgment, which is a different order, not a bigger seat at the same table.

Question: Should the world’s main institutions be reformed to give rising powers more weight, even if the United States and its allies lose control?
Tag: value
Poles: Share the power / Keep the anchor
Why contested: The Security Council’s permanent members and the IMF and World Bank voting shares reflect the world of 1945, not today’s distribution of people and output. Almost everyone favors reform in principle, but every specific plan takes power from someone, and those who would lose it doubt that institutions run by more diverse majorities would protect what they value.
Case for share the power: Institutions that half of humanity sees as unrepresentative cannot command loyalty. India, with more people than the G7 combined, has no permanent Security Council seat, Africa has none, and the United States alone can veto major IMF decisions. The case for reformed multilateralism, as Jaishankar put it at the UN, “only gets stronger day after day.” Sharing power is the price of keeping the institutions relevant; if they do not adapt, rising powers will build alternatives, as they already have with BRICS institutions, and the West will end up with less influence, not more.
Case for keep the anchor: The institutions work, where they work, because they are anchored by powers that pay for them and share broadly liberal commitments. Diluting that anchor without agreement on purposes risks paralysis or capture: giving more votes to authoritarian governments could turn bodies meant to protect rights and open markets into shields for repression and state-directed economics. The Security Council’s veto, for all its faults, keeps great powers inside the UN rather than outside it. Reform is better done in increments that rising powers earn by contributing, rather than by redistributing control in principle.

Question: Will the dollar remain the world’s dominant currency?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Dollar endures / Dollar erodes
Why contested: The dollar’s role is the financial core of the US-led order: it lets the United States borrow cheaply and enforce sanctions worldwide. Since 2022, Russia’s sanctions, US tariffs and US debt have led many governments to talk about alternatives, and central banks have bought gold at record pace. Yet the dollar’s share of reserves and trading has barely moved, and forecasts of its decline have been wrong for decades.
Case for dollar endures: No alternative has what the dollar has: the deepest and most liquid bond market, open capital flows, the rule of law, and a vast network effect that makes it cheaper to trade in dollars than in anything else. The euro lacks a unified bond market and political union, and the renminbi is held back by China’s capital controls; it was 1.99 percent of reserves in early 2026. The dollar was on one side of 89.2 percent of currency trades in 2025, up from 2022. As the Federal Reserve’s 2025 review notes, the reserve share has been stable since 2022 despite sanctions on Russia.
Case for dollar erodes: Dominance can erode slowly and then quickly, as sterling’s did after 1914. The reserve share has fallen from 72 percent in 2001 to about 57 percent, central banks have shifted heavily into gold, and China has built its own payment system and expanded renminbi settlement with Russia, Gulf producers and others. Using the dollar as a weapon, through sanctions and asset freezes, gives every government that fears Washington a reason to diversify. US debt, tariffs and pressure on the Federal Reserve’s independence add to the risk. The change may not mean a new single reserve currency but a fragmented system, which is itself a form of multipolarity.

## Positions

Order note: The first two camps are the poles of the debate as most of the world frames it: the Western liberal internationalists who want the US-led order rebuilt, and the governments of China, India and much of the Global South that want a multipolar order with a larger say for themselves. The third is the governing view in Washington since 2025, which rejects the liberal order from the American side without embracing the multipolar camp’s idea of fairness. The fourth is the hybrid: middle powers that want to keep rules-based cooperation but no longer expect the United States to anchor it, which in practice rejects the question as posed (it denies that the choice is between American leadership and great-power multipolarity). The fifth is the realist school of restraint, which treats multipolarity as a fact to adjust to rather than a goal or a threat; it is ordered last because its adherents are mainly scholars and think tanks rather than governments, though its arguments run through the whole debate. Camps 3 and 5 overlap on burden-shifting and skepticism of liberal crusades, and differ on the use of force and on whether the United States should seek dominance. Russia’s position, an explicit spheres-of-influence order, is noted as a variation inside camp 2 rather than as its own camp, because few governments outside Russia endorse it openly.

Camp: Renew the US-led rules-based order
Adherents: Atlantic Council, https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/programs/scowcroft-center-for-strategy-and-security/global-strategy-initiative/democratic-order-initiative/ (its Democratic Order Initiative aims at “reenergizing American leadership” and advancing “a democratic, rules-based international order”); Robert Kagan, senior fellow at the Brookings Institution, “America vs. the World,” The Atlantic, 18 January 2026, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/2026/03/trump-national-security-greenland-spheres-of-interest/685673/ (arguing that Trump has chosen to destroy the American-led order and that a return to nineteenth-century great-power politics will leave America less prosperous and the world less secure). Also: the Biden administration’s 2022 National Security Strategy; most mainstream foreign-policy figures in both US parties before 2016; the governments of Japan, South Korea, Australia and most of Europe in their public statements, which continue to call for the rules-based order and close ties to Washington.
Case: The order the United States built after 1945 is the most successful international arrangement in history. It ended the cycle of great-power war that killed tens of millions in the first half of the twentieth century, made conquest rare and costly, opened markets that lifted billions out of poverty, and gave smaller countries rules to appeal to and allies to rely on. It worked because the United States was willing to carry costs no other power would: guaranteeing the sea lanes, extending its nuclear umbrella so that Germany, Japan and South Korea did not need their own bombs, and absorbing the imports that fueled others’ growth. The order did not decay because America could no longer afford it; it is being abandoned by choice. A multipolar world is not a fairer world but a more dangerous one, the world of 1914 and 1939, in which strong states take what they can, small states suffer what they must, and authoritarian powers in Beijing and Moscow set the terms for their regions. The answer is to renew the order, not mourn it: repair alliances, return to institutions and reform them from within, compete with China through coalitions of democracies, and make the open economy work better for those it left behind.
Best evidence: The United States still accounts for about 26 percent of world output at market prices, the same as in 1990, and its alliance network has no equivalent: the 32 NATO members alone spent 55 percent of world military spending in 2025 (SIPRI). The dollar remains on one side of 89.2 percent of currency trades (BIS, 2025). Uppsala recorded eight wars between states in 2025, the most since 1946, as the order weakened. The period of US leadership coincided with the longest absence of war between great powers in modern history and the largest fall in extreme poverty on record.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the order was hypocritical, serving American interests and exempting America from its rules, from Iraq to Gaza vetoes, and that the rest of the world has no reason to want it back. The camp’s answer is that the order’s flaws are real but are arguments for fixing it, not for replacing it with something worse: no plausible alternative is less hypocritical, and the rules, however unevenly applied, gave weaker states standing that they lose in a world run by force. Countries vote with their feet; they joined American alliances and institutions far more often than they joined the alternatives, and many of the order’s critics in Asia want more US presence, not less, because of China.
Variation inside the camp: Some, such as the Biden administration, framed renewal as a contest of democracies against autocracies; others, more realist, emphasize alliances and deterrence over democracy promotion. Some, like Kagan, now write as if the order is already lost and the task is to rebuild it later; others argue it can be restored after Trump. G. John Ikenberry distinguishes the liberal order from American hegemony and argues the former can persist as the latter declines (Asia Experts Forum interview, February 2026, https://asiaexpertsforum.org/john-ikenberry-whether-liberal-international-order-can-survive-trump-2-0/), a view that shades into camp 4.

Camp: Build a multipolar order with a fairer say for rising powers
Adherents: Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, “Concept Paper on the Global Governance Initiative” (1 September 2025), https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/xw/wjbxw/202509/t20250901_11699912.html (calling for the representation of the Global South to be boosted, unilateralism “rejected,” and the UN’s role enhanced), and Foreign Minister Wang Yi, 8 March 2026, https://www.mfa.gov.cn/mfa_eng./wjbzhd/202603/t20260308_11870524.html (“multipolarity is what the international landscape should look like”; China proposes “an equal and orderly multipolar world”); S. Jaishankar, India’s Minister of External Affairs, UN General Assembly, 26 September 2026, https://theprint.in/world/case-for-reformed-multilateralism-getting-stronger-day-by-day-jaishankar-at-unga/3054538/ (“We are now well on our way towards a multi-polar world. The post-1945 order is giving way to new realities,” and the case for reformed multilateralism “only gets stronger”). Also: the BRICS New Delhi Declaration of 12 September 2026, in which members “reiterate our commitment to strengthening multilateralism and multipolarity” (https://www.pmindia.gov.in/en/news_updates/brics-new-delhi-declaration-building-for-resilience-innovation-cooperation-and-sustainability-september-12-2026/); Brazil’s President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva; South Africa; Russia, in the harder form described below.
Case: The order called “liberal” and “rules-based” was built by a handful of Western powers in 1945, when most of today’s countries were colonies, and it still bears that stamp: five permanent Security Council seats, none for Africa, Latin America or India; an IMF where one country can veto major decisions; rules written by and enforced against the weak but not the strong. The rest of the world has risen. The countries where half of humanity lives now produce more of the world’s output than the G7, and they will not keep accepting a system in which their voice does not match their weight. Multipolarity is not chaos; it is the democratization of international relations. The proper basis for order already exists in the UN Charter, sovereign equality, non-interference and international law, applied to everyone equally rather than selectively. Unilateral sanctions, tariffs and military action outside UN authority are the real threats to order. A world of several centers, each respecting the others’ choices of political system and development path, is fairer, and because no one power can impose its will, it is also more likely to rest on genuine consensus.
Best evidence: The ten BRICS members produced about 39 percent of world output at purchasing power parity in 2025, against 28 percent for the G7, and are home to about 48 percent of the world’s people (IMF; World Bank). About 70 percent of economies trade more with China than with the United States (Lowy Institute). In 2026, Pew found China viewed more favorably than the United States in 25 of 36 countries surveyed, especially in middle-income countries. The 2026 US withdrawals from 66 international organizations, its tariffs and its military actions in Venezuela and Iran without Security Council authorization are cited as proof that the old order’s guardian no longer follows its own rules.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that “multipolarity” is a slogan that lets authoritarian powers escape criticism of how they treat their own people and their neighbors, and that Russia’s invasion of Ukraine shows what a multipolar order looks like in practice. The camp’s answer, as China and India put it, is that the principles they invoke, the UN Charter’s ban on the use of force and respect for sovereignty, condemn aggression by anyone, and that Western critics are selective, having invaded Iraq and protected Israel’s conduct in Gaza while condemning others. India adds that its own pursuit of multipolarity includes partnerships with the United States, Europe and Japan and does not mean alignment with China or Russia; BRICS declarations are adopted by consensus among democracies and autocracies alike.
Variation inside the camp: The camp shares a goal but not a vision. China presents itself as the defender of the UN-centered system and of “true multilateralism” against US unilateralism, and proposes its own global initiatives on development, security, civilization and governance. India seeks “reformed multilateralism,” including a permanent Security Council seat, and practices “multi-alignment,” partnering with the United States in the Quad while sitting in BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation; it explicitly does not want a China-led Asia, and Jaishankar has argued that a multipolar world requires “a multipolar Asia.” Brazil and South Africa emphasize development finance, climate and representation. Russia takes the hardest line: President Putin told the Valdai Club in October 2025 that “the multipolarity that has in fact already emerged is shaping the framework within which the states act” (Valdai Club transcript, https://valdaiclub.com/events/posts/articles/vladimir-putin-meets-with-members-of-the-valdai-club-transcript-plenary-session/), and Russia’s war in Ukraine rests on a claim to a sphere of influence over its neighbors that India, Brazil and many other camp members do not endorse.

Camp: Put US interests first and bargain from strength
Adherents: The White House, National Security Strategy of the United States (published December 2025), https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/2025-National-Security-Strategy.pdf (“The days of the United States propping up the entire world order like Atlas are over”; “the world’s fundamental political unit is and will remain the nation-state”; a “Trump Corollary” to the Monroe Doctrine to “restore American preeminence in the Western Hemisphere”); Marco Rubio, Secretary of State, opening statement to the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, 15 January 2025, https://www.state.gov/opening-remarks-by-secretary-of-state-designate-marco-rubio-before-the-senate-foreign-relations-committee (“The post-war global order is not just obsolete, it is now a weapon being used against us”). Also: the Heritage Foundation, which urges the United States to “quit U.S. membership in United Nations agencies, international organizations, and treaties that are redundant, wasteful, or contrary to U.S. values and interests” (https://static.heritage.org/solutions/2026/38_solutions_2026-international-organizations-and-treaties.pdf); Vice President JD Vance; national-conservative writers.
Case: After the Cold War, American elites convinced themselves that permanent American domination of the whole world was in the national interest, and the American people paid for it. They paid in wars that did not concern them, in a trade system that let China and others hollow out American industry, in subsidizing the defense of rich allies who spent little on their own, and in membership of international bodies that often oppose American interests and try to override American sovereignty. Other nations never stopped acting in their own interest; they used the order to get ahead at America’s expense. The answer is neither isolation nor a new crusade but a foreign policy that puts American interests first, as every other nation does for itself. That means using America’s unmatched economic and military power as leverage, through tariffs, deals and, when necessary, force, to secure better terms; making allies carry their own weight; leaving institutions that do not serve the United States; securing the Western Hemisphere first; and preventing any rival, above all China, from dominating regions vital to the United States. Strength, not rules written for a vanished world, keeps the peace.
Best evidence: Allies have responded to pressure: NATO members committed at The Hague in 2025 to raise defense spending toward 5 percent of GDP, and European military spending rose 14 percent in 2025 (SIPRI). The Lowy Institute’s data show that China gained its trade position under the old open-trading rules, which supporters cite as proof the old bargain favored rivals. The United States still has the world’s largest economy at market prices and by far its largest military, which supporters argue gives it bargaining power it had been failing to use. Supporters credit US pressure and force with removing Maduro in Venezuela, bringing Russia and Ukraine to talks and setting back Iran’s nuclear program, though each of those outcomes is disputed.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that this approach squanders the alliances, trust and institutions that multiplied American power, driving allies to hedge and handing influence to China, as Pew’s polling showing China now more favorably viewed in most countries suggests. The camp’s answer is that popularity is not power and that allies stay because they need American protection and markets more than the United States needs them; that the old order’s popularity abroad was purchased with American money and jobs; and that respect comes from strength and reliability to one’s own people, not from deference to international bodies. It argues that the institutions the United States left had become hostile or useless, and that those it stays in, NATO above all, are stronger because allies are finally paying their share.
Variation inside the camp: Some in the administration and its allied think tanks are “prioritizers” who want to shift resources from Europe and the Middle East to deterring China in Asia; others are “restrainers” closer to camp 5 who want fewer foreign commitments overall; and others are hawks comfortable with using force, as in Venezuela and Iran. The camp agrees on sovereignty, burden-shifting and tariffs, and disagrees on how much to confront China and how far to pull back from Europe.

Camp: Keep the rules alive through coalitions of middle powers
Adherents: Mark Carney, Prime Minister of Canada, address to the World Economic Forum, 20 January 2026, https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/speeches/2026/01/20/principled-and-pragmatic-canadas-path-prime-minister-carney-addresses (“We are in the midst of a rupture, not a transition”; “The old order is not coming back”; middle powers must act together because “if you are not at the table, you are on the menu,” building “the coalitions that work, issue by issue”); European Policy Centre, “Like-minded traders: What a Euro-Pacific trade alliance could deliver” (16 December 2025), https://www.epc.eu/publication/like-minded-traders-what-a-euro-pacific-trade-alliance-could-deliver/ (arguing that like-minded nations in Europe and the Pacific “must join forces” to keep trade rules-based). Also: Alexander Stubb, President of Finland, and his “values-based realism” (“The West’s Last Chance,” Foreign Affairs, https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/wests-last-chance); the Jacques Delors Institute’s March 2026 report “After the Rupture,” https://institutdelors.eu/content/uploads/2026/03/R131-After_the_Rupture-EU-CPTPP-Fabry_Gonzelez-Laya_Kohler-Suzuki_EN.pdf; the European Commission’s trade diplomacy with the CPTPP countries.
Case: The old order is gone, and pretending otherwise is a mistake; but so is accepting a world in which great powers do as they please. The rules-based order was always partly a useful fiction, in which the strongest exempted themselves when convenient, but the fiction worked for countries like Canada, Finland, Japan or Chile because it gave them rules to rely on. Now the great powers, including the United States, use tariffs, finance and supply chains as weapons. Middle powers cannot restore American leadership and should not wait for it, but together they are large: the European Union and the twelve members of the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) together account for a large share of world trade, and with partners in Asia, Africa and Latin America they can keep open, rules-based trade, climate cooperation and collective security going without a single hegemon. That means coalitions issue by issue, diversified trade, collective investment in defense and resilience, and honesty about values: universal rights and international law, applied with realism about other countries’ histories and with genuine respect for the Global South rather than lectures. The aim is a multilateral order among many players, not a multipolar order among a few great powers.
Best evidence: When the United States left the Trans-Pacific Partnership in 2017, the remaining eleven members completed it anyway, and the European Union and CPTPP members agreed in March 2026 at the WTO ministerial in Yaoundé to deepen cooperation on rules-based trade (European Commission, https://policy.trade.ec.europa.eu/news/joint-ministerial-statement-eu-and-cptpp-14th-wto-ministerial-conference-2026-03-27_en). A group of WTO members built an interim appeals arrangement after the United States blocked the Appellate Body. European military spending rose 14 percent in 2025 (SIPRI). The reaction to the January 2026 Greenland tariff threat, in which European governments responded together and Trump withdrew the threat within days, is cited by the camp as evidence that coordinated middle powers have leverage.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that middle powers lack the military power and unity to enforce anything: they could not stop Russia in Ukraine without American weapons, and Europe still depends on US intelligence, nuclear deterrence and the dollar. The camp’s answer is that this is a reason to build capacity, not to give up, and that much of what the order provides, trade rules, standards, climate and health cooperation, does not require military enforcement but only enough members to make the rules worth following. It does not reject the United States: it keeps the door open for Washington to return and still values NATO, but it refuses to make everything depend on one capital’s politics.
Variation inside the camp: Some members, especially in Europe, emphasize “strategic autonomy” and rearmament; others, like Canada, emphasize trade diversification and issue-by-issue coalitions. Some, like Stubb, stress a triangular bargain among the “Global West,” “Global East” and “Global South,” giving the Global South a decisive role; others focus narrowly on keeping Western rules alive among like-minded states. Ikenberry’s view that the liberal order can outlast American hegemony gives the camp a scholarly foundation.

Camp: Scale back US commitments and accept a balance of power
Adherents: Quincy Institute for Responsible Statecraft, https://quincyinst.org/about/ (“a foreign policy that emphasizes military restraint and diplomatic engagement and cooperation with other nations will serve American interests and values better than policies that prioritize the maintenance of U.S. global dominance through force,” suited to a century “where economic power is more evenly shared across nations”); Stephen Walt, Harvard University, “America Is Too Scared of the Multipolar World” (Foreign Policy, March 2023, reposted by Quincy), https://quincyinst.org/2023/03/07/america-is-too-scared-of-the-multipolar-world/ (arguing that “restoring unipolarity is probably impossible” and that US leaders should stop resisting a world of several powers). Also: John Mearsheimer, University of Chicago, with Walt, “The Case for Offshore Balancing,” Foreign Affairs, 2016, https://www.foreignaffairs.com/articles/united-states/2016-06-13/case-offshore-balancing; Defense Priorities; parts of the Trump coalition.
Case: The unipolar moment was an accident of the Soviet collapse, and Washington misused it, expanding NATO to Russia’s borders, invading Iraq, toppling Libya’s government and trying to remake societies it did not understand. A world of several great powers is the normal condition of international politics, and the United States, protected by two oceans, nuclear weapons and the strongest economy, is safer in it than almost any other country. The sensible strategy is to stop trying to police the world, let wealthy allies in Europe defend themselves, avoid wars of choice, negotiate with rivals over their core interests, and concentrate American power where it truly matters: preventing any single power from dominating a key region, which today means balancing China in Asia, mainly by helping local states balance it. This is neither isolationism nor America First militarism: it means more diplomacy and less force, and rejecting both the liberal crusade and the use of tariffs and raids as tools of dominance.
Best evidence: The wars of the unipolar era, in Afghanistan, Iraq and Libya, cost trillions of dollars and hundreds of thousands of lives without producing stable democracies. Europe, whose combined economy dwarfs Russia’s, raised military spending 14 percent in 2025 once US pressure mounted (SIPRI), suggesting it can carry its own defense. The 2025 National Security Strategy itself concedes that “permanent American domination of the entire world” was an elite mistake, which Quincy analysts welcomed while criticizing its militarized revival of the Monroe Doctrine (Quincy, https://quincyinst.org/research/the-2025-nss-and-restraint-experts-react/).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that restraint invites aggression: Russia invaded Ukraine and China presses on Taiwan precisely as American resolve was doubted, and spheres of influence mean abandoning small nations to big neighbors. Restrainers answer that Russia’s invasion followed NATO expansion that realists had warned against for decades, so the lesson runs the other way; that restraint does not mean abandoning the balance of power, only letting those most threatened carry most of the burden; and that many restrainers, such as Quincy’s Sarang Shidore, explicitly reject spheres of influence as “neither workable nor desirable” and favor negotiated settlements instead.
Variation inside the camp: Offensive realists such as Mearsheimer favor pulling out of Europe while containing China hard in Asia; others at Quincy favor détente with China as well. Some see multipolarity as already here; Walt has suggested the world may instead become bipolar or a “lopsided” multipolarity with the United States first among unequal powers. Restrainers divide over the Trump administration: some welcome its skepticism of alliances and liberal crusades, others oppose its tariffs, its Venezuela operation and its war with Iran as the opposite of restraint.

## Crux

The question under the others: without a single dominant power to underwrite them, will the world’s common rules hold, and is an order with several centers of power worth its risks for the greater say it gives the rest of the world?

What evidence could settle: Much of the disagreement turns on things that can be measured and watched over the next decade. Whether the rise in wars between states since 2023 continues as US commitments shrink, or levels off as new balances form. Whether institutions keep working when the United States withdraws, as the trade pact it left in 2017 did, or wither, as the WTO’s appeals body has. Whether coalitions of middle powers actually deliver trade agreements, defense capacity and crisis responses without Washington. Whether China, as its weight grows, keeps to the rules it endorses, such as the UN Charter’s ban on force and peaceful settlement of disputes, or uses its power to change borders and punish critics. Whether the dollar’s share of reserves and trading erodes when it is used as a weapon. And how much of the old order’s record, the long peace among great powers and the fall in poverty, it actually caused, which historians and economists can study with better evidence even if they cannot run the counterfactual. Progress on these would move people: a decade of stable rules without a hegemon would strengthen the multipolar and middle-power camps, while a cascade of wars and collapsing institutions would strengthen the case for renewing American leadership or for a hard-headed balance of power.

What no data can close: Who should have a say in writing the rules, and what the rules should protect. Someone who believes the order must protect individual rights and democracy will judge a fairer distribution of power among governments as no gain if those governments jail their critics; someone who believes sovereign equality and non-interference come first will see the liberal order’s values as a cover for domination however well it performed. A related choice is how to weigh the interests of one’s own country against those of everyone else: whether an American government should pay to sustain an order that benefits others as well as Americans, and whether others should accept rules they did not write in exchange for stability. And there is the question of how much risk of great-power conflict is acceptable in exchange for a more equal world, or how much inequality of power is acceptable in exchange for a safer one. Evidence can show what each arrangement tends to produce; it cannot say whose voice, whose rights and whose security should count most.
