# Net-Zero Pace — research

Slug: net-zero-pace
Scope: How fast rich economies should decarbonize. Pace and cost, not whether growth must stop.
Researched: 2026-11-23

## Overview

Almost every rich economy has a dated promise to reach net-zero greenhouse gas emissions, most of them by 2050, and a set of interim targets on the way: the EU’s legally binding cut of 90 percent below 1990 by 2040, the UK’s seventh carbon budget of roughly 87 percent below 1990 for 2038–42, Japan’s, Canada’s and Australia’s 2035 pledges. This topic is about the speed of that path in the high-income countries: whether the dates and interim targets are too slow, about right, or too fast; what each pace costs households, budgets and industry; who should bear the cost; and what is gained or lost by going faster or slower than the rest of the world. It deliberately excludes the question of whether economic growth itself must stop or can be decoupled from emissions, which belongs to degrowth versus green growth; it leaves the design of carbon prices to carbon pricing, the supply-side question of restricting extraction to fossil fuel phase-out, and the role of removals to carbon dioxide removal, touching each only where it changes the pace argument.

The why-now event: on 22 November 2026, the UN climate conference COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, closed nearly 50 hours late without adopting its cover decision, the proposed “Antalya pact.” At least 32 countries rejected the text in the closing plenary; the EU said it would not endorse a pact that “does not even mention fossil fuels,” Saudi Arabia and Russia blocked the Mitigation Work Programme, and the United States sent no official delegation for the second year running (Carbon Brief, “COP31: Key outcomes agreed at the UN climate talks in Antalya,” November 2026). The collapse came two weeks after two annual stocktakes framed the pace question in numbers. The Global Carbon Budget 2026 (University of Exeter release, 9 November 2026) projected fossil CO2 emissions to dip 0.4 percent in 2026, to 38.2 billion tonnes, largely because the Iran war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz from March 2026 cut oil use, and put the remaining budget for a 50 percent chance of 1.5 °C at 150 billion tonnes, under four years of current emissions. UNEP’s Emissions Gap Report 2026 projected 2.8 °C of warming by 2100 under current policies and 2.6 °C if every national pledge is met, concluded that holding 1.5 °C without overshoot is no longer possible, and estimated that each five years of delay in starting cuts locks in about another 0.1 °C (as reported by Carbon Brief, “UNEP: Opportunity has ‘been lost’ to meet 1.5C warming limit without ‘overshoot’,” October 2026).

The debate is live because rich-country politics are pulling in opposite directions at once. The EU finalized its 2040 target in March 2026 but with flexibilities (up to 5 points of international credits from 2036, a one-year delay of its second emissions trading system to 2028) that its own scientific advisory board warned could weaken domestic cuts, and in November 2026 the European Parliament’s environment committee voted to soften the 2035 car CO2 standard. The UK Parliament legislated the seventh carbon budget in June 2026, while the opposition Conservatives and Reform UK both pledge to scrap the net-zero legal framework, and Australia’s Liberal and National parties dropped net zero by 2050 in November 2025. In the United States, the Trump administration has left the Paris Agreement and the UN climate treaty and rescinded the 2009 endangerment finding in February 2026, now under challenge in the D.C. Circuit; Democrats won both chambers of Congress on 3 November 2026. The Hormuz energy shock gave both sides a new argument: EU officials such as Teresa Ribera called for “faster electrification, renewables and efficiency,” while others pressed for more domestic oil and gas. The people in this argument are governments and their statutory advisers (the UK Climate Change Committee, the European Scientific Advisory Board on Climate Change), center-right parties split between easing and repealing targets, climate campaigners and fair-share researchers who want rich countries at net zero well before 2050, innovation-first voices such as Bill Gates and the Breakthrough Institute, economists arguing over damages and discount rates, industry, unions, and the households paying the energy bills.

### 2. Remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C
- Label: Remaining carbon budget, 1.5 °C (50 percent chance)
- Value: 150
- Unit: billion tonnes CO2 (GtCO2), from the start of 2027
- Comparison rows (same source, same date, budgets for a 50 percent chance): 1.5 °C: 150 GtCO2; 1.7 °C: 510 GtCO2; 2 °C: 1,035 GtCO2. Global CO2 emissions including land use in 2026: 43.3 GtCO2.
- Data year: 2026 assessment
- Source label: Global Carbon Budget 2026, Global Carbon Project; University of Exeter release, 9 November 2026 (1.7 °C and 2 °C figures from Carbon Brief’s analysis of the same release)
- Source URL: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1150611
- Note: At current rates the world will have emitted the remaining budget for a 50 percent chance of 1.5 °C in under four years.
- Context: The budget is the cumulative CO2 that can still be emitted for a stated chance of holding a stated temperature. It shrinks each year by that year’s emissions and is revised as the science improves: the Global Carbon Project put it at 170 GtCO2 from January 2026 a year earlier. Carbon Brief’s analysis of the 2026 release (https://www.carbonbrief.org/analysis-fossil-fuel-co2-emissions-set-to-dip-in-2026-as-iran-conflict-hits-oil-use) gives the 1.7 °C and 2 °C budgets as 510 and 1,035 GtCO2. For the pace debate the point is arithmetic: a 1.5 °C budget this small cannot be met by any realistic path, which is why UNEP and most governments now speak of overshoot and return, and why the argument has shifted to how much warming above 1.5 °C each extra year of emissions buys.

### 9. Net cost of the UK’s legislated path
- Label: Net cost of UK net zero, CCC pathway
- Value: about 0.2
- Unit: percent of UK GDP per year, average to 2050
- Data year: 2025 (Seventh Carbon Budget advice, published 26 February 2025)
- Source label: Climate Change Committee, The Seventh Carbon Budget
- Source URL: https://www.theccc.org.uk/publication/the-seventh-carbon-budget/
- Note: The UK’s statutory adviser estimates the net cost of its net-zero pathway at around 0.2 percent of GDP a year on average.
- Context: The figure is net: upfront investment in heat pumps, grids, electric vehicles and industry, minus the fuel and running-cost savings that follow. The CCC expects the investment to produce net savings during the 2038–42 budget period itself, and says household savings on driving roughly match any extra home-energy costs over 2025–2050, provided policy costs are moved off electricity bills. The figure is contested from both sides: the Conservative Party and the think tank Onward argue the system costs of a fully clean grid are higher than the CCC assumes, while climate campaigners argue the CCC understates the cost of climate damage avoided. The government’s own impact assessment for the budget, legislated in June 2026, uses a different basis: it puts net investment costs at about 1.2 percent of GDP a year (range 0.8 to 1.6), because it assumes less behavior change and slower technology cost falls than the CCC, and it puts the net present value of meeting the budget at £865 billion over 2025–2050 against a “no net-zero” scenario, most of the benefit being avoided climate damage (Carbon Brief, “Q&A: How UK’s seventh carbon budget will deliver ‘£865bn’ in economic benefits,” 3 June 2026, https://www.carbonbrief.org/qa-how-uks-seventh-carbon-budget-will-deliver-865bn-in-economic-benefits). The spread between 0.2 and 1.2 percent of GDP for the same target is itself part of the argument.

- Label: Social cost of carbon
- Value: $185
- Unit: US dollars per tonne CO2 (2020 dollars, 2 percent discount rate)
- Comparison rows (rows differ in method, price year and discount rate; each is the study’s headline estimate):
  - Rennert et al., Nature 2022: $185 (range $44–$413), 2020 dollars
  - Barrage & Nordhaus, DICE-2023, PNAS 2024, baseline: $66 for 2020, 2019 dollars
  - Bilal & Känzig, NBER working paper 32450 (revised): more than $1,200
  - US federal interim value in use in 2022: $51 (as stated on the RFF page)
- Data year: studies published 2022–2025
- Source label: Resources for the Future summary of Rennert et al. (2022)
- Source URL: https://www.rff.org/publications/journal-articles/comprehensive-evidence-implies-a-higher-social-cost-of-co2/
- Note: Peer-reviewed estimates of the damage done by one more tonne of CO2 range from about $66 to more than $1,200, depending mostly on damage functions and discount rates.
- Context: The social cost of carbon is the present value of future damage caused by emitting one extra tonne today, and it is the main bridge from climate science to the pace question: a high value justifies fast cuts even at high cost per tonne, a low one argues for a gradual path. Rennert and colleagues’ $185 is 3.6 times the old $51 federal figure. Barrage and Nordhaus (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10990119) get a much lower value with the DICE model. A widely cited 2024 Nature paper by Kotz, Levermann and Wenz projecting a 19 percent income loss by 2049 was retracted in December 2025 after data errors were found; its authors say a corrected analysis gives about 17 percent and will be resubmitted (Retraction Watch, 3 December 2025, https://retractionwatch.com/2025/12/03/authors-retract-nature-paper-projecting-high-costs-of-climate-change). The US administration withdrew federal use of the social cost of carbon in 2025 [unverified].

- Comparison rows (same source, same model, present-value wealth gain over the baseline path, 2019 international dollars): cost-benefit optimal path: about $120 trillion; 2 °C-limited path: about $107 trillion; extended Paris Accord path: about $85 trillion
- Data year: 2024
- Source label: Barrage & Nordhaus, “Policies, projections, and the social cost of carbon: Results from the DICE-2023 model,” PNAS, March 2024
- Source URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10990119
- Note: The DICE-2023 model’s economically optimal path warms the world 2.6 °C by 2100, and holding to 2 °C costs it about $12 trillion of the gains.

## Questions

### 1. Are the damages a faster pace avoids larger than the costs it adds?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Damages outweigh costs / Costs rival damages
- Why contested: Every pace argument eventually rests on a comparison between what an extra tonne of emissions will do and what it costs to avoid it now, and both sides of that comparison are uncertain by an order of magnitude. Estimates of the social cost of carbon run from about $66 a tonne in the DICE-2023 model to more than $1,200 in Bilal and Känzig’s work on global temperature shocks, and a headline Nature estimate of climate damages was retracted in December 2025. On the cost side, the UK’s own adviser and its own government differ by a factor of six on the net cost of the same target (0.2 versus 1.2 percent of GDP a year).
- Best case for “Damages outweigh costs”: The newer empirical work, which ties damages to global rather than local temperature and captures the extremes that do the most harm, finds losses far larger than the old models assumed: over 20 percent of world GDP for each degree in Bilal and Känzig, $185 a tonne in the most comprehensive peer-reviewed estimate by Rennert and colleagues. Even the UK fiscal watchdog finds climate damage, not mitigation, is the bulk of the long-run fiscal risk: around three-fifths of the extra debt in its 3 °C scenario against a tenth for net zero. Meanwhile mitigation keeps getting cheaper, so every recent revision has moved the cost-benefit answer toward more and faster action; Barrage and Nordhaus’s own 2023 update lowered the optimal temperature and the cost of 2 °C. Tail risks, from tipping points to food-system shocks, are poorly priced in every model, and they argue for buying insurance early.
- Best case for “Costs rival damages”: The most transparent integrated model still puts the economically optimal path at about 2.6 °C by 2100, not 1.5, and the gain from squeezing to 2 °C is small next to the cost. The headline-grabbing damage estimates have proved fragile: one was retracted, others rest on a few decades of noisy data extrapolated across centuries. Mitigation costs in rich economies are not only the technology costs in the models but also rising network charges, industrial closures and the political cost of higher bills, which show up now and with certainty, while damages fall later and depend mostly on what China, India and other large emitters do. On this view, spending heavily to go faster than the cost-benefit path is paying a premium for a small and uncertain benefit.

### 2. Do rich countries owe a faster pace because of their historical emissions?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Historical debt / Shared timetable
- Why contested: High-income countries emitted about 62 percent of all fossil CO2 since 1750 but about 36 percent of it in 2025, and China’s cumulative total now roughly equals the EU’s. Whether the past creates an obligation to go faster now, and how large, is a moral question that the Paris Agreement left deliberately vague under the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.
- Best case for “Historical debt”: Warming depends on cumulative emissions, so the atmospheric space rich countries used is space nobody else can use. Equal-per-capita and capability-based allocations of the remaining budget leave wealthy nations with little or none, which is why fair-share analyses by Anderson and colleagues, and campaigners such as CAN Europe, conclude rich countries should reach net zero around 2035–2040 and finance cuts elsewhere. Rich countries also have the capital, institutions and technology to move fastest at the lowest relative cost, and their early deployment is what made solar, wind and batteries cheap for everyone else. Demanding the same timetable from a country with a tenth of the income per person is not equal treatment.
- Best case for “Shared timetable”: Most historical emissions were made before anyone knew of the harm, by people now dead, in the course of building the technologies and medicines the whole world uses. Responsibility in the present should follow present and future emissions, which are now dominated by middle-income economies; China emits more per person than the EU. Asking today’s rich-country households, many of them on low incomes, to pay a premium for their great-grandparents’ coal is hard to defend and harder to sustain politically. The climate does not care where a tonne is cut, so the efficient and fair course is to cut where it is cheapest and help others do the same, not to race a domestic deadline.

### 3. Does faster rich-country action pull global emissions down, or does it leak elsewhere?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Leadership spreads / Leakage offsets
- Why contested: Rich economies are about a third of current emissions, so their pace matters to the climate mainly through what it does to everyone else’s. The evidence cuts both ways: clean technology costs collapsed after early deployment in Germany, China and the US, but heavy industry has also moved to places with cheaper energy and laxer rules, and consumption-based accounts show some rich-country cuts were offshored.
- Best case for “Leadership spreads”: Deployment drives learning curves, and learning curves are global; the collapse in solar costs that now makes it the cheapest new power in most of the world was bought partly by early rich-country subsidies. Credible rich-country targets anchor investors, set standards that exporters must meet (the EU’s carbon border adjustment is now pulling trading partners into carbon pricing), and give negotiators the standing to ask others to move. Forty countries with 26 percent of global emissions cut emissions while growing over 2016–25, up from 22 in the decade before, which suggests the example is being followed.
- Best case for “Leakage offsets”: When energy-intensive industry in Europe closes, the steel, chemicals and fertilizer are made elsewhere, often with more emissions, and the climate gains nothing while workers lose jobs. Consumption-based figures show UK and EU footprints far above their territorial numbers. The clean-tech learning curves are now driven by China’s scale, not by Western deadlines, so a faster domestic pace no longer buys much additional cost decline. And if leadership worked, COP31 would not have collapsed over a text that did not even mention fossil fuels; on this view, the main effect of going it alone is to transfer industry and supply chains to competitors.

### 4. Does a faster transition make rich economies more or less exposed to energy-price shocks?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Faster is safer / Faster is costlier
- Why contested: Two price shocks in four years, from Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and the Iran war and Hormuz closure in 2026, have made energy security the frame in which most voters now meet the pace argument, and each side claims the shock proves its case.
- Best case for “Faster is safer”: Fossil fuels are priced on world markets that a single strait can close; domestic wind, solar, nuclear and heat pumps are not. The CCC found that after the 2026 shock, bills for a gas-boiler and petrol-car household rose almost four times as much as for a heat-pump and electric-car household, and the IEA estimated that clean energy saved the five largest fuel-importing regions about $260 billion in import costs in 2025. Every year of delay is another year of exposure to the next shock, and the fiscal cost of absorbing one shock rivals years of transition investment.
- Best case for “Faster is costlier”: A grid that relies heavily on weather-dependent power needs backup, storage and transmission that levelized-cost figures leave out, and those costs show up in network charges and policy levies on electricity bills, which is why UK and German power prices are among the highest in the industrial world. Closing domestic oil and gas production while still consuming imported fuel worsens security. Clean technology supply chains are themselves concentrated, mostly in China, so a rushed transition swaps one dependency for another. The secure path is a gradual one that keeps firm domestic capacity, gas and nuclear, while electrification proceeds at the speed equipment turnover allows.

### 5. Should future harms be discounted heavily or lightly against today’s costs?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Low discount rate / Market discount rate
- Why contested: The pace that a cost-benefit analysis recommends depends heavily on the discount rate, because the costs of cutting come now and most of the benefits come decades later. The discount rate is one of the main reasons published estimates of the social cost of carbon differ, and the choice is partly about ethics (how much later generations count) and partly about economics (what returns capital earns).
- Best case for “Low discount rate”: There is no ethical reason to value a child born in 2070 less than one born today simply because of the date. The pure rate of time preference should be near zero, as Frank Ramsey and Nicholas Stern argued, leaving only an allowance for future people being richer. Climate damage is not a small marginal loss that growth will cover; it may be large, irreversible and concentrated on poorer people, which argues for a declining or very low rate. The US government’s 2023 update and Rennert and colleagues’ estimate both moved to about 2 percent for these reasons.
- Best case for “Market discount rate”: Money spent on mitigation today is money not invested in schools, health and infrastructure that would make future generations richer and more resilient. The discount rate should reflect the real returns those alternatives earn, as Nordhaus has long argued; setting it near zero makes almost any present sacrifice look worthwhile and is a value choice dressed as arithmetic. Future people will very likely be much richer and technologically better equipped, so asking today’s poorer households to pay heavily on their behalf is a transfer from poorer to richer.

### 6. Do binding legal deadlines hold the pace, or do they provoke the backlash that stops it?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: Deadlines anchor investment / Deadlines breed backlash
- Why contested: The UK’s Climate Change Act and the EU Climate Law were designed to take pace out of the electoral cycle. Since 2023 the same deadlines have become campaign targets, with the UK Conservatives and Reform UK pledging repeal, Australia’s Coalition dropping net zero, and the EU loosening its car and carbon-market rules.
- Best case for “Deadlines anchor investment”: Investors in grids, factories and power plants work on 20- to 40-year horizons and need to know the rules will not change. Under the UK’s legally binding budgets, emissions fell to 54 percent below 1990 while the economy grew, and the framework has survived every change of government since 2008. Business groups and the CCC repeatedly say policy reversals, not targets, raise the cost of capital. Backlash follows visible costs and weak delivery, not the deadline itself; fixing delivery, such as moving policy costs off electricity bills, keeps public support for the destination, which in the UK remains strong over the long term.
- Best case for “Deadlines breed backlash”: A fixed date set in law turns every missed milestone into a crisis and every cost into a mandate, which gives populists an easy target: Sunak in 2023 warned that the path risked losing public consent, and that warning has since been borne out by the rise of parties promising repeal. Where targets are loosened in practice anyway (car standards, ETS2, credits in the 2040 target), the law loses credibility while still carrying the political cost. Durable progress, on this view, comes from making clean options cheaper and more attractive so they need no deadline, as Breakthrough’s Ted Nordhaus argues: in the United States the long-run trend in decarbonization has changed little across administrations of either party.

### 7. Now that 1.5 °C will be overshot, should it still set the rich-country pace?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Keep 1.5 compass / Reset the target
- Why contested: UNEP and the Global Carbon Project now treat overshoot as inevitable; human-induced warming was 1.37 °C in 2025 and rising 0.27 °C a decade. Whether that makes the pace more urgent or makes the old timetable obsolete is a judgment about what targets are for.
- Best case for “Keep 1.5 compass”: Every tenth of a degree matters to damages, and the lowest-overshoot path still holds peak warming to 1.7–1.8 °C and returns it toward 1.5 °C by 2100; abandoning the goal would remove the main argument for that path. The Paris temperature goal is a legal commitment, reaffirmed by the International Court of Justice’s 2025 advisory opinion as the standard against which state conduct is judged. Overshoot makes speed more, not less, important, because UNEP estimates each five years of delay locks in about another 0.1 °C and every year of overshoot must later be paid back with costly removals.
- Best case for “Reset the target”: A target that cannot be met stops guiding behavior and starts to look like a reason not to trust any target. The 1.5 °C timetables were built on budgets that are now gone, so pacing rich-country policy to them yields dates (net zero by 2035 or 2040) that are impossible without economic disruption voters will not accept. A realistic goal, such as holding warming well below 2 °C with an emphasis on adaptation and resilience, would let governments set credible paths, as Bill Gates argued in 2025 when he urged that human welfare rather than temperature be the measure of progress.

### 8. Should rich countries count international credits and removals toward their targets?
- Tag: value
- Poles: Domestic cuts only / Cut where cheapest
- Why contested: The EU’s 2040 target lets up to 5 of its 90 percentage points come from international credits from 2036, and both the EU and UK expect permanent removals to offset residual emissions. The European Scientific Advisory Board on Climate Change warned that the credit flexibility risks weakening domestic cuts; supporters say it lowers the cost of the same climate outcome.
- Best case for “Domestic cuts only”: Rich countries are the ones with the money to transform their own economies, and every tonne they buy abroad is a tonne of domestic transformation postponed; the history of carbon offsets is full of credits that did not represent real cuts. The world needs every country to reach net zero eventually, so a cheap cut bought in a developing country is one that country can no longer use toward its own target. Domestic transformation also builds the industries and supply chains that are the real prize of the transition.
- Best case for “Cut where cheapest”: The atmosphere does not care where a tonne is avoided, and the same money buys several times more abatement in a coal-heavy developing economy than in an already-decarbonized European grid. High-integrity credits under the Paris Agreement’s Article 6 rules, agreed in detail at COP29 and refined at COP31, move finance to where it is most needed and let rich countries keep demanding targets without imposing the steepest, costliest last cuts at home. Removals are not a loophole but the only way to deal with residual emissions in farming, aviation and cement.

## Positions

### 1. Hold the 2050 course with steep cuts this decade
- Adherents:
  - European Commission (institution): describes the EU’s legally binding 2040 target of 90 percent below 1990, adopted March 2026, as “a pragmatic and flexible pathway towards a decarbonised European economy by 2050” that provides “predictability for citizens, businesses and investors.” https://climate.ec.europa.eu/eu-action/climate-strategies-targets/2040-climate-target_en
  - UK government, Energy Secretary Ed Miliband (institution and person): proposed the seventh carbon budget (about 87 percent below 1990 for 2038–42), legislated in June 2026, saying “the only way to protect family and business finances is to drive for clean homegrown power that we control.” https://www.gov.uk/government/news/energy-security-jobs-and-investment-boost-through-climate-action
  - Also in this camp: the Climate Change Committee as statutory adviser, whose pathway the budget follows; the IEA’s net-zero roadmap; most rich-country governments with legislated 2050 targets (Japan, Canada, the EU member states).
- The case in its own voice: Net zero by mid-century, with the deepest cuts front-loaded into the 2030s, is the pace the science sets and the economics now supports. The cheap phase is under way: renewables undercut new fossil power in most markets, electric cars are a quarter of global sales, and heat pumps and EVs already give households lower and steadier bills. The legislated dates give investors the certainty to build grids, factories and power plants on 30-year horizons, and the interim budgets keep the path honest. Going slower would leave rich economies exposed to the next oil and gas shock, cost more in climate damage than it saves in mitigation, and hand the clean industries of this century to China. Going faster than the legislated path risks the public consent the whole project depends on. The job now is delivery, not renegotiation.
- Best evidence: The CCC estimates the UK pathway’s net cost at about 0.2 percent of GDP a year, with net savings in the 2038–42 budget period; the OBR finds the fiscal cost of net zero (£803 billion to 2050–51) has fallen by 9 percent of GDP since 2021 and is far smaller than the fiscal cost of climate damage in a 3 °C world. After the 2026 Hormuz shock, the CCC found gas-and-petrol households’ bills rose almost four times as much as heat-pump-and-EV households’. Under the framework the UK has cut emissions 54 percent since 1990.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that the interim targets are already slipping and the costs are hitting voters now): Slippage is a delivery problem with known fixes: moving levies off electricity bills, faster grid connections and planning, targeted support for low-income households. Missing a milestone is a reason to strengthen policy, not to move the goalposts, because every delay means steeper and costlier cuts later and more warming locked in. Those demanding faster cuts are right about the science but wrong about what voters and supply chains can absorb in the 2020s; those demanding repeal would trade a manageable transition cost for the certainty of higher exposure to fossil price shocks.
- Variation inside the camp: governments differ on instruments (the EU leads with carbon pricing, the UK with sector budgets and auctions, Japan with green transformation bonds) and on whether international credits should count (the EU’s 2040 target allows up to 5 points from 2036).

### 2. Keep net zero but ease near-term mandates
- Adherents:
  - European People’s Party Group in the European Parliament (institution): in its position paper “Securing the competitiveness of the European automotive industry” (December 2024) called for the 2035 ban on new CO2-emitting cars to be reversed in favor of a technology-neutral approach, arguing that EU climate policy should prioritize market-based measures such as emissions trading over blanket bans. https://eppgroup.eu/newsroom/epp-group-position-paper-securing-the-competitiveness-of-the-european-automotive-industry
  - Rishi Sunak, as UK prime minister (person): in September 2023 delayed the petrol and diesel car phase-out to 2035 and eased boiler rules, saying “we will still meet our international commitments and hit Net Zero by 2050” through “a more pragmatic, proportionate, and realistic approach to meeting Net Zero that eases the burdens on working people.” https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/pm-speech-on-net-zero-20-september-2023
  - Also in this camp, in practice: the governments of France and Germany, which in October 2026 jointly proposed a 90 percent rather than 100 percent car CO2 cut for 2035; the EU Council and Parliament majority that added international credits and a one-year ETS2 delay to the 2040 package.
- The case in its own voice: The destination is right and should stay in law, but the near-term schedule was set when energy was cheap and before two price shocks, and it now asks households and industry to move faster than technology, supply chains and incomes allow. Forcing people to replace boilers and cars on a fixed date, or loading carbon costs onto heating and fuel bills in a cost-of-living crisis, risks the public consent that the 2050 target needs. The sensible course is to keep the long-term goal and the carbon price, give flexibility on the route (technology neutrality, credits, phased mandates, more time for the hardest sectors), and let cheaper technology do more of the work in the 2030s. Easing the near term is what saves the long term.
- Best evidence: The DESNZ tracker finds 39 percent of Britons expect net zero to hurt the economy in the next two years against 22 percent who expect it to help, while a majority expect long-term benefits. The CCC itself finds credible UK policies cover only 44 percent of the cuts needed for the 2030 target, and the EEA projects the EU just short of its 2030 goal even with planned measures; the delivery gap suggests the near-term dates are out of step with what is deliverable. The EPP notes that the emissions trading system has far more climate impact than the car rules, so easing the mandate costs little in emissions.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that every softening signals that targets are negotiable, raising investment risk and making later cuts steeper): A target that is visibly unrealistic damages investment certainty more than an honest adjustment does, and a target repealed by a backlash government damages it most of all. The flexibilities keep the legal end point and the carbon price, which is what long-lived investment depends on. To those who would repeal the targets outright, this camp answers that the low-cost decarbonization already achieved shows the goal is affordable when the route is flexible; to those who want faster cuts, that consent is the binding constraint and ignoring it is how targets get lost altogether.

### 3. Repeal binding net-zero targets
- Adherents:
  - Conservative Party (UK) (institution): Shadow Energy Secretary Claire Coutinho said in August 2026 that “we should abolish counterproductive Net Zero legislation pushing us towards more expensive electricity,” proposing a “Cheap Power” plan with new gas and nuclear under which, the party says, the electricity system “would still be nearly 80% clean.” https://www.conservatives.com/news/the-hidden-cost-of-net-zero
  - Liberal Party of Australia (institution): voted on 13 November 2025 to abandon its net zero by 2050 policy; Shadow Energy Minister Dan Tehan said the party would reduce emissions in five-year blocks in line with other countries, and the party would remove the 2030 and 2050 targets from the Climate Change Act if elected. https://www.thedailyaus.com.au/politics/liberal-party-has-ditched-its-net-zero-policy
  - Also in this camp: Reform UK, which pledges to scrap net zero to cut energy bills; Net Zero Watch, the campaign of the Global Warming Policy Foundation, which urged Parliament to reject the seventh carbon budget (https://www.netzerowatch.com/); the US administration, which withdrew from the Paris Agreement and rescinded the 2009 endangerment finding.
- The case in its own voice: Legally binding deadlines decided decades in advance force governments to buy decarbonization at any price, and the price is landing on households and industry as some of the highest electricity costs in the developed world, closed factories and lost jobs, while emissions simply move to China and India. A country with about 1 percent of global fossil CO2 cannot change the climate by going faster than everyone else; it can only make itself poorer and less secure. Energy policy should aim first at cheap, reliable, secure power; emissions will keep falling where clean options are genuinely cheaper, through nuclear, gas replacing coal and market-led renewables, but without legal targets that override cost. This is not climate denial: Coutinho’s party says it is not in denial about climate change, and the Australian Liberals still say they will cut emissions in line with comparable countries.
- Best evidence: Party-commissioned modeling by Onward and Transira Energy claims a “Cheap Power” system could save UK consumers over £320 billion by 2050, about £540 per household per year, while staying nearly 80 percent clean (a party claim, not an independent estimate). The UK and EU combined emit about 7 percent of global fossil CO2 (OWID, 2025), while India’s emissions are projected to rise 4.4 percent in 2026 alone. COP31 collapsed without agreeing any fossil-fuel language, which this camp reads as evidence that unilateral ambition does not produce a global deal.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that repeal would raise investment risk, increase exposure to fossil price shocks and cost more in climate damage): Investment follows cheap energy and clear market rules, not targets; nuclear and gas investment would rise under a cheap-power policy. The 2022 and 2026 price shocks hit the UK hard partly because it had shut domestic production and firm capacity, so more domestic gas and nuclear, not faster electrification, is the security answer. Climate damage depends on global emissions, which UK or Australian legal targets barely move. To the ease-the-mandates camp, this camp answers that keeping a target you plan to miss is the worst of both worlds; to the innovation camp, that it agrees on cost but sees no reason to keep the legal deadline at all.
- Variation inside the camp: from repealing legal targets while still pursuing a largely clean grid (UK Conservatives, Australian Liberals) to withdrawing from international climate commitments and rolling back emissions regulation altogether (US administration).

### 4. Reach net zero well before 2050
- Adherents:
  - Climate Action Network Europe (institution): said in February 2024 that “Aiming for anything less than net zero by 2040 (...) shows a lack of global leadership” and “contradicts the EU’s fair share commitment to fulfilling the 1.5°C goal of the Paris Agreement.” https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13345/19/2040-climate-target-can-europe-says-aiming-for-a-90-reduction-is-not-enough
  - Kevin Anderson, Tyndall Centre, with John Broderick and Isak Stoddard (persons): their 2020 Climate Policy study concluded that “double-digit annual mitigation rates are required of developed countries, from 2020,” that Paris-compliant budgets imply “full decarbonization of energy by 2035-40,” and that UK and Swedish plans imply a budget “at least a factor of two greater than their fair contribution.” https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/even-climate-progressive-nations-fall-far-short-of-paris-agreement-targets/
  - Also in this camp: Finland, whose 2022 Climate Act sets carbon neutrality by 2035 at the latest (https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1410903/new-climate-change-act-into-force-in-july); Friends of the Earth and Greenpeace; many small island and least developed country negotiators, who press developed countries to bring their net-zero dates forward.
- The case in its own voice: The remaining 1.5 °C budget is less than four years of current emissions, and the countries that used most of the atmosphere’s capacity have the most money, technology and institutional capacity to stop. A fair division of what is left gives rich economies little or none of it, which means net zero around 2035–2040, with finance for poorer countries on top, not 2050. Clean technology is now cheap enough to make this possible; what is missing is political will. Every five years of delay locks in about another 0.1 °C of warming, borne mostly by people who did least to cause it. Slowing down now, after a decade of easy wins, would be a betrayal of the Paris promise that developed countries lead.
- Best evidence: High-income countries account for about 62 percent of cumulative fossil CO2 since 1750 (OWID). The Global Carbon Budget 2026 puts the 1.5 °C budget at 150 GtCO2 from 2027. The Way, Ives, Mealy and Farmer study (Joule, 2022) found that a rapid green transition is likely to produce “trillions of net savings” compared with staying on fossil fuels, even before counting climate damage. Finland has legislated carbon neutrality by 2035, and Germany and Sweden have legislated 2045 [unverified for Germany and Sweden], which this camp cites as proof that earlier dates are politically possible in rich democracies.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that cuts of 10 percent a year are unprecedented outside economic collapse and would destroy public consent): The comparison is not with a recession but with a mobilization: the post-war reconstruction of Europe and the wartime retooling of industry show what rich economies can do when they decide to. The cost of the transition falls with speed because learning curves reward deployment, while the cost of delay rises. Consent is lost when the burden falls on the poor; a fast transition designed around public investment, frequent-flyer levies and support for low-income households can keep it. To the hold-the-course camp: 2050 for rich countries is the global average date, which is not leadership. To the repeal camp: small shares add up, and nearly every country is a small share.

### 5. Pace cuts to cheap clean technology, not deadlines
- Adherents:
  - Breakthrough Institute (institution): its executive director Ted Nordhaus writes that the institute has long opposed “arbitrary and unrealistic emissions targets,” that progress depends on “the pace of technological change,” and that “The long-term trajectory of decarbonization has not changed much over the last 50 years.” https://www.breakthroughjournal.org/p/policy-built-to-last
  - Bill Gates (person): in his October 2025 memo “Three tough truths about climate,” argued that a doomsday outlook has led the climate community to focus too much on near-term emissions goals, diverting resources from the most effective work, and that the aim should be driving the cost premium of clean options to zero. https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/bill-gates-calls-for-climate-fight-to-shift-focus-from-curbing-emissions-to-reducing-human-suffering
  - Also in this camp: the Tony Blair Institute’s April 2025 report “The Climate Paradox,” in which Blair wrote that “any strategy based on either ‘phasing out’ fossil fuels in the short term or limiting consumption is a strategy doomed to fail” (https://www.globalgovernmentforum.com/current-climate-policy-doomed-to-fail-says-former-uk-pm-tony-blair/); Bjørn Lomborg and the Copenhagen Consensus Center, which argue for large increases in green R&D instead of costly near-term cuts.
- The case in its own voice: Asking how fast rich economies should decarbonize treats the pace as a dial governments can set by law. It is not. Emissions fall when clean options become cheaper and better than fossil ones, and not much faster; that is why the deepest real cuts have come from cheap gas displacing coal, cheap solar and wind, and soon cheap batteries, not from deadlines. Targets set far ahead of the technology produce mandates people resent, costs they notice, and backlash that endangers the whole effort. The right questions are where the green premium is still high (steel, cement, aviation, heat, firm power) and how to drive it to zero through research, demonstration, nuclear, permitting reform and early markets. Make clean cheap and the whole world, not just the rich countries, will adopt it; keep it expensive and no target will hold.
- Best evidence: This camp points to the cost curves: IRENA reports utility-scale battery storage about 95 percent cheaper than in 2010 and more than 90 percent of new renewable projects undercutting fossil alternatives, a fall it attributes to cheaper equipment and scale. Gates wrote in his memo that projected emissions have been cut by more than 40 percent in the past ten years, largely through cheaper technology (as quoted in coverage of the memo [verify on primary]). The rapid swings in rich-country climate law since 2023 (repeal pledges in the UK and Australia, US withdrawal, EU softening) support this camp’s claim that target-led policy is fragile and that durable policy is built on cost.
- Answer to the strongest objection (that without deadlines nothing forces deployment, and deployment is itself what drives costs down): Deployment does drive learning, which is why this camp backs early markets, procurement and standards in the sectors where they make technologies cheaper; it opposes economy-wide deadlines that force adoption before the technology is ready. To the hold-the-course camp, it says the dates are now a political liability that cheap technology would make unnecessary; to the repeal camp, that abandoning decarbonization policy would forfeit the innovation that makes energy cheap; to the faster-cuts camp, that the countries whose emissions are rising will adopt clean technology only when it is cheaper, and rich-country deadlines do nothing to make it so.
- Note on placement: this camp rejects the question as posed (a legislated pace) rather than choosing a date. Its weight is in elite and philanthropic opinion more than in party platforms, which places it fifth.

## Crux

Is the extra cost of cutting faster in rich economies worth what it buys, once you count both the damage avoided and what rich-country action does to everyone else’s emissions?

Every camp on this page accepts that emissions must eventually reach net zero; they part over whether the next tonne cut in Europe, the UK, Japan or North America this decade is worth its price. If damages per tonne are high, clean technology gets cheaper the faster it is deployed, and rich-country action pulls global emissions down, then the case for holding or speeding the pace is strong. If damages are moderate, costs rise steeply with speed, and rich-country cuts mostly leak abroad or are swamped by growth elsewhere, then the case for easing, repealing or re-basing the targets on technology is strong.

What evidence could settle: the size of climate damages per tonne, which now ranges from about $66 to more than $1,200 across peer-reviewed estimates; how steeply the cost of decarbonization rises when the timetable is compressed, including the grid, backup and network costs that levelized-cost figures leave out; whether rich-country deployment still drives global clean-technology costs down now that China dominates manufacturing; how much industrial activity and emissions actually move abroad when rich economies cut faster; and whether faster electrification reduces or raises households’ exposure to energy-price shocks. Each of these is measurable, and the record of the 2022 and 2026 price shocks, the EU’s carbon border adjustment and the next decade of cost data will narrow them.

What no data can close: how much weight to give people living in 2070 or 2100 against households paying bills today, which is what the discount rate encodes; whether rich countries owe a faster pace because of the emissions their economies made in the past; how much risk of severe, irreversible warming a society should pay to avoid; and how much present cost, and how much loss of political consent, is acceptable to reduce it. Two people who agree on every number can still disagree about the pace, because they disagree about these.
