# Housing Affordability — research

## Overview

Housing affordability is the relationship between what homes cost to buy or rent and what households earn. This topic is about why that relationship has worsened across much of the rich world and which force is mainly responsible: supply (too few homes built, because of zoning and land-use rules, slow permitting, construction costs and a decade of underbuilding after 2008), demand (population growth and immigration, household formation, rising incomes at the top, and above all the cost and availability of mortgage credit), or financialization (housing treated as an investment asset, bought by institutional investors, private equity landlords, second-home and short-term-rental owners and global capital looking for a safe store of wealth). The three diagnoses lead to different cures — legalize and build, restrain demand or credit, or restrict who may own housing and take more of it out of the market — and most of the policy fight is about which cure to reach for first. The scope covers the United States in most depth, with comparisons to Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand and continental Europe where their experience is the evidence. It deliberately excludes homelessness services and policing as policy areas in their own right (homelessness appears as an outcome of affordability), the technical design of mortgage finance and the government-sponsored enterprises, property insurance and climate risk except as a cost pressure, commercial real estate, and informal settlements in low-income countries, where the drivers are different.

Why now: on 11 July 2026 the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law in the United States without President Trump’s signature, after he canceled the signing ceremony to protest the Senate’s failure to pass an unrelated voter identification bill (CBS News, https://www.cbsnews.com/news/housing-bill-becomes-law-trump-sign/). Congress had passed it 85 to 5 in the Senate on 22 June and 358 to 32 in the House on 23 June 2026. It is the broadest US housing law in decades, and it enacts two of this topic’s diagnoses at once: most of its more than 45 provisions aim at supply (streamlined environmental review, grants for zoning reform, factory-built housing, conversions of commercial buildings), while Title 10 bars “large institutional investors” controlling 350 or more single-family homes from buying more, with carve-outs for build-to-rent (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21st_Century_ROAD_to_Housing_Act_(United_States)). It followed Executive Order 14376 of 20 January 2026, “Stopping Wall Street from Competing with Main Street Homebuyers” (White House, https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/01/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-stops-wall-street-from-competing-with-main-street-homebuyers). Two weeks before the law passed, on 25 June 2026, New York City’s Rent Guidelines Board voted 7 to 1 to freeze rents on roughly one million rent-stabilized apartments for both one- and two-year leases, the first freeze on two-year leases in the city’s history and the signature promise of Mayor Zohran Mamdani (NY1, https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/news/2026/06/25/nyc-rent-guidelines-board-final-vote).

The debate is live because the evidence now points several ways at once. Prices and rents rose sharply after 2020, and the US median home now costs 4.7 times the median household income against 3.2 in the 1990s, while a record 22.7 million renter households spend more than 30 percent of their income on housing (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, June 2026). Supply advocates can point to cities such as Auckland, Minneapolis and Austin, where large building booms were followed by flat or falling rents. Critics of financialization can point to the rise of corporate single-family landlords and to the shrinking stock of low-rent homes, which fell by more than 7 million units in a decade even as building picked up. Macroeconomists note that the most dramatic swings in prices tracked interest rates, and that the 30-year US mortgage rate went from a record low of 2.65 percent in January 2021 to 7.79 percent in October 2023. And the sudden collapse of US immigration in 2025 is turning the demand argument into a live experiment. The participants include pro-building “YIMBY” groups and many economists, tenant unions and democratic-socialist politicians, homeowner and neighborhood associations defending local zoning control, homebuilders and landlords’ trade associations, national governments of every political stripe, central banks, and a populist right that blames both Wall Street landlords and immigration.

The ratio understates the monthly squeeze because it ignores interest rates: the 30-year mortgage rate averaged 7.04 percent in January 2025 and 6.23 percent by the fourth quarter, against under 3 percent in 2021, so the same price-to-income multiple costs far more a month than it did at the start of the decade. Note: A record 22.7 million US renter households, 49 percent of all renters, spent more than 30 percent of their income on housing in 2024, including 12.1 million who spent more than half.
Context: “Cost burdened” is the standard US measure: housing costs above 30 percent of pre-tax income, and “severely cost burdened” above 50 percent. The count has set a new record in each of the last several releases and has grown by 2.3 million since 2019, even though market rents stopped rising in many metros in 2024 and 2025, because the data lag and because burdens are concentrated among lower-income renters whose incomes have not kept up. Homeowners are not exempt: 20.7 million owner households (24 percent) were also cost burdened, and the Joint Center reports that property taxes rose 31 percent and average insurance premiums 72 percent between 2019 and 2025. The 30 percent threshold is a convention, not a law of nature: a high-income household spending 35 percent is not in the same position as a low-income one, which is why the severe-burden figure is often the more telling one.

Context: Mortgage rates are the strongest short-run driver of what buying a home costs each month, and the 2021 to 2023 jump is the largest in four decades. At a fixed price, moving from 3 percent to 7 percent raises the monthly principal-and-interest payment by roughly 60 percent. High rates cut both ways for prices: they reduce what buyers can borrow, which restrains prices, but they also “lock in” owners who hold older low-rate mortgages, which keeps homes off the market; the Joint Center reports residential mobility at a record-low 11.2 percent in 2024 and existing home sales stuck near a three-decade low of 4.1 million. Rates are set largely by the bond market and the central bank rather than housing policy, which is why the demand-and-credit camp treats them as the variable that housing debates most often leave out. Label: Effect of falling real interest rates on UK house prices
Value: 20
Unit: percent fall in real house prices from a sustained 1 percentage point rise in real interest rates (just under)
Viz: bar
Data year: 1985 to 2018
Source label: David Miles and Victoria Monro, Bank of England Staff Working Paper No. 837 (published in Economic Policy)
Source URL: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2019/uk-house-prices-and-three-decades-of-decline-in-the-risk-free-real-interest-rate
Note: Bank of England staff research finds that the fall in real interest rates can more than account for the rise in UK house prices relative to incomes from 1985 to 2018, and that a sustained 1 point rise in real rates would eventually cut real prices by just under 20 percent.
Context: This is the strongest single piece of evidence for the view that housing costs are driven by the price of money rather than the number of homes. UK real house prices almost quadrupled over forty years, far faster than real incomes. Miles and Monro model the house as an asset whose price reflects the discounted value of the housing services it provides, so when the risk-free real interest rate falls, as it did for three decades, the price rises even with no change in supply. The finding does not say supply is irrelevant: in their framework, a place with more elastic supply would see more building and less price growth for the same fall in rates. But it shifts the question from “why haven’t we built enough?” to “why did capital pour into housing?”, which is the question credit-focused economists such as Josh Ryan-Collins and Ian Mulheirn have pressed. The 2022 to 2023 rate rises offered a partial test: UK prices fell in real terms and the England affordability ratio improved every year since 2021.

Note: When San Francisco extended rent control to small multifamily buildings built before 1980, landlords of those buildings reduced the rental housing they supplied by 15 percent, while covered tenants became about 20 percent more likely to stay at their address.
Context: This is the most cited modern study of rent control and it supports both camps in the rent-control argument, which is why both cite it. Modern “second-generation” rent stabilization, such as caps tied to inflation that exempt new construction, is designed to avoid these responses, and supporters argue the San Francisco result reflects loopholes rather than regulation as such.

## Questions

Question: Are high housing costs mainly the result of too few homes, or of too much money chasing the homes that exist?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Too few homes / Too much money
Why contested: Prices are set where supply meets demand, so both forces are always at work; the dispute is about which one explains the rise of the last three decades and which one policy can move. The same price chart fits both stories: prices soared when interest rates fell and in places that built little, and those two things happened at the same time.
Case for too few homes: The places with the worst affordability are the places that make building hardest. Glaeser and Gyourko show that across most of the United States home prices sit near the cost of construction, and only in a set of heavily regulated metros do they run far above it; if cheap credit were the main cause, it would have inflated prices everywhere equally, and it did not. Houston and Atlanta faced the same mortgage rates as San Jose and Los Angeles and remained several times more affordable. Where cities have changed their rules and let building happen, costs have followed: Auckland’s 2016 upzoning was followed by a construction boom and three-bedroom rents 26 to 33 percent below a synthetic comparison, Minneapolis added 12 percent more homes from 2017 to 2022 while rents rose 1 percent, and the US apartment wave of 2023 to 2025 pushed national asking rents down for the first time since 2021. Low interest rates raise the value of scarce land; they do not create the scarcity. A market that could build freely would turn cheap money into more homes, not higher prices.
Case for too much money: Housing is an asset as well as a shelter, and asset prices move with the cost of money. Bank of England staff found that the fall in real interest rates can more than account for the rise in UK house prices relative to incomes between 1985 and 2018, and the economist Ian Mulheirn has argued that England added homes faster than households over much of that period. The sharpest price moves of the last five years tracked rates, not construction: US prices jumped when mortgages hit 2.65 percent in 2021 and stalled when they reached 7.79 percent in 2023. Credit expanded enormously as banks shifted lending from business to mortgages, and tax systems reward owning property with exemptions on capital gains and, in some countries, deductions for interest or rental losses. Building more at the margin cannot offset a structural surge in demand for housing as a store of wealth, because developers build only as fast as they can sell without lowering prices. The supply story also struggles with the evidence that expensive cities are expensive mainly because high-paying jobs cluster there, which new construction in those cities would reinforce rather than relieve.

Question: Does building new market-rate housing make homes cheaper for lower-income renters?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Filters down / Serves the affluent
Why contested: New market-rate apartments rent near the top of the market, so their direct beneficiaries are households with above-median incomes. Whether that relieves pressure lower down depends on chains of moves and on neighborhood effects that are hard to observe, and the two most visible facts in a changing neighborhood — a new luxury building and rising rents nearby — can be read as cause or as coincidence.
Case for filters down: Every household that moves into a new building leaves a home behind, and someone else moves into that one. Evan Mast traced these chains using address histories for 52,000 residents of new buildings in 12 large US cities and found that a new market-rate building housing 100 people ultimately leads 45 to 70 people to move out of below-median-income neighborhoods, mostly within three years. Studies of the immediate surroundings of new buildings in several US cities find that they lower rather than raise nearby rents, because the added supply outweighs any signal of neighborhood desirability. A 2025 Pew analysis of US metros found rent growth slowed most in older, cheaper “Class C” apartments where supply grew most. And the counterfactual matters: when new homes are not built, higher-income newcomers do not disappear, they bid for older homes in working-class neighborhoods, which is how the US lost more than 7 million units renting under $1,000 in a decade.
Case for serves the affluent: Filtering is slow, uneven and conditional. It works when a region builds far more than it needs, which almost no expensive region has done, and it takes decades for a new unit to age into affordability, during which the households who need it most are displaced. The Harvard Joint Center, which supports building more, concludes that “private markets are incapable of producing enough deeply affordable units” and notes that the low-rent stock shrank even through a construction boom. Some neighborhood-level studies, including Damiano and Frenier’s work on Minneapolis, find that new high-end buildings raise rents in the lowest-cost nearby units even as they soften the upper market, consistent with new buildings signaling to landlords and higher-income renters that a neighborhood is changing. For the 11 million extremely low-income US renter households, who can afford rents well below the cost of operating any new building, filtering will never reach far enough without subsidy.

Question: Do large investors and corporate landlords push up home prices and rents?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Investors drive prices / Investors a sideshow
Why contested: The national numbers are small and the local numbers are large. Large investors own only a few percent of US single-family rentals, but about a quarter in metro Atlanta, and in particular neighborhoods they can be the dominant buyer. Research can show correlation between investor buying and price growth in those places, but investors deliberately buy where prices are expected to rise, which makes cause and effect hard to separate.
Case for investors drive prices: Institutional buyers pay cash, close fast, use algorithms to bid on hundreds of homes at once and target exactly the starter homes that first-time buyers want, so even a modest share of purchases can set the price at the margin. Studies reviewed by the Congressional Research Service find that Wall Street landlords raised rents after consolidating ownership, that institutional purchases reduced homeownership in the neighborhoods where they concentrated, and that large landlords can exercise market power over rents. Across the wider investor market, the share of single-family homes bought by investors has risen from 6 to 8 percent in the early 2000s to about 19 percent in 2026 by Redfin’s count and 30 percent by Cotality’s broader one. Short-term rentals do similar damage in tourist cities: a US-wide study found that more Airbnb listings raise rents and house prices by converting long-term rentals to short-term ones without adding homes. Across the political spectrum, from the Trump administration to the UN’s special rapporteur on housing, the conclusion has been the same: housing bought as a financial asset is housing taken away from people who need a place to live.
Case for investors a sideshow: Investors cannot explain a price rise that happened in places where they barely operate. Large firms own under 2 to 5 percent of single-family rentals nationally and a much smaller share of all homes, and their buying fell sharply after 2022, yet prices kept rising. Most investor purchases are by small landlords owning a handful of homes. Research summarized by the Congressional Research Service finds that institutional buyers helped stabilize prices after the 2008 crash, that they tend to buy homes needing more repair than a typical family can take on, and that a GAO study of six heavily targeted metros found homeownership rates rose in four of them. Institutional landlords also supply rental homes to families who cannot buy, and an outright ban redirects capital away from build-to-rent communities that add new homes. The Harvard Joint Center judged that restrictions on institutional buyers “may reduce competition for homes but do little to address the high cost of homebuying.” Blaming investors is politically satisfying because it names a villain, but it diverts attention from the rules that keep homes from being built.

Question: Should neighborhoods or higher levels of government decide what housing gets built?
Tag: value
Poles: Local residents decide / State sets rules
Why contested: Land-use decisions have always been local in the United States, Canada, Britain and Australia, and local control is valued in itself as a form of self-government. But the benefits of new housing are spread across a region and the future residents who would live in it do not yet vote there, while the costs of change fall on current neighbors who do.
Case for local residents decide: The people who live in a place know its streets, schools, sewers and fire routes, and they bear the consequences of what gets built. Local planning lets communities negotiate affordability requirements, infrastructure contributions and design in exchange for growth; a one-size mandate from a state capitol removes that leverage and hands it to developers. Blanket upzoning near transit can put rent-controlled older buildings at risk of demolition, which is why some tenant advocates in Los Angeles opposed California’s SB 79 alongside homeowner groups. Places that have chosen to stay low-density are not denying anyone a home; they are choosing a way of life that the residents paid to live in, and democracy includes the right to choose it. Where local decisions have been slow, the cure is better local planning, not removing the neighbors’ voice.
Case for state sets rules: A city council elected by current residents has every incentive to block new neighbors and no one on the other side of the table, so local control systematically produces too little housing. The costs of that choice fall on renters, young people and newcomers who have no vote in the neighborhood that excludes them, and on the whole region through longer commutes and higher rents. Zoning in the United States has a documented history of being used to exclude by race and income, and single-family-only rules still cover most residential land in many cities. States set rules for schools, roads and the environment; the housing market is regional, so housing rules should be too. California, Oregon, Montana, Washington and New Zealand have all moved decisions upward for this reason, and the most dramatic affordability improvements, such as Auckland’s, came from reforms that overrode neighborhood-by-neighborhood decisions.

Question: Do rent controls help renters more than they hurt them?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Protects tenants / Shrinks supply
Why contested: Rent regulation has clear, immediate winners, sitting tenants who keep their homes and their rent, and diffuse, delayed losers, future renters who face a smaller or pricier market. Economists have mostly opposed hard caps for decades; tenant movements and many voters strongly support them. The designs also vary so much that evidence from one version is often not about another.
Case for protects tenants: Housing is not a product people can easily do without or shop around for; moving is expensive and disruptive, and a large rent increase can force a family out of its school, job and community. Rent stabilization gives tenants the security homeowners take for granted. The most cited study of rent control, from San Francisco, found that covered tenants were about 20 percent more likely to stay at their address and that it kept many long-term, older and lower-income residents in the city. New York’s stabilization system covers roughly a million apartments and has kept a large part of the city’s working population housed in a market that would otherwise have priced them out. Modern designs, which allow increases tied to inflation, exempt new construction and let rents reset between tenants or not, answer the textbook objection: they protect people without deterring new building. And the supply argument assumes the alternative is abundant market housing, which in most expensive cities has not materialized.
Case for shrinks supply: When rents are capped below market, owners find ways out. In the San Francisco study, landlords of newly covered buildings cut the rental housing they supplied by 15 percent by converting to condominiums and redeveloping, shifting the city’s stock toward higher-income owners and, the authors conclude, likely raising rents for everyone not already protected. When St. Paul, Minnesota, passed a 3 percent cap in 2021 that applied to new construction, residential permits fell sharply and the city amended the law to exempt new buildings for 20 years. Rent control also freezes people in place, rewards whoever happens to hold a lease rather than whoever most needs help, and gives owners little reason to maintain buildings. The underlying problem is scarcity, and a price cap on a scarce good does not make more of it; it only decides who gets the homes that exist.

Question: Should a home be a way for ordinary households to build wealth?
Tag: value
Poles: Home as wealth / Home as shelter
Why contested: For most middle-class families in the United States, Britain, Canada and Australia, home equity is their largest asset, and policy has spent a century encouraging ownership for exactly that reason. But a home can only build wealth if it rises in value, and a home that rises in value becomes less affordable to the next buyer. Every policy that would make housing cheaper is, from an owner’s balance sheet, a policy that makes them poorer.
Case for home as wealth: Owning a home is the most successful savings program ever devised for ordinary people: it forces saving through mortgage payments, protects against rising rents in old age and gives families a stake in their community. The gap in wealth between owners and renters, and between Black and white households in the US, is largely a gap in homeownership, and the homeownership rate for households under 35 has fallen to 37 percent. Helping more people buy, including through first-time buyer assistance and keeping mortgage credit available, is how a society spreads property ownership widely rather than concentrating it among landlords. The goal should be making ownership reachable, not making the asset worthless for the tens of millions of families whose retirement depends on it.
Case for home as shelter: Treating housing as an investment guarantees that it will be unaffordable, because a good investment must keep rising faster than incomes, and that means each generation pays more than the last. The political weight of homeowners, who vote at higher rates and dominate local planning, turns the desire to protect home values into rules that block building. The UN special rapporteur on housing described the result as housing disconnected from its social function of providing a place to live. The healthier goal is stable, secure, affordable housing, whether owned or rented, with wealth built through wages and savings rather than through rising land values that transfer money from young to old and from renters to owners.

Question: How much do population growth and immigration drive housing costs?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Major driver / Minor factor
Why contested: More people need more homes, and nobody disputes that population growth raises housing demand. The dispute is over magnitude relative to other forces, over whether immigrants also add to the supply side by building homes, and over whether it is a legitimate reason to change immigration policy; the argument often carries more political weight than its evidence.
Case for major driver: When population grows faster than homes can be built, prices and rents must rise, and in the early 2020s several countries saw exactly that. Canada’s population grew 3.2 percent in 2023, the fastest since 1957, with about 98 percent of the growth from international migration, while housing starts barely moved. The Canadian government’s own immigration plan in October 2024 said lower admissions would cut the country’s housing supply gap by about 670,000 units by 2027, and its budget officer estimated the plan would shrink the 2030 gap by 534,000 units, or 45 percent. In the United States, even economists who favor high immigration, such as the Cato Institute’s Alex Nowrasteh, accept that immigration raises housing prices. Supply cannot respond in a year or two; population can. Matching arrivals to the pace of building is simple prudence.
Case for minor factor: Housing costs rose sharply in places with little immigration and in periods when it was low; US prices soared in 2020 and 2021 when international migration had collapsed during the pandemic, and affordability in the US and UK began deteriorating long before the recent migration waves. Immigrants make up a large share of construction workers, so cutting immigration can slow building as much as it slows demand, and the Harvard Joint Center lists restrictive immigration among the pressures on construction labor. Claims such as the Vice President’s that 30 million illegal immigrants are taking homes rest on a figure more than twice the independent estimates of about 12 to 14 million. And the lesson from cities that grew fast and stayed affordable, such as Houston, is that growth is a problem only when the rules prevent housing from keeping up.

Question: Should governments build and own housing directly, at scale?
Tag: value
Poles: Public builder / Private builders
Why contested: Most rich countries built large amounts of public housing in the mid-twentieth century and then stopped, and the reputations of those programs differ enormously by country: celebrated in Vienna and Singapore, deeply troubled in many US cities. Whether the difference lies in the model or in how it was run and funded is itself disputed, and so is whether public building adds homes or merely replaces private building that would have happened anyway.
Case for public builder: Private developers build only when they can earn a market return, which means they will never build for households that cannot pay market rents, and they slow down precisely when interest rates rise and the need is greatest. A public or non-profit developer can build through the cycle, borrow at government rates, hold land permanently and charge rents that cover costs rather than maximize returns. Vienna, where the city says more than 60 percent of residents live in municipal or limited-profit housing, shows that social housing can be well built, mixed-income and unstigmatized, and that a large non-market sector disciplines rents across the whole city. Seattle voters approved a public developer for mixed-income social housing in 2023; People’s Action’s Homes Guarantee calls for 12 million social housing units nationally, and New York’s mayor has proposed 200,000 permanently affordable, rent-stabilized homes over ten years. Housing is infrastructure, and governments already build roads, schools and water systems.
Case for private builders: The United States tried large-scale public housing and much of it failed, through underfunding, poor maintenance, concentration of poverty and political neglect that no new program is guaranteed to avoid. Government builders face the same land costs, zoning obstacles, construction costs and neighborhood opposition as private ones, and often higher costs per unit. The fastest way to add homes is to let the private sector, which builds almost all housing in every market economy, build more by removing the rules that stop it, and to direct public money to households through vouchers rather than to buildings. Vienna’s model grew over a century in a city with slow population growth and cannot be recreated quickly in a fast-growing one; its waiting lists and restrictions are also a form of rationing.

Question: Does the United States actually have a national housing shortage?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Real shortage / Income mismatch
Why contested: A shortage is measured against a target, and the target is a choice: how many households would exist if housing were cheaper, what vacancy rate counts as healthy, which year counts as normal. Different choices yield anything from zero to 5.5 million homes. The answer matters because it decides whether the cure is building in general or targeting help where the gap is.
Case for real shortage: Almost every indicator that a shortage would produce appeared at once after 2020: record-low vacancy rates for both owners and renters, rapidly rising prices and rents, young adults living with parents or roommates longer, and a surge in homelessness in the most expensive regions. Freddie Mac, Zillow, Brookings, Up for Growth and the National Association of Realtors, using different methods, all find a gap of roughly 3.7 to 5.5 million homes. The country completed fewer homes in the 2010s than in any decade since the 1960s [unverified]. Rents fell once the 2023 to 2025 apartment wave arrived, which is exactly what a shortage being partly relieved looks like.
Case for income mismatch: Kirk McClure and Alex Schwartz find that from 2000 to 2020 the country added more homes than households, both nationally and in most metro areas, and that vacancy rates were not low by historical standards; the Congressional Research Service notes there is “not universal agreement” that a national shortage exists. The Harvard Joint Center’s vacancy-based measure found the gap had shrunk to about 600,000 units by early 2026. What does exist is a severe shortage of homes affordable to low-income renters: 35 affordable and available rentals for every 100 extremely low-income households. That is a problem of incomes relative to rents, and building more market-rate homes in general is an inefficient cure for it; direct subsidy and non-market housing are the targeted ones.

## Positions

Camp: Legalize and build many more homes
Adherents: YIMBY Action and 128 other pro-housing organizations, joint statement on the Senate passage of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, 12 March 2026 (https://yimbyaction.org/blog/pro-housing-coalition-statement-on-the-senate-passage-of-the-21st-century-road-to-housing-act/); Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko, “The Economic Implications of Housing Supply,” Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2018 (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.32.1.3)
Case in its own voice: Housing is expensive because we made it illegal to build enough of it. In most of the places people want to live, zoning allows only detached houses on large lots, parking mandates eat land and money, and every apartment building faces years of hearings, lawsuits and environmental review that a single objector can drag out. Prices in most of the country sit close to what it costs to build a home; in the metros that block construction they run far above it, and that gap is the price of the rules. As the coalition statement put it, “housing costs are too high, and the solution is to build more homes.” More homes of every kind — apartments, townhouses, duplexes, accessory units, factory-built houses, conversions of empty offices — at every price, because every new home relieves pressure somewhere in the chain. We are not against subsidy for the lowest-income households, and many of us support it, but subsidy spent in a market that cannot build just bids up the price of the homes that exist. Fix the rules first, and every other tool works better.
Best evidence: Auckland upzoned about three-quarters of its residential land in 2016, construction boomed, and six years later three-bedroom rents were 26 to 33 percent below a synthetic comparison. Minneapolis grew its housing stock 12 percent from 2017 to 2022 while rents rose 1 percent, against 4 percent and 14 percent in the rest of Minnesota. The US apartment boom of 2023 to 2025 pushed national asking rents down for the first time since 2021, with the steepest drops in the Sun Belt metros that built most. Migration-chain research finds a new market-rate building housing 100 people ultimately frees homes for 45 to 70 people in below-median-income neighborhoods. Builders estimate regulation accounts for 26.4 percent of the price of a new single-family home.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that market-rate building serves the affluent and cannot reach the poorest renters. Our answer is that the affluent are already here, and when we do not build for them they outbid everyone else for older homes; that is how the US lost more than 7 million units renting under $1,000 in a decade. Filtering is slow only where building is scarce. We agree that households at the very bottom need subsidy, and we note that subsidized housing faces the same zoning and permitting barriers, so legalizing construction is the precondition for every public program too. On investors, we part company with those who would ban them: the pro-housing coalition warned that the investor section of the ROAD to Housing Act “would jeopardize the financial viability of building single-family rental homes.” [Correction 2026-10-05: no source confirms this quote or its speaker, and it is not from the pro-housing coalition; the phrase traces to build-to-rent industry commentary. The page paraphrases without quote marks: builders of single-family rentals warned that the investor limit in the 2026 federal law would make such projects unviable.] Within this camp there is variation of degree and emphasis: “abundance” liberals pair deregulation with public investment and are comfortable with tenant protections that exempt new construction, while market-oriented economists and homebuilders emphasize cutting regulation and fees and oppose rent regulation altogether.

Camp: Curb investor and speculative ownership of homes
Adherents: The White House, Executive Order 14376, “Stopping Wall Street from Competing with Main Street Homebuyers,” 20 January 2026 (https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/01/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-stops-wall-street-from-competing-with-main-street-homebuyers); Leilani Farha, then UN Special Rapporteur on adequate housing, report on the financialization of housing, A/HRC/34/51, January 2017 (https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/g17/009/56/pdf/g1700956.pdf)
Case in its own voice: People live in homes, not corporations. Over the last fifteen years, housing has become one of the world’s favorite assets: private equity firms, hedge funds, real estate investment trusts, short-term-rental operators and wealthy buyers from abroad have poured capital into homes because they rise in value and throw off rent. Ordinary families now bid against buyers who pay cash, close in days and use algorithms to make offers on hundreds of homes at once. The White House said it plainly: “institutional buyers with vast resources outbid hardworking families, turning neighborhoods into investor rental portfolios instead of communities.” The UN’s special rapporteur described the same process worldwide as capital investment that “disconnects housing from its social function of providing a place to live in security and dignity.” Building more homes does not help if the new homes are bought by investors, left empty, or rented to tourists by the night. The fix is to decide who housing is for: limit bulk purchases by large investors, tax vacant and speculative holdings, restrict short-term rentals and foreign buyers, and give families and tenants the first chance to buy.
Best evidence: Investors bought about 19 percent of single-family homes sold in 39 large US metros in early 2026, by Redfin’s count, against 6 to 8 percent in the early 2000s; Cotality’s broader definition puts it at 30 percent. Institutional investors own about a quarter of single-family rental homes in metro Atlanta. Research reviewed by the Congressional Research Service finds that institutional landlords raised rents after consolidating ownership, reduced homeownership in the neighborhoods where they concentrated, and can exercise market power over rents. A US-wide study found that growth in Airbnb listings raises both rents and house prices by converting long-term homes into short-term rentals without adding any. Farha’s report put the value of global real estate at about $217 trillion, nearly 60 percent of the value of all global assets, three-quarters of it residential.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that large investors own only a few percent of homes nationally, too little to move prices. National averages hide what matters: investors concentrated their buying in specific metros, neighborhoods and price tiers, the starter homes that first-time buyers need, and a buyer who wins a quarter of the bids in a neighborhood sets the price there. Nor is our argument only about the largest firms: the share of homes bought as investments of every size has roughly tripled since the early 2000s. We accept that building more is necessary, and we note that Congress agreed, passing supply measures and an investor limit in the same law. The two are complements: new homes should go to the families who will live in them. Within this camp there are differences of instrument and emphasis: the Trump administration and many Republicans focus on Wall Street buyers and immigration while opposing rent regulation; many progressive Democrats pair investor limits with tenant protections and public building; and a macro-finance wing, including Bank of England staff research and economists such as Josh Ryan-Collins, argues the deeper speculative force is cheap credit and tax privileges for property, and favors tighter mortgage lending rules, reforms to property taxation and land value taxes over bans on particular buyers.

Camp: Build more homes and protect tenants at the same time
Adherents: Mayor Zohran Mamdani of New York City, whose administration secured a rent freeze and proposed 200,000 new permanently affordable homes alongside citywide rezoning (City & State New York, May 2026, https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/05/5-things-know-about-mamdanis-big-housing-plan/413771/; Mayor’s Office statement, June 2026, https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/06/mayor-mamdani-s-statement-on-the-rent-guidelines-board-s-final-v); Shane Phillips, UCLA Lewis Center Housing Initiative, The Affordable City and its “supply, stability and subsidy” framework (https://www.planetizen.com/features/110948-affordable-city-offers-solutions-us-housing-affordability-crisis)
Case in its own voice: The fight between “just build” and “just protect tenants” is a false choice, and it has cost renters a decade. A city needs enough homes, or every other policy is fighting over scraps; it needs stability, so that the people who live there now are not displaced by rent spikes and no-fault evictions while the new supply arrives; and it needs subsidy, because no market will ever build homes that the lowest-income households can afford. Shane Phillips calls these the three S’s: supply, stability and subsidy, each necessary and none sufficient. In practice that means legalizing apartments near transit and across wealthy neighborhoods that have shut them out, streamlining approvals, and at the same time capping rent increases on existing homes in ways that exempt new construction, funding public and non-profit developers, and guaranteeing tenants a lawyer in eviction court. New York is attempting all of it at once: a rent freeze for about a million stabilized apartments, a plan for 200,000 permanently affordable homes, and rezoning that goes further than the city’s previous reform.
Best evidence: The evidence for each half is the evidence the other camps cite. The building booms in Auckland, Minneapolis and the US Sun Belt show supply lowers rents; the San Francisco rent control study shows that tenants covered by stabilization are about 20 percent more likely to stay in their homes; the loss of more than 7 million low-rent units while construction rose shows that supply alone does not reach the bottom. The Harvard Joint Center, which documents both the shortage and the collapse in low-rent housing, concludes that “market forces alone are unlikely to meet the need for deeply affordable homes,” [Correction 2026-10-05: this quote is not in the Joint Center’s 2026 report; the report’s actual line is “significant public subsidies are essential for generating the deeply affordable units in shortest supply” (Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, The State of the Nation’s Housing 2026).] while also crediting zoning reform and the construction wave with easing vacancy shortages.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection from the supply side is that rent regulation deters building; from the left, that market-rate construction gentrifies. Our answer to the first is design: stabilization tied to inflation, with new buildings exempt for a long period, protects tenants without changing the math for a new project, and the cities that got it wrong, such as St. Paul in 2021, fixed it by exempting new construction. Our answer to the second is that the alternative to new market-rate buildings in a growing city is not no gentrification but gentrification of the existing stock, and that stabilization and public building are how a neighborhood keeps its people while it grows. Critics on both sides doubt the combination holds under pressure: whether a 0 percent rent increase, as New York adopted in June 2026, starves older buildings of maintenance money, and whether a city can finance 200,000 public homes. Those are fair tests, and they will be answered by results.

Camp: Keep land-use decisions with local communities
Adherents: Neighbors for a Better California, campaign against SB 79 (https://www.neighborsforabettercalifornia.org/sb79); Los Angeles City Councilmember Traci Park, co-author of the council’s August 2025 resolution opposing SB 79 (LAist, https://laist.com/news/housing-homelessness/los-angeles-city-council-votes-oppose-sb-79-housing-density-transit-wiener-bill)
Case in its own voice: We want affordable housing too, but the people who live in a community should decide how it grows. When a state overrides local zoning with blanket rules, it does not produce affordability; it hands developers the right to build market-rate towers wherever a bus line runs, with no guarantee of affordable units, no plan for the roads, sewers, schools and fire response the new residents will need, and no say for the neighbors who will live with the result. As Councilmember Park put it, “Sacramento is hijacking local planning, stripping away neighborhood voices, ignoring safety and infrastructure, and handing the keys to corporate developers.” Local planning is where communities negotiate what growth delivers: affordable units, parks, infrastructure contributions, protection for historic buildings and for older rent-controlled apartments that blanket upzoning puts at risk of demolition. Many communities have plans to add housing; they want to do it in a way that fits. Taking away the public’s ability to weigh in undermines trust in government and in the housing that follows.
Best evidence: The Harvard Joint Center reports that new market-rate construction is “largely out of reach” for low-income households and often unaffordable even to median-income renters, with a median asking rent of about $1,900 for a new apartment in 2025, which is why local advocates insist on affordability requirements that state preemption can strip away. In the August 2025 Los Angeles debate, concern was not confined to homeowners: Councilmember Ysabel Jurado, whose district includes Boyle Heights and who usually sides with tenant advocates, warned that upzoning could lead to redevelopment of older rent-controlled buildings, and tenant-advocacy organizations such as the Western Center on Law and Poverty sought amendments to protect affordability. Local infrastructure capacity is real and finite, and wildfire-prone communities in particular face evacuation constraints that a statewide rule does not see.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that local control is a veto held by current homeowners over people who do not yet live there, and that it has produced the shortage. We answer that the shortage has many causes, including interest rates, construction costs and labor, and that cities have approved far more units than developers have chosen to build; entitlement is not the binding constraint in many places. Removing local review does not guarantee affordable homes, only more market-rate ones on developers’ timelines. And the decisions made in a state capitol are not more democratic for being made farther away. This view is held in different forms: homeowner associations focused on neighborhood character and property values, preservationists, some local governments defending their authority on principle, and some tenant and community groups in lower-income neighborhoods who fear that upzoning will bring demolition and displacement. In Britain the same argument is made by rural and green-belt campaigners against national housebuilding targets.

Camp: Take housing out of the market through social ownership and rent regulation
Adherents: People’s Action Institute, Homes Guarantee Agenda 2025 (https://peoplesactioninstitute.org/homes-guarantee-agenda-2025/); Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Senator Tina Smith, the Homes Act to create a federal social housing development authority, September 2024 (https://www.smith.senate.gov/u-s-senator-tina-smith-and-congresswoman-ocasio-cortez-introduce-homes-act-to-tackle-americas-housing-crisis/)
Case in its own voice: Housing costs too much because it is treated as a commodity to be bought and sold for profit rather than as a basic necessity and a right. A private market builds what earns the highest return, and the highest return is never in homes that working people and the poor can afford; it lets landlords raise rents as far as the market will bear, use price-setting software to coordinate increases, and evict tenants who complain. More market housing will not change that logic, and deregulation hands developers the gains. As the Homes Guarantee puts it, “housing must be de-commodified and treated as a basic necessity and civil right rather than an investment.” The answer is to build a large public and non-profit housing sector, financed by government and held permanently outside the speculative market, with rents set by what it costs to run a home or by what households earn rather than by what the market will bear; to cap rent increases for everyone else; and to give tenants real rights. Representative Ocasio-Cortez described the Homes Act as “a public option to meet the needs of people and communities that aren’t being served by the private market.”
Best evidence: Vienna, where the city says more than 60 percent of residents live in municipal or limited-profit housing, has kept housing costs well below those of comparable European capitals for a century. In the United States there are only 35 affordable and available homes for every 100 extremely low-income renter households, a gap of 7.2 million, and the low-rent stock fell by more than 7 million units between 2014 and 2024 despite a construction boom. The Harvard Joint Center, no advocate for this camp, concludes that “private markets are incapable of producing enough deeply affordable units.” Some researchers, including Kirk McClure and Alex Schwartz, argue there is no general national shortage at all but rather a mismatch between what homes cost and what people earn, which supply-side reform cannot close.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that rent control shrinks supply and public housing failed in America. We answer that the San Francisco study everyone cites measured a law with a large loophole — landlords could convert to condos and exit — and that stabilization closed to such exits, paired with public building, does not face the same problem. American public housing did not fail because public ownership fails; it failed because Congress deliberately limited it to the very poorest, capped how much could be built, and then starved it of maintenance funding for decades. Where social housing is mixed-income, well funded and permanent, as in Vienna, it is the most desirable housing in the city. We do not oppose building; we oppose leaving the question of who can afford to live in a city to investors. Within this camp, some groups oppose private market-rate upzoning as a gift to developers that accelerates displacement, while others accept it provided it comes with strong affordability and anti-displacement requirements.

Camp: Slow population growth to ease housing demand
Adherents: Government of Canada, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2025–2027 Immigration Levels Plan announcement, 24 October 2024 (https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/news/2024/10/government-of-canada-reduces-immigration.html); Vice President JD Vance, interview remarks on 13 November 2025 blaming immigration for housing costs, as reported and fact-checked by PolitiFact (https://politifact.com/factchecks/2025/nov/20/jd-vance/housing-affordability-supply-illegal-immigrants/)
Case in its own voice: Housing takes years to plan and build; population can grow by hundreds of thousands in a single year. When a country admits people far faster than it can house them, rents and prices rise, and the people hurt first are young adults and working families already here, along with the newcomers themselves, who end up crowded into overpriced rentals. Matching the pace of arrivals to the pace of building is not hostility to immigrants; it is basic planning. The Canadian government said reducing admissions “will help to alleviate some pressure in the housing market, with the housing supply gap expected to decrease by approximately 670,000 units by the end of 2027.” Vice President Vance put the US version more bluntly, saying in November 2025 that housing is expensive “because we flooded the country with 30 million illegal immigrants,” alongside a shortage of homes. Building more is necessary too, but no plausible rate of construction can keep up with population growth at the levels of the early 2020s.
Best evidence: Canada’s population grew more than 3 percent in 2023 while housing starts barely rose; its Parliamentary Budget Officer estimated that the 2025–2027 immigration plan alone would shrink the country’s 2030 housing gap by 534,000 units, or 45 percent. In the US, net international migration of 2.7 million in 2024 accounted for most population growth, and as it fell to 1.3 million in 2025, household growth slowed from an average of 2.0 million a year in 2021 to 1.1 million and national rents began to fall. Economists who favor high immigration, such as the Cato Institute’s Alex Nowrasteh, accept that immigration raises housing prices, while arguing the trade-off is worth it.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that immigrants build a large share of homes, so cutting immigration slows construction as much as demand, and that housing costs rose in periods and places with little immigration. We accept that construction needs workers and support targeted admission of skilled trades. But demand from population growth is immediate and cumulative, while construction capacity grows slowly, and every other lever in this debate takes years. Reducing the rate of growth buys time for supply to catch up. Within this camp there are sharp differences of tone and aim: Canada’s Liberal government framed its cuts as a temporary pause to restore sustainable growth, and its budget officer’s figures support a measured version; others, including Vance, attach the housing argument to a broader case for deportation and restriction, and some of their figures have been disputed, with PolitiFact noting that his “30 million illegal immigrants” is more than twice independent estimates of 12 to 14 million.

## Crux

Under the arguments about zoning, investors, rent control and immigration sits one question: if a region were allowed to build freely, would the market bring housing within reach of most of the people who need it? Every camp agrees that housing in the most expensive cities costs far more than it did a generation ago, and almost every camp now supports building more in some form; the same 2026 law that restricted institutional investors carried the largest package of supply measures in decades, and it passed with overwhelming bipartisan majorities. What divides them is what building can accomplish on its own. One side holds that high prices are overwhelmingly the price of scarcity created by rules, that a region which lets homes be built will see prices fall toward the cost of construction, and that every other intervention — investor bans, rent caps, public developers, immigration cuts — is either a distraction from that or works only once it is done. The other side holds that housing prices are set by forces building cannot outrun — the cost of credit, the pull of wealth into property, the clustering of high incomes, the investor’s return — so that even abundant construction leaves the bottom of the market unserved and the middle exposed, and that affordability therefore requires deciding who owns housing and on what terms, not just how much of it there is. The positions in between, and the disputes over investors and immigration, are arguments about how much of each answer is true.

What evidence could settle: how much prices and rents fall, and for whom, when a region builds a great deal more housing for a sustained period; and how that effect compares in size with the effect of interest rates and credit. This is measurable, and the evidence is accumulating faster than at any time in decades. Auckland’s upzoning, Minneapolis’s reforms, the Sun Belt apartment wave of 2023 to 2025, the sharp rise in mortgage rates after 2022, the collapse of US immigration in 2025, and Canada’s deliberate cut in admissions are all natural experiments in progress. Studies that follow them over a full decade, measuring rents at the bottom of the distribution and not only the average, tracking how quickly new homes change hands down the income ladder, and separating the effect of added homes from the effect of rates, would narrow the dispute a great deal. So would an honest national accounting of how many homes exist relative to households, on a definition every side accepts.

What evidence cannot settle: whose interests the housing system should serve first when they conflict. Suppose the evidence showed exactly how much each new home lowers rents. It would not say whether the neighbors who bought into a quiet street of single-family houses have a stronger claim than the renters who would like to live there, whether rising home values are a legitimate reward for owners or an unearned transfer from the young, whether a home is primarily a household’s savings account or its place to live, or whether housing should be allocated by price at all rather than guaranteed as a right. Someone who believes the market is the fairest way to allocate a scarce good will read a successful building boom as proof that the market works; someone who believes shelter should never depend on what the market will bear will read the same boom as a partial fix for a system that is wrong in principle. Both can accept the same numbers, and both will be reasoning consistently from where they start.

On 22 October 2026 the US Treasury issued proposed regulations implementing Section 1001 of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, the bar on single-family home purchases by large institutional investors; the release says that “beginning on January 7, 2027, a large institutional investor generally may not purchase, or enter into a contract to purchase, a single-family home unless the purchase qualifies as an ‘excepted purchase,’” and that the law defines such an investor partly by “direct or indirect investment control over at least 350 such homes” (US Department of the Treasury, “The Trump Administration is Making Homeownership More Affordable,” 22 October 2026, https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0665).
