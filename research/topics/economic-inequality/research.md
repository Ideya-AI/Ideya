# Economic Inequality — research

## Overview

Economic inequality is the gap between what the rich and the poor have, measured either as income (what people earn and receive in a year) or as wealth (what they own, net of debts), within one country or across the whole world. This topic covers the causes and cures of that gap: why it is as wide as it is, how much it has actually grown and where, whether it matters in itself or only through what it does to poverty, mobility, health and politics, and which remedies would narrow it or make it matter less. The remedies on the table are taxes and transfers, public investment in children and places, competition and deregulation aimed at rents, and industrial and trade policy aimed at rebuilding well-paid work. The topic deliberately excludes two neighboring debates that have their own pages: wages specifically (minimum wages, unions and the bargaining power of workers, under labor power) and the taxation of extreme wealth (wealth taxes, inheritance taxes and billionaire minimum taxes, under how to tax extreme wealth). Both appear here only where they bear on the overall gap. It also leaves the fairness of meritocracy, universal basic income and universal healthcare to their own pages.

Why now: on 23 September 2026, during the UN General Assembly’s high-level week, the governments of Brazil, Norway, South Africa and Spain convened an event to launch an International Panel on Inequality, a standing scientific body modeled on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, and UN Secretary-General António Guterres called unchecked inequality “a ticking time bomb,” citing the richest 10 percent of the world’s population owning 75 percent of all wealth (UN News, https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/09/1168489). [Correction 2026-10-05: the 23 September event convened backers of a proposed International Panel on Inequality; the panel was not launched, and Guterres said it could deepen understanding of inequality and how to address it. The “ticking time bomb” quote is not attributed to him on the page (UN News, https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/09/1168489).] The panel grew out of a report commissioned by South Africa’s G20 presidency from a committee chaired by Joseph Stiglitz and released in November 2025, which found that 83 percent of countries, home to 90 percent of the world’s people, meet the World Bank’s definition of high inequality, and that the richest 1 percent captured 41 percent of new wealth created since 2000 (G20 Extraordinary Committee of Independent Experts on Global Inequality, summarized by PERI, https://peri.umass.edu/publication/g20-global-inequality-report/). The proposal is now seeking a General Assembly resolution. The United States, which boycotted the November 2025 Johannesburg G20 summit (Al Jazeera, https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/11/8/trump-says-us-to-boycott-south-africa-g20-summit-over-white-genocide), was not among the convening governments.

The debate is live for three further reasons. Top fortunes reached records in the AI-driven market boom: Forbes counted more than 3,400 billionaires worth about $20 trillion in March 2026, and Oxfam timed a January 2026 report on billionaire political power to the Davos meeting. Economists are openly fighting over the basic facts: a 2024 study by two US government tax economists, Gerald Auten and David Splinter, found that the top 1 percent’s share of US after-tax income has barely risen since 1979, against the far larger rise measured by Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, and the two teams have traded rebuttals since. And the politics of the gap have scrambled the old left-right map: alongside the familiar argument between redistributive progressives and free-market conservatives, antimonopoly and “abundance” reformers blame rents and rigged rules, and a national-conservative movement now in government in the United States blames globalization and argues for rebuilding industrial work rather than redistributing income. The participants are international bodies (the UN, World Bank, OECD and IMF), anti-poverty organizations such as Oxfam, academic inequality researchers, free-market think tanks such as the Cato Institute and the American Enterprise Institute, antimonopoly groups, national-conservative think tanks, unions, and the governments that set tax, spending and competition policy.

Income here is measured before taxes and transfers but after pensions and unemployment benefits, and both income and wealth are converted at 2025 purchasing power parity, which adjusts for lower prices in poorer countries. Wealth is more concentrated than income because most people own little beyond a home and some savings. The report also finds that the share of global wealth held by the richest 0.001 percent, about 56,000 adults, rose from around 3.8 percent in 1995 to nearly 6.1 percent in 2025, while the poorer half’s share has stayed around 2 percent since the early 2000s; between 1995 and 2025 the top 1 percent captured 36.7 percent of all global wealth growth and the bottom half 1.1 percent. The G20 committee chaired by Joseph Stiglitz, drawing on similar data over a different window, reports that the richest 1 percent captured 41 percent of new wealth since 2000. Global estimates rely on imputation in countries with weak tax and survey data, so they carry more uncertainty than national series for rich countries. Context: The Distributional Financial Accounts combine the Federal Reserve’s quarterly national balance sheet with its triennial Survey of Consumer Finances, so they are the most current official estimate of how US wealth is divided. The striking movement since 1989 is not only at the bottom. The households between the 50th and 90th percentiles, roughly the middle class, saw their share fall by about 7 points, from 35.7 to 28.8 percent, while the top 1 percent gained about 10 points. The usual explanation is the composition of what each group owns: middle-class wealth is mostly housing and pensions, while top wealth is mostly corporate equity and private businesses, whose values rose much faster over the period. Note: Two leading research teams disagree about how much the richest 1 percent’s share of after-tax income grew in the United States between 1979 and 2019: about 6.4 points in one, about 1.4 points in the other.
Context: This is the central measurement dispute in the debate. Both teams start from IRS tax data and then add the income that never appears on tax returns, such as employer health insurance, retained corporate profits, government transfers and public spending, and the result depends heavily on how that missing income is assigned. Gerald Auten of the Treasury and David Splinter of Congress’s Joint Committee on Taxation, in the Journal of Political Economy in July 2024, found the top 1 percent pre-tax share rose 4.4 points from 1979 to 2019 and the after-tax share 1.4 points, leaving after-tax top shares “about the same as in the early 1960s”; their own table puts the after-tax share at 8.1 percent in 1960, 7.4 percent in 1979 and 8.8 percent in 2019 (https://davidsplinter.com/AutenSplinter-Tax_Data_and_Inequality.pdf). Piketty, Saez and Zucman’s series shows pre-tax growth of 8.3 points and after-tax growth of 6.4 points. In their September 2024 comment they argue that Auten and Splinter assign tax-exempt partnership income to the wrong owners and treat public goods spending like a universal basic income worth 9 percent of national income, and that correcting these choices raises Auten and Splinter’s after-tax rise to 5.4 points. Auten and Splinter have published a reply defending their choices. Both sides agree that the concentration of income reported on tax returns rose sharply; they disagree about how much taxes, transfers and untaxed income offset it. The Congressional Budget Office, using its own method, reports the top 1 percent share of income before transfers and taxes rising from 9 percent in 1979 to 18 percent in 2022 [unverified: from a summary of CBO’s January 2026 report, whose site blocked direct reading].

Data year: latest available year for each country, 2015 to 2024 as shown
Source label: Luxembourg Income Study, via Our World in Data
Source URL: https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/gini-coefficient-before-and-after-tax-lis
Note: The United States and Germany start with almost the same inequality of market income, a Gini of about 0.51, but after taxes and transfers the US figure is 0.394 and Germany’s 0.312.
Context: The Gini coefficient summarizes a whole distribution in one number between 0 and 1. The Luxembourg Income Study harmonizes national household surveys so that countries can be compared on the same definitions, and it reports inequality both of market income (wages, business and capital income) and of disposable income (after income taxes, payroll taxes and cash transfers). The comparison is the core evidence for the view that differences among rich countries come mainly from policy: market inequality in the United States, Germany, Denmark and Belgium is similar, but taxes and transfers cut the Gini by about 23 percent in the United States, 39 percent in Germany and 50 percent in Belgium (calculated from the figures above). There is a known caveat that cuts the other way. Public pensions are counted as transfers, so countries where retirees live on state pensions show many households with near-zero market income, which inflates their market Gini and makes their redistribution look larger than it is. The comparison also leaves out in-kind benefits such as public health care and education, which are larger in Europe, and consumption taxes, which fall harder on the poor.

Note: About 90 percent of Americans born in 1940 grew up to earn more than their parents; for those born in the 1980s the figure is about 50 percent.
Context: This measure, absolute mobility, compares a child’s household income around age 30 with their parents’ at the same age, adjusted for inflation. Its decline is a headline fact on both sides of the debate, but the counterfactuals are what make it contested. The authors estimate that restoring the faster growth of the mid-century economy while keeping today’s distribution would lift the 1980s figure only to 62 percent, whereas keeping today’s growth but sharing it as broadly as in the 1940s would lift it to 80 percent, reversing more than two-thirds of the decline; they conclude that the unequal distribution of growth, more than slower growth, drove the fall. Those who argue the gap is overstated reply that the result depends on the price index used to adjust for inflation and on how household size is handled, and that with other choices a larger share of today’s adults out-earn their parents: Gonzalo Schwarz of the Archbridge Institute (2018) cites roughly three in four.

The curve’s exact shape has been challenged: Adam Corlett of the Resolution Foundation (2016) showed that part of the dip reflects changing country composition, notably stagnating Japan and the collapse of the former Soviet economies, rather than falling incomes for the same rich-country workers. It is evidence for both sides of the trade argument.

Note: In 1980 more than half of all income inequality among the world’s people came from differences between countries; by 2020 it was about a third, with the rest coming from inequality inside countries.
Context: Global inequality can be split into a part caused by some countries being richer than others and a part caused by inequality within each country. From 1820 to 1980 the between-country part grew as Western Europe and North America industrialized and the rest of the world did not. Since 1980 two things have happened at once: fast growth in China, India and other emerging economies narrowed the gap between countries, and inequality rose inside most countries. Overall global inequality has therefore stayed roughly flat at a very high level; the authors find that the ratio of the average income of the world’s top 10 percent to that of its bottom 50 percent rose from under 20 in 1820 to about 40 in 1910 and was still about 40 in 2020 (World Inequality Database summary: https://wid.world/news-article/global-income-inequality-1820-2020/). Which component a reader cares about shapes which camp they find persuasive: the shrinking between-country gap is a central exhibit for growth and trade, the rising within-country gap for redistribution.

Label: Estimated effect of inequality on economic growth, OECD countries
Value: 3
Unit: percent of GDP, cumulative gain over 25 years from a 1-point reduction in the Gini index
Viz: bar
Data year: estimated on data for OECD countries, 1970 to 2010
Source label: Cingano, “Trends in Income Inequality and its Impact on Economic Growth,” OECD Social, Employment and Migration Working Paper 163 (2014)
Source URL: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/trends-in-income-inequality-and-its-impact-on-economic-growth_5jxrjncwxv6j-en.html
Note: The OECD estimated that reducing inequality by 1 Gini point would raise growth by slightly more than 0.1 percentage point a year, adding about 3 percent to GDP over 25 years.
Context: This is the best-known official estimate that inequality slows growth rather than being its price. The paper finds the effect works mainly through the gap between low-income households and everyone else, which it links to lower investment in education by poorer families, and finds no evidence that redistribution through taxes and transfers harms growth. The OECD’s summary brief (December 2014) put the same result as a rise of 3 Gini points, the average increase across the OECD over two decades, dragging growth down by 0.35 percentage point a year for 25 years, a cumulative loss of 8.5 percent of GDP. An IMF team, Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides (2014), reached a similar conclusion: lower inequality after taxes and transfers was associated with faster and more durable growth (https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2014/09/ostry.htm). The literature is not settled. Kristin Forbes (American Economic Review, 2000) found that within countries a rise in inequality was followed by higher short-run growth, and Robert Barro (2000) found inequality slowed growth in poor countries but not in rich ones. Cross-country growth regressions of this kind are sensitive to the sample, the period and the inequality data used, and the OECD itself cautions that its country-by-country illustrations should not be read as the causal effect of each country’s actual change in inequality.

## Questions

Question: Should policy aim to narrow the gap between rich and poor, or only to raise the floor?
Tag: value
Poles: Narrow the gap / Raise the floor
Why contested: This is the moral fork under the whole debate. If what matters is that everyone has enough, a widening gap is harmless whenever the poor are also getting richer; if relative position matters in itself, or through what it does to status, health and power, then a rising floor is not enough. No measurement settles which of these a society should care about.
Case for narrow the gap: People live in societies, not in absolute income tables. What counts as a decent life, a good school, a safe neighborhood or a political voice is set relative to others, so a family can gain in dollars and lose in standing, security and say. Large gaps convert into unequal access to housing, schooling, health and political influence, and into a sense that the system is rigged, which surveys in almost every country now report. A society that tolerates any gap as long as the bottom rises has no answer to a world in which a few thousand people hold a growing share of everything and the rules are increasingly written by them.
Case for raise the floor: The moral emergency is deprivation, not difference. Harry Frankfurt’s argument is that what matters is whether each person has enough, and that fixating on relative shares invites envy and distracts from what people actually need. A policy that cuts the top without lifting anyone at the bottom makes no one better off, while a fortune made by building something millions of people buy usually leaves them richer too. The great reduction in extreme poverty since 1990 happened during a period of high and often rising top shares, which shows the two are not the same problem and that solving the one that matters most does not require attacking the other.

Question: Has inequality in rich countries really risen much, once taxes, transfers and untaxed income are counted?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Sharply risen / Largely overstated
Why contested: Everyone agrees that income reported on US tax returns became far more concentrated after 1980. The fight is over how to count the income that never appears there, such as employer health insurance, retained corporate profits, government transfers and public services, and the leading estimates for the change in the top 1 percent’s after-tax share since 1979 range from about 1.4 to about 6.4 percentage points.
Case for sharply risen: Every major data source shows top shares rising since around 1980: tax data, the Federal Reserve’s wealth accounts, the Congressional Budget Office’s household series and the World Inequality Database. Piketty, Saez and Zucman argue that the low estimates rest on contestable assumptions, such as assigning tax-exempt partnership income to modest sole proprietors and counting public spending on roads and defense as if it were cash income for the poor, and that correcting them brings the estimates close together. Wealth, which is harder to dispute because it is measured directly, shows the top 1 percent’s share rising by about ten points since 1989.
Case for largely overstated: Gerald Auten and David Splinter, career tax economists at the Treasury and Congress, argue that much of the apparent rise is an artifact of tax law changes after 1986 that moved business income onto individual returns, of falling marriage rates among non-rich households, and of ignoring the growth of transfers and refundable tax credits. On their estimates, real after-tax income of the bottom half rose by about two-thirds between 1979 and 2019, and the top 1 percent’s after-tax share is about where it was in the early 1960s. If the gap after government action has barely moved, the case for new redistribution is much weaker than the headline numbers suggest.

Question: Are the largest fortunes mostly earned by creating value, or extracted through rents, market power and political influence?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Earned by contribution / Extracted as rents
Why contested: Both sides agree that some top income is a reward for innovation and some is a return to monopoly, regulatory protection or lobbying. The disagreement is over the proportions, which are hard to measure because a patent, a network effect or a zoning rule can be both a reward and a barrier.
Case for earned by contribution: The fortunes that dominate the current billionaire lists were mostly built by founding companies whose products hundreds of millions of people chose to buy, and economists such as William Nordhaus have estimated that innovators capture only a small fraction of the value their innovations create, with most going to consumers. Top earners increasingly earn their income from work and business ownership rather than inherited capital. Treating every large fortune as suspect would tax and discourage the very risk-taking that raises everyone’s living standards.
Case for extracted as rents: Much top income comes from positions shielded from competition: concentrated industries with rising markups, finance that profits from implicit public guarantees, intellectual property terms far longer than needed to reward invention, licensing rules that keep out competitors, and land-use rules that inflate the value of existing property. Brink Lindsey and Steven Teles call this “regressive regulation,” and on the left Joseph Stiglitz and the Open Markets Institute make the same diagnosis about monopoly. Pew finds that 61 percent of Americans think the political influence of the rich contributes a great deal to inequality, more than any other cause offered.

Question: Does inequality slow economic growth, or is it part of the price of growth?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Drag on growth / Price of growth
Why contested: The classic view, associated with Arthur Okun’s 1975 “leaky bucket,” is that redistribution costs some efficiency. Since 2014, OECD and IMF research has argued the reverse, that inequality itself slows growth, but cross-country growth studies are fragile, and results depend on the countries, period and data used.
Case for drag on growth: OECD research by Federico Cingano (2014) estimates that a 1-point fall in the Gini index raises growth by slightly more than 0.1 percentage point a year, mainly because poorer families underinvest in education, and finds no evidence that redistribution itself harms growth. IMF economists Jonathan Ostry, Andrew Berg and Charalambos Tsangarides (2014) found lower inequality associated with longer and more durable growth spells. Mechanisms include wasted talent among children who never get a chance, household debt build-ups as lower earners borrow to keep up, and political instability.
Case for price of growth: Incentives matter at the margin: high returns to skill, risk and entrepreneurship are what draw people into starting companies and acquiring scarce skills, and high marginal tax rates blunt them. Kristin Forbes (2000) found that within countries rises in inequality were followed by faster growth in the short and medium run, and Robert Barro (2000) found higher inequality associated with slower growth in poor countries but faster growth in rich ones. The growth miracles that cut global poverty, in China and India, were accompanied by rising inequality inside those countries, and the regressions linking inequality to slower growth cannot separate cause from correlation.

Question: What mainly drove the rise in inequality within countries: technology and global markets, or policy and power?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Markets and technology / Policy and power
Why contested: The rise since 1980 coincided with computerization, the opening of China and other economies to trade, financial deregulation, cuts in top tax rates and the decline of unions. All of these plausibly matter, and the answer determines whether the gap is a natural result of economic change that policy can only cushion, or a set of choices that policy can reverse.
Case for markets and technology: Inequality rose across very different countries at the same time, which points to common forces. Technology raised the reward for education and skill faster than schools supplied it, the race between education and technology described by Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz. “Superstar” dynamics let the best firms and performers serve global markets. Trade with low-wage economies hit manufacturing workers in rich countries, as the elephant curve shows for the lower-middle of the rich world. Policy can soften these forces but did not create them.
Case for policy and power: Countries exposed to the same technology and trade ended up with very different levels of inequality. The United States and Germany start with almost identical market-income inequality but end with very different disposable-income inequality, and top income shares rose far more in the English-speaking countries than in continental Europe or Japan. That pattern points to choices about top tax rates, labor law, corporate governance, financial regulation and antitrust. Piketty, Saez and Stefanie Stantcheva (2014) found that countries that cut top tax rates most saw the largest rises in top income shares, without faster growth.

Question: Does concentrated wealth translate into concentrated political power?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Wealth captures politics / Influence is overstated
Why contested: The worry that economic inequality corrupts democracy is one of the main reasons people who are relaxed about the gap for its own sake still oppose it. But measuring influence is hard, because the preferences of the rich and the middle class usually coincide.
Case for wealth captures politics: Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page (2014) found that across nearly 1,800 US policy questions, the preferences of economic elites and organized business groups predicted outcomes while those of average citizens had little independent effect. Campaign finance, lobbying and media ownership are concentrated at the top; Oxfam’s January 2026 report says billionaires are about 4,000 times more likely than ordinary people to hold political office. Policies that favor the wealthy, from carried-interest tax treatment to patent extensions, persist despite public opposition.
Case for influence is overstated: Re-analyses of the Gilens and Page data, including by Peter Enns (2015) and by Branham, Soroka and Wlezien (2017), found that the rich and the middle class agree on most issues and that when they disagree the rich win only slightly more often. Large fortunes are spread across both parties and often cancel each other out, heavily outspent candidates frequently lose, and the expansion of transfers and refundable credits since the 1970s is hard to square with a government captured by the rich. Free speech, including political spending, is also a value in its own right.

Question: Does high inequality cause low mobility, or are they separate problems?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Inequality blocks mobility / Separate problems
Why contested: More unequal countries tend to have less mobility between generations, the “Great Gatsby curve.” Whether the gap causes children’s prospects to diverge, or both stem from other differences such as schooling, family structure and geography, decides whether reducing inequality is a route to opportunity.
Case for inequality blocks mobility: When the rungs of a ladder are far apart, it is harder to climb, and parents at the top have more resources to make sure their children stay there, through housing in good school districts, tutoring, internships and inheritance. Raj Chetty and coauthors estimate that the fall in the share of Americans earning more than their parents, from about 90 percent to about 50 percent, owes more to how growth was distributed than to slower growth. Richard Reeves argues that the upper middle class “hoards” opportunity through exclusionary zoning, legacy admissions and unpaid internships.
Case for separate problems: Mobility varies enormously within a single country with the same national inequality. Chetty and coauthors’ own maps (2014) show large differences between US commuting zones, which correlate with segregation, school quality, social capital and family stability, and with inequality among the bottom 99 percent, but not with the top 1 percent’s share of income. A society could cut its top shares and leave mobility unchanged if the barriers facing poor children are about schools, neighborhoods and family stability; it could also raise mobility without touching the top. Gonzalo Schwarz of the Archbridge Institute argues that conflating poverty, inequality and mobility leads to policies that help none of them.

Question: Should the cure work before the market, by changing what people earn, or after it, by taxing and transferring?
Tag: value
Poles: Change market outcomes / Redistribute afterward
Why contested: Almost everyone in the debate supports some of both. The disagreement is about emphasis and about what each approach costs: transfers are fast and measurable but are criticized for dependence and disincentives; changing market outcomes through education, competition, industrial policy or labor rules is slower and its effects are harder to prove.
Case for change market outcomes: Work is a source of dignity, independence and social standing that a transfer check does not replace, and a labor market in which ordinary workers can support a family reduces the need for transfers in the first place. Removing rents through competition and deregulation makes the market itself fairer instead of correcting it after the fact. Oren Cass argues that the bipartisan formula of growth plus redistribution has left places and people without productive work, whatever their income after transfers.
Case for redistribute afterward: Taxes and transfers are the proven tool: they explain most of the difference between the United States and Northern Europe in final inequality, they reach people who cannot work, including children, older people and people with disabilities, and their effects can be measured and adjusted every year. Market-shaping policies such as industrial subsidies and tariffs have uncertain payoffs, can be captured by the industries they help, and raise prices for the poor. Investing in children is valuable but takes a generation to pay off, while poverty is now.

Question: Is the gap that matters most the one between countries, or the one within them?
Tag: value
Poles: Global gap first / National gap first
Why contested: Most inequality debate takes place inside rich countries, but the largest gaps in the world are between countries: a worker’s country of birth still explains a large part of global income differences. Policies that narrow one gap can widen the other, as with trade that lifted Asian incomes while pressing on rich-country manufacturing workers.
Case for global gap first: A person’s country of birth is the single largest determinant of their income, and moral luck does not stop at borders. The world’s poorest people live on under $3 a day while the working poor of rich countries are, by global standards, well off. Trade, migration and aid that raise incomes in poor countries have done more to reduce human deprivation than any domestic redistribution, and the shrinking between-country share of global inequality since 1980 is the most important equalizing trend in modern history.
Case for national gap first: Political communities carry special obligations among their members: citizens share institutions, pay into common systems and need enough equality to deliberate as equals. Rising inequality within countries is what drives political distrust and populist backlash, and it is the gap that national governments can actually act on. Telling a laid-off worker that global inequality has fallen is no answer to the question of whether their own society is fair.

## Positions

Order note: the first two camps are the main poles and are held most widely; the third is a hybrid drawing adherents from left and right; the fourth rejects the redistributive framing from a different direction and is now the governing economic philosophy of the US administration. Camp 2 is the camp that rejects the question as posed (it denies that the gap is the right target). A further strand that wants to change who owns capital, through social wealth funds, worker ownership or codetermination, differs from camp 1 mainly in degree and instrument and is noted inside it. The meritocracy critique (that rewarding merit itself entrenches inequality) is left to the meritocracy page.

Camp: Shrink the gap with taxes, transfers and public power
Adherents: Oxfam International, https://www.oxfam.org/en/press-releases/billionaire-wealth-jumps-three-times-faster-2025-highest-peak-ever-sparking (19 January 2026, calling on governments to adopt national inequality reduction plans with benchmarks, to tax the richest at rates high enough to reduce extreme inequality, and to wall off private wealth from politics); Joseph Stiglitz, Nobel laureate and chair of the G20 Extraordinary Committee of Independent Experts on Global Inequality, https://peri.umass.edu/publication/g20-global-inequality-report/ (November 2025 report calling inequality an emergency and proposing an International Panel on Inequality, wage increases, reduced corporate concentration, expanded public services and fair taxation of multinationals). Also: the governments of Brazil, Norway, South Africa and Spain, which convened the panel’s launch [Correction 2026-10-05: they convened backers of the panel, which as of October 2026 is still a proposal (UN News, https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/09/1168489).]; the International Trade Union Confederation; economists Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman.
Case: Inequality at today’s level is not a natural law but the product of choices: tax codes that favor capital over work, labor laws that weaken workers, financial and corporate rules written by the powerful, and public services left to decay. Because it was made by policy it can be unmade by policy. A society in which the richest tenth owns three-quarters of the world’s wealth, and a few thousand billionaires add trillions of dollars in a single year while progress against poverty stalls, is not one where growth is trickling down. The gap is harmful in itself: it turns economic power into political power, corrodes trust, shortens lives at the bottom, and locks children into their parents’ position. The remedy is a deliberate program: progressive taxation that reaches the top, generous transfers and public services that reach everyone, and rules that rebalance bargaining power, with international cooperation so capital cannot simply move elsewhere. Countries that do this, such as the Nordic and Benelux democracies, are rich, innovative and far more equal.
Best evidence: Market inequality in the United States and Germany is almost identical (Gini about 0.51), but after taxes and transfers the US figure is 0.394 and Germany’s 0.312, which shows that final inequality is a policy outcome (Luxembourg Income Study via Our World in Data). OECD and IMF research found that lower inequality goes with faster and more durable growth and that redistribution does not measurably harm growth. Chetty and coauthors estimate that how growth was shared, not how fast it was, explains most of the fall in the share of Americans out-earning their parents. The top 1 percent’s share of US household wealth rose from 22.8 percent in 1989 to 32.5 percent in 2026 (Federal Reserve).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that taxing the top discourages the work, investment and innovation that make everyone richer, and that the poor gain more from growth than from redistribution. The camp’s answer is that the evidence for large incentive costs is weak: US growth was fastest in the decades when top tax rates were highest, countries that cut top rates most did not grow faster, and the best cross-country evidence finds redistribution benign for growth. The floor and the gap are also not separable: the political power that comes with extreme wealth is used to block the very floor-raising policies the other side endorses. Global poverty reduction has stalled since 2019, which suggests growth alone is no longer doing the job.
Variation inside the camp: Social democrats emphasize taxes, transfers and universal public services. Others emphasize “predistribution,” shaping market incomes before taxes through stronger unions, minimum wages and public investment in children (the wage instruments are covered on the labor power page). A more radical wing, including Piketty’s proposals for “participatory socialism,” argues for changing who owns capital through social wealth funds, worker seats on corporate boards and universal capital endowments. Internationally, the camp’s current focus is the International Panel on Inequality and global minimum taxation of multinationals and the very rich (wealth taxation itself is covered on its own page).

Camp: Lift the floor rather than narrow the gap
Adherents: Cato Institute, Chris Edwards and Ryan Bourne, “Exploring Wealth Inequality” (Policy Analysis No. 881, 5 November 2019), https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/exploring-wealth-inequality (arguing that measures of wealth inequality tell us nothing about the well-being of the poor, which is the more important focus for public policy; that most of today’s wealthy built their fortunes by adding to growth; and that policymakers should end cronyist programs and reduce barriers to wealth-building by moderate-income households); Michael R. Strain, American Enterprise Institute, “The American Dream Is Alive and Well (and the Problem of U.S. Inequality Greatly Exaggerated)” (2024), https://www.aei.org/research-products/journal-publication/the-american-dream-is-alive-and-well-and-the-problem-of-u-s-inequality-greatly-exaggerated/. Also: the Archbridge Institute (Gonzalo Schwarz), the philosopher Harry Frankfurt’s “doctrine of sufficiency” in On Inequality (2015), and former Senator Phil Gramm and John Early, authors of The Myth of American Inequality (2022).
Case: What matters morally is whether people have enough and can improve their lives, not how their income compares with a billionaire’s. By that standard the modern era is a triumph: extreme poverty has fallen from more than four in ten people in 1990 to about one in ten, and in the United States the real incomes of the bottom half have risen substantially once taxes and transfers are counted. Much of the alarm about inequality rests on measures that leave out transfers, tax credits and household changes; counted properly, the after-tax share of the top 1 percent has barely moved in sixty years. Large fortunes mostly come from building companies whose products people choose to buy, and the economy that produces them is the same one that lifts wages and finances the safety net. Policy should therefore target poverty and opportunity directly, by removing barriers that keep people from working, building homes, starting businesses and getting skills, and by supporting work through measures such as the Earned Income Tax Credit, rather than by trying to pull down the top.
Best evidence: The share of the world living below $3 a day fell from 43.4 percent in 1990 to about 10 percent in 2024 (World Bank via Our World in Data), during a period of high and often rising top shares. Auten and Splinter (Journal of Political Economy, 2024) find the top 1 percent’s US after-tax share rose only 1.4 points from 1979 to 2019 and real after-tax incomes of the bottom half rose by about two-thirds. The elephant curve shows the biggest income gains of the globalization era going to the middle of the world distribution. Strain points to recent fast wage growth at the bottom of the US distribution and a falling share of low-income households.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the gap matters even if the floor rises, because concentrated wealth buys political power and blocks opportunity. The camp’s answer is that the remedy for crony capitalism is to shrink the favors government can sell, through deregulation, ending corporate subsidies and simplifying the tax code, not to tax success in general. Re-analyses of the best-known evidence for elite capture find that the rich and the middle class usually want the same things. Barriers to mobility, from bad schools to zoning and licensing rules, can be removed without targeting the top at all, and doing so helps the poor directly.
Variation inside the camp: Libertarians emphasize growth, deregulation and a limited state. Reform conservatives such as Strain support an active work-based safety net, including a larger Earned Income Tax Credit and wage subsidies. Measurement critics such as Gramm and Early focus on the claim that official statistics overstate inequality by leaving out transfers and taxes. The philosophical root is Frankfurt’s sufficiency view, which some on the left also hold.

Camp: Strip out the rents that rig markets
Adherents: Niskanen Center, Brink Lindsey and Steven M. Teles, The Captured Economy: How the Powerful Enrich Themselves, Slow Down Growth, and Increase Inequality (Oxford University Press, 2017), https://capturedeconomy.com/about/ (arguing that wealthy interests have captured policymaking to produce “regressive regulation” that redistributes upward, through financial regulation, intellectual property, occupational licensing and land-use rules); Open Markets Institute, https://www.openmarketsinstitute.org/learn/income-inequality-monopoly (arguing that antimonopoly policy has been central to America’s efforts to keep wealth and power from concentrating “in the hands of the few”). Also: Richard Reeves, author of Dream Hoarders (Brookings Institution Press, 2017), on how the upper middle class hoards opportunity through exclusionary zoning, legacy admissions and unpaid internships, https://belonging.berkeley.edu/richard-reeves-opportunity-hoarders-its-not-just-1; economist Luigi Zingales; and the “abundance” movement on housing supply.
Case: The problem is not that some people get rich but how. Much of the income at the top, and in the comfortable upper-middle class, comes from positions protected against competition by rules the beneficiaries helped write: concentrated industries with rising profit margins, banks that gamble with implicit public backing, patents and copyrights stretched far beyond what invention needs, licensing laws that shut people out of trades, and zoning rules that turn homeowners in prosperous cities into rentiers while pricing out the young and the poor. These rents both widen the gap and slow growth, so removing them is a rare policy that is fairer and more efficient at once. Taxing and transferring after the fact treats the symptom and leaves the rigged rules in place; opening markets, breaking up monopolies and legalizing housing attacks the cause.
Best evidence: Matthew Rognlie (Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, 2015) found that the rise in the capital share of income in rich countries since the mid-twentieth century came overwhelmingly from housing, which points to scarce, regulated land rather than machines. Jan De Loecker, Jan Eeckhout and Gabriel Unger (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2020) estimate that average markups of US public firms rose from about 21 percent above cost in 1980 to about 61 percent in 2016, though other economists dispute the measurement. Pew finds that 61 percent of Americans say rich people’s political influence contributes a great deal to inequality, the top-ranked cause offered.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from the redistributive camp, is that rents are only part of the story, that reform is slow and politically hard because every rent has a defender, and that people in poverty need help now. The camp’s answer is that it does not oppose a safety net; it opposes treating redistribution as a substitute for fixing the rules. From the other side, the objection is that what looks like monopoly is often scale and innovation. The answer is that the test is whether profits are competed away over time, and in housing, licensing and much of health care they plainly are not.
Variation inside the camp: The camp is a genuine left-right hybrid. Its left wing, around the Open Markets Institute and the antitrust revival of the 2020s, focuses on corporate concentration, platform power and antitrust enforcement. Its center-right wing, around the Niskanen Center, focuses on zoning, occupational licensing, intellectual property and financial subsidies. Reeves’ version targets the top fifth rather than the top 1 percent.

Camp: Rebuild work rather than redistribute income
Adherents: American Compass, founded by Oren Cass, author of The Once and Future Worker (2018), https://americancompass.org/how-the-consumerist-consensus-led-america-astray-and-how-to-recover/ (transcript of a talk in which Cass, introduced as challenging the bipartisan consensus that the answer is more growth and more redistribution, argues that the economic-pie model “ignores who’s baking the pie” and makes the case for what he calls “the working hypothesis,” that a labor market in which workers can support families and communities should be the central focus of policy); Vice President JD Vance, in conversation with Cass at American Compass’s gala on 3 June 2025, https://americancompass.org/transcript-vice-president-vance-speaks-to-oren-cass-at-the-new-world-gala/ (explaining his focus on manufacturing: “I care about workers and I care about their wages”). Also: trade adviser Robert Lighthizer, and on the left some trade-skeptical unions and politicians.
Case: The gap that matters is not the one in a tax table but the one between people who can support a family through productive work and those who cannot. For forty years both parties pursued the same formula: maximize growth through free trade and financial markets, then redistribute some of the gains. The result was rising consumption and rising transfers alongside hollowed-out industrial towns, falling marriage and labor-force participation among men without degrees, and the despair that followed. A transfer check cannot replace the dignity, independence and social standing of a good job. The cure is to rebuild the productive economy: tariffs and industrial policy that bring manufacturing back, vocational education as respected as college, labor institutions that give workers real bargaining power, and an end to an immigration and trade regime that holds down wages at the bottom.
Best evidence: The elephant curve shows that the people who gained least from globalization between 1988 and 2008 were the lower-middle classes of rich countries, around the 80th to 90th percentile of the world distribution, whose incomes grew less than 5 percent while the global middle and top 1 percent grew about 65 to 70 percent (Lakner and Milanovic, 2013). Work by David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson on the “China shock” found that import competition from China after 2000 cost manufacturing jobs and depressed employment and wages in exposed US communities for more than a decade. The share of US wealth held by the middle class, the 50th to 90th percentiles, fell from 35.7 percent in 1989 to 28.8 percent in 2026 (Federal Reserve).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that tariffs raise prices for the poor, invite retaliation, and cannot bring back jobs that automation, not trade, eliminated. The camp’s answer is that consumption is the wrong yardstick: paying somewhat more for goods in exchange for communities where people can work and raise families is a trade most voters would accept. Germany, Japan and South Korea kept large manufacturing sectors despite the same technology, which shows that the loss of industry was a policy choice, not destiny.
Variation inside the camp: National conservatives in the Trump administration lean on tariffs, immigration restriction and reshoring. Cass’s wing also supports new forms of worker representation and a work-based safety net. On the left, trade-skeptical unions and politicians share the diagnosis of globalization but pair it with redistribution rather than against it. The wage and trade instruments are covered in depth on the labor power and free trade pages.

## Crux

The question under the others: is today’s gap between rich and poor mainly the side effect of an economy that is making most people better off, or mainly the result of rules and power that let some people capture what others create, and if it is the first, is a wide gap still something a society should act to narrow?

What evidence could settle: How much of top income and wealth is a return to innovation and productive investment, and how much is rent from market power, protected land, regulatory barriers and political influence; markups, housing’s share of capital income, the returns to lobbying and the persistence of profits are all measurable, if imperfectly. Whether the incomes of the bottom half, counted after taxes and transfers, have kept rising alongside the top, which is the core of the Auten and Splinter versus Piketty, Saez and Zucman dispute. Whether redistribution and higher top tax rates measurably slow growth, and whether high inequality causes lower mobility and unequal political influence or merely accompanies them. Whether reshoring industry raises the earnings of non-college workers by more than it raises their prices. Each of these is contested, but each is the kind of question better data and better research designs can narrow, and progress on them would move people between the camps: a finding that most top income is rent strengthens both the redistributive and the antimonopoly camps, while a finding that the floor has risen steadily and redistribution is costly strengthens the case for focusing on the floor.

What no data can close: Whether relative position matters in itself. Someone who believes that what matters is that everyone has enough can accept a rising floor with a widening gap; someone who believes that great inequality is a wrong in itself, or that it inevitably becomes inequality of status and voice, cannot. A related choice is what to count as the measure of a good economy: what households can consume, the dignity and independence of earning a living, or the absence of domination by the powerful. And there is the question of whose gap counts: the one among fellow citizens, who share institutions and obligations, or the one among all the world’s people, where the largest gaps lie between countries. Evidence can show what each policy does; it cannot tell a society which of these to care about most.
