# Privacy vs. Surveillance — research

## Overview

Governments watch their own people and others in more ways and at greater scale than ever before: intelligence agencies collect hundreds of thousands of foreign targets’ communications and search the results for citizens’ names; police scan crowds with live facial recognition, run photos against databases of driver’s license and mugshot images, and query national networks of license-plate cameras; communications-data powers let officials learn who called or emailed whom without reading the content. This topic covers the argument over how far those state powers should reach into the lives of ordinary citizens and on what terms: when a search of already collected data should need a judge’s warrant, whether facial recognition belongs in public spaces at all, how much oversight is enough, and whether the security these tools buy is worth the privacy they cost. It deliberately excludes the collection and sale of personal data by companies for commercial ends, which belongs to Data Ownership & Surveillance Capitalism, and the fight over whether governments may require access to end-to-end encrypted messages, which belongs to Encryption vs. Lawful Access. Commercial data enters here only where the state buys it or reaches into it, as with license-plate networks and the purchase of location data from brokers.

Why now: two of the democratic world’s largest surveillance programs are being reset at once. In the United States, Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, the law under which the National Security Agency collects foreign targets’ communications from US companies, lapsed at midnight on June 12, 2026, the first time Congress had let it expire since it was enacted in 2008, after the House voted 198 to 218 against a short-term extension (NBC News, June 11, 2026, https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/fisa-spying-power-scheduled-expire-house-fails-extend-leaving-town-rcna349595). Collection continues under court certifications approved on March 17, 2026, which run until March 2027, and the fight is now over whether to renew the law in the lame-duck session, possibly by attaching it to the defense authorization bill, or to wait for the new Congress and votes on a warrant requirement (The American Prospect, November 18, 2026, https://prospect.org/2026/11/18/intelligence-hawks-plot-to-sneak-through-warrantless-spying/). On November 13, 2026 the attorney general, the director of national intelligence and the CIA director wrote to congressional leaders calling Section 702 “the single most indispensable tool we have to keep our homeland and the American people safe” and asking for action by December 9 (Fox News, https://foxnews.com/politics/trumps-spy-chiefs-put-republican-leaders-clock-huge-priority-power-shift-looms-congress). In Britain, on November 4, 2026 the Home Office announced a specific law for police use of facial recognition, with a new independent regulator able to seek a court injunction against a force that repeatedly breaks the rules, alongside £11 million to triple the number of live facial recognition vans available to police in England and Wales (GOV.UK, https://gov.uk/government/news/stronger-laws-for-police-using-facial-recognition-technology).

The debate is live because both sides can point to fresh evidence. Officials point to a 2026 oversight-board report finding that 63 percent of the articles in the President’s Daily Brief in 2025 drew on Section 702, and to London police crediting live facial recognition with 962 arrests in a year. Critics point to Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court findings that the FBI ran hundreds of thousands of improper searches of Section 702 data in 2020 and 2021, including of protesters and of 19,000 donors to a congressional campaign, and later used a filtering tool that let searches go uncounted; to documented wrongful arrests after facial recognition matches; and, since 2025, to federal immigration agents scanning faces in the street with a phone app, which lawsuits allege has been used more than 100,000 times. The participants are intelligence agencies and police forces, interior and justice ministries, legislators who split within both major US parties, oversight bodies such as the US Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board and the UK Investigatory Powers Commissioner, civil-liberties groups such as the Electronic Frontier Foundation, the Brennan Center, the ACLU, Big Brother Watch, Liberty and Amnesty International, the surveillance-technology industry, and city councils deciding whether to keep facial recognition and license-plate cameras.

Context: The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act has two very different modes. Under Section 702, added in 2008, the court approves annual certifications and targeting procedures, and the agencies then choose targets themselves. The comparison shows why Section 702 carries most of the debate: almost all foreign intelligence collection under FISA now happens without an individual court order. For criminal investigations, the separate wiretap system reported 1,735 wiretaps authorized by federal and state judges in 2025 (Administrative Office of the US Courts, Wiretap Report 2025, https://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/reports/statistical-reports/wiretap-reports/wiretap-report-2025).

## Questions

Question: Should searching data the government already holds for a particular citizen require a judge’s warrant?
Tag: value
Poles: Warrant first / Internal approval enough
Why contested: The data was lawfully collected while targeting someone else, often a foreigner abroad, so one side sees a search for an American’s name as a new intrusion that needs a warrant and the other as a routine look through information the government already has. Courts have not settled whether the Fourth Amendment requires a warrant; a federal district court in New York held in 2025 that a Section 702 query was a search requiring one, without excluding the evidence, and the ruling was on appeal as of February 2026 (Stewart Baker, answers to Senate Judiciary Committee questions, https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/download/2026-01-28_qfr-responses_bakerpdf?download=1). In Britain the parallel question is whether each live facial recognition deployment should need outside approval or a senior officer’s authorization under a statutory code.
Case for warrant first: Section 702 was sold as a foreign intelligence tool, but it sweeps in every American who talks to a foreign target, and the FBI then searches that pool for Americans in ordinary investigations. That is a search of a citizen’s private communications, and the Constitution’s answer for such searches is a warrant from a neutral judge, not a supervisor’s sign-off inside the agency that wants to look. The record shows why internal approval fails: the surveillance court found about 278,000 improper FBI queries in 2020 and early 2021, including of protesters and 19,000 campaign donors, and after Congress codified internal controls in 2024 the FBI ran a filtering tool whose searches were not counted, tracked or audited. A warrant rule with exceptions for emergencies, consent and searches meant to protect a victim keeps the program’s foreign value intact, since the FBI holds data on only about 3 percent of Section 702 targets and the NSA’s foreign collection would be untouched. The idea is mainstream: a warrant amendment failed in the House in April 2024 on a 212 to 212 tie (Just the News, https://justthenews.com/government/congress/debate-over-fisa-renewal-bill-warrant-requirement-amendment-underway-house). The Brennan Center puts it simply: self-policing by the FBI and the Justice Department is not working.
Case for internal approval enough: A query is a search of information the government already lawfully holds, like using Ctrl-F on a file in its own cabinet; no court has required a warrant to read evidence already in hand, and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court approves the querying rules each year. Requiring a warrant for each query would mean the FBI could look only after it already had probable cause, which defeats the purpose: queries are most valuable at the start of an investigation, to learn quickly whether a foreign spy, hacker or terrorist has been in contact with an American, often a victim. Stewart Baker, a former NSA general counsel, told the Senate Judiciary Committee in 2026 that a warrant requirement imposes lengthy delays on a process that takes seconds, and that when the government is trying to find out whether a foreign terrorist or spy has recruited an American, delay can be fatal. The abuses critics cite predate the 2021 and 2024 reforms, after which FBI queries of Americans fell by about 87 percent, and the remaining problems are compliance matters for audits and discipline, not proof that only a judge can be trusted.

Question: Do mass surveillance programs stop enough serious crime and terrorism to justify their reach?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Proven lifesaver / Marginal returns
Why contested: Most of the evidence is classified or comes from the agencies that run the programs, and the counterfactual is hard to measure: a plot disrupted with Section 702 information might have been caught another way. Public accounting exists for a few programs and points in different directions.
Case for proven lifesaver: The intelligence community says Section 702 information appeared in 63 percent of the articles in the President’s Daily Brief in 2025, and the oversight board’s staff reports that information from the program has been used to capture foreign terrorists, defend critical infrastructure against cyber and other attacks, and interdict international narcotics trafficking. The oversight board’s 2026 staff report, with access to classified material, judged it one of the country’s most valuable tools. At street level, London’s live facial recognition led to 962 arrests in a year, more than a quarter linked to violence against women and girls, and the Croydon pilot of fixed cameras led to 173 arrests in six months with crime in the area falling. Many of these are people wanted by the courts for serious offenses who would otherwise stay at large. The value of a surveillance tool lies in the cases where nothing happens because the plot was caught, which by nature are hard to show the public.
Case for marginal returns: When independent overseers have been able to measure a program, the results have been thin. The oversight board found in 2014 that it could not identify a single instance in which the bulk phone-records program Snowden exposed had made a concrete difference in the outcome of a counterterrorism investigation, and its successor cost about $100 million and yielded two reports with unique information for the FBI. A 40-year meta-analysis found camera surveillance cuts crime only modestly, mostly property crime in places like car parks. Agency claims about Section 702 are untested because the examples are chosen by the agencies, the sources are secret and the question is never whether the same information could have been obtained with a targeted, court-approved order. Arrest counts from facial recognition measure activity, not crime prevented: Big Brother Watch reports that in deployments outside London, hundreds of thousands of faces were scanned with few or no arrests. Without independent evaluation, claims of effectiveness are an assertion, and the burden of proof belongs to those who want to watch everyone.

Question: Can facial recognition be made accurate and fair enough for police to rely on?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Accurate enough now / Errors are unacceptable
Why contested: Accuracy varies enormously by algorithm, image quality, threshold and how officers use a match. Laboratory tests show the best systems performing far better than early ones, while field cases show people wrongly arrested, most of them Black.
Case for accurate enough now: Modern algorithms tested by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology are far more accurate than the systems that produced early headlines, and NIST found that the most accurate systems were also the most equitable. Police can choose those systems and set strict thresholds: the Met’s live system produced 10 false alerts out of more than 3.1 million faces scanned in a year, none leading to arrest, and every alert is checked by an officer before anyone is stopped. The documented wrongful arrests in the United States came from departments that treated a match as proof and skipped basic investigation, a failure of practice that rules can fix, such as Detroit’s June 2024 settlement with Robert Williams, which bars its police from arresting anyone based only on a facial recognition match. Human eyewitnesses, the traditional alternative, misidentify suspects far more often and with documented racial bias of their own.
Case for errors are unacceptable: NIST’s 2019 study found false-positive rates for some demographic groups 10 to 100 times higher than for others across the algorithms tested, and the systems police actually buy are not always the best performers. Even a tiny error rate becomes many wrongly stopped people when millions of faces are scanned, and in the Met’s own data 8 of the 10 people wrongly flagged in 2024 to 2025 were Black. Officers given a computer match tend to trust it, as the Washington Post found in its review of at least eight wrongful arrests in which police ignored alibis and contradictory evidence. Accuracy is also not the main objection: a perfectly accurate system that identifies everyone in a crowd is a more powerful tool of control, not a safer one. The Electronic Frontier Foundation argues that even if the disparate error rates were addressed, face surveillance must be stopped.

Question: Do people have a right to be anonymous in public spaces?
Tag: value
Poles: Public is public / Anonymity in crowds
Why contested: The law in most democracies has long held that what a person shows in public is not private, but that rule was made when following someone required an officer’s time. Facial recognition and networked cameras make it possible to identify everyone, everywhere, retroactively, which some see as a difference of degree and others as a difference of kind.
Case for public is public: Anyone walking down a street can be seen, photographed and recognized by a police officer, a neighbor or a stranger; a camera that recognizes a wanted person does what an officer with a good memory and a stack of wanted posters has always done. Live facial recognition as used in Britain compares faces to a watchlist and, by the Met’s account, automatically deletes the biometric data of everyone who does not match [unverified: Met policy page returned 403], so the innocent passer-by leaves no record. Most of the public accepts this: in a 2025 Home Office survey, 88 percent said police use of live facial recognition to find suspects was acceptable at least sometimes. A right to be unidentifiable in public has never existed and would protect mainly those who are being sought.
Case for anonymity in crowds: Being seen is not the same as being identified, logged and tracked. In practice, people have always moved through cities in relative anonymity because no one could recognize and record everyone; that practical obscurity is what lets people attend a protest, a clinic, a mosque or a meeting without the state knowing. In 2018 the US Supreme Court recognized in Carpenter v. United States that compiling a person’s movements over time from cell-phone location records is a search requiring a warrant, even though each movement happened in public. Facial recognition and license-plate networks do the same thing with faces and cars. When everyone can be identified retroactively, the knowledge that one might be changes behavior; after the Snowden disclosures, views of terrorism-related Wikipedia articles fell by close to 30 percent, according to a 2016 study by Jon Penney.

Question: Can courts, legislatures and watchdogs keep surveillance powers within their intended limits?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Oversight works / Powers always creep
Why contested: Every major surveillance power in the US and UK now has layers of oversight, from secret courts to inspectors general to commissioners, and every one has a record of violations found by that same oversight. Whether that record shows the system working or failing depends on what one expected of it.
Case for oversight works: The abuses the public knows about were found and disclosed by the oversight system itself: the surveillance court’s opinions on improper FBI queries, the Justice Department’s audits, the inspector general’s reports and the oversight board’s reviews. Each discovery led to change, from the end of bulk phone-records collection in 2015 to the 2024 query reforms, after which FBI queries of Americans fell sharply. In Britain, the Investigatory Powers Commissioner reviews every intrusive warrant with judicial commissioners and publishes error counts, and the government is now creating a facial recognition regulator with power to seek injunctions. No system of human institutions is free of error; what matters is whether errors are caught and corrected, and these are.
Case for powers always creep: The pattern across decades is that surveillance powers are created for a narrow purpose, expand to others, and are reined in only after leaks or scandals. Bulk phone-records collection ran secretly for years until Snowden revealed it; Section 702, created to target foreigners abroad, is now routinely searched by the FBI for Americans; in Britain, surveillance powers under the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 were used by local councils to investigate matters such as school-catchment applications and dog fouling. Oversight is weakened when it becomes inconvenient: the US oversight board has had no quorum since the president removed its Democratic members in January 2025, and the surveillance court’s March 2026 opinion on new querying violations had not been declassified by its statutory deadline. Immigration agents are now using a facial recognition app on people in the street, including citizens, with no specific law authorizing it. Powers built for one government will be inherited by the next, whoever that is, and the only reliable limit is not to build what cannot be safely inherited.

Question: Should governments be able to buy data about citizens that they would otherwise need a warrant to obtain?
Tag: value
Poles: Open market data / Warrant still needed
Why contested: Location histories, license-plate reads and app data are sold commercially by data brokers, and US agencies including the FBI, the Defense Intelligence Agency and immigration authorities have bought such data. The law has not settled whether buying what anyone can buy is a search.
Case for open market data: Information that companies sell openly to marketers, insurers and anyone else is not private in any meaningful sense, and it would be perverse to let every advertiser buy it while barring the police who are investigating a kidnapping or a drug cartel. Buying data is not compelling it: no one is forced to hand anything over, and agencies use it under internal legal review. Commercial data often fills gaps in fast-moving investigations, and requiring a warrant for any purchase would put the government at a disadvantage against criminals and foreign intelligence services who face no such limit. The answer to concerns about data brokers is consumer-privacy law that limits what is collected in the first place, applied to everyone.
Case for warrant still needed: In Carpenter, the Supreme Court held that the government needs a warrant to obtain a person’s historical cell-phone location records from a phone company. Buying the same kind of record from a broker, often derived from apps on the same phone, lets agencies evade that ruling with a credit card. The Government Surveillance Reform Act introduced in March 2026 by Senators Ron Wyden and Mike Lee would ban the federal government from buying Americans’ data from brokers without a warrant, and the House passed a similar Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act on April 17, 2024, by 219 to 199 (House Clerk, roll call 136, https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2024/roll136). Without such a rule, every limit Congress places on compelled surveillance can be bypassed by purchase, and the growing networks of license-plate cameras run by private vendors show how quickly local data can end up searchable by federal agencies.

Question: Should foreigners abroad get the same privacy protections as citizens?
Tag: value
Poles: Citizens first / Universal right
Why contested: Section 702 may only target non-US persons abroad, and US constitutional protections do not generally extend to them, but international human-rights law recognizes privacy as a right of every person, and European courts have twice struck down EU-US data transfer agreements partly because of US surveillance.
Case for citizens first: Spying on foreign governments, terrorists and criminals abroad is what intelligence agencies exist to do, and every major country does it. A government’s constitutional duties run to its own people; extending warrant requirements to foreigners abroad would make foreign intelligence collection impossible and would bind democracies while their adversaries remain free. The privacy debate rightly focuses on what happens to Americans, Britons and Europeans swept up in foreign collection, which is where limits such as minimization rules and query restrictions apply.
Case for universal right: Privacy is a human right recognized in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and a person’s emails do not deserve less protection because of their passport. In practice the line between foreign and domestic is porous, since every American who talks to a foreigner is collected along with them, and programs justified as foreign-only become tools for domestic searches. Europe’s highest court invalidated the Privacy Shield data framework in 2020 because US surveillance lacked proportionality and redress for Europeans, and the current framework depends on a 2022 executive order that a president can revoke. Treating foreigners’ privacy as worthless also lowers the standard other governments apply to democracies’ own citizens.

Question: Is it safe to build surveillance capacity that a future government could misuse?
Tag: value
Poles: Build with safeguards / Do not build it
Why contested: The same tools serve very different purposes depending on who controls them, and control changes with elections. In the United States, partisan positions on Section 702 and facial recognition have reversed more than once as the party in power changed.
Case for build with safeguards: Threats from terrorism, foreign espionage, cyberattacks and organized crime are real now, and refusing to build effective tools because a hypothetical future government might abuse them leaves people exposed to present dangers. Democracies have courts, elections, a free press and oversight bodies precisely so that powerful tools can be held in check, and the record shows abuses being exposed and corrected. A government determined to abuse power would build its own tools anyway; the better protection is strong institutions, transparency and laws that bind whoever holds office.
Case for do not build it: Every surveillance system is inherited by the next government, whatever its intentions. Infrastructure built for counterterrorism or finding wanted criminals can be turned against journalists, protesters, political opponents or minorities in a single administrative decision, and once cameras, databases and search tools exist the barrier to misuse is a policy, not a capability. US officials of both parties have alternated between defending and denouncing the same powers as their side entered and left office, which itself shows the danger. China’s networks of cameras and facial recognition, used to monitor Uyghurs in Xinjiang, show where the capacity can lead when institutions fail. The safest safeguard is a capacity that does not exist.

## Positions

Note on shape: the debate runs on two tracks that share one structure. In intelligence surveillance, chiefly Section 702 in the US and bulk powers under the UK Investigatory Powers Act, the live fork is whether searches of collected data about citizens need a warrant. In policing technology, chiefly facial recognition and license-plate networks, the fork is whether the tools should be used under a statutory code, used only with case-by-case outside approval, or banned. The three camps below hold across both tracks. No camp that rejects the question as posed met the bar of two independent adherents including an institution: the closest is the “transparent society” view of the writer David Brin, who argues that since surveillance cannot be stopped, the answer is to make it reciprocal so that citizens can watch the watchers (https://davidbrin.com/transparency.html); it has individual followers but no institutional voice in the current fights, and its practical demands, such as body cameras and public logs, have been absorbed into the first camp’s accountability agenda.

Camp: Allow surveillance only with warrants and specific laws
Adherents:
- Brennan Center for Justice (institution), April 12, 2026: “Self-policing by the FBI and DOJ isn’t working,” and “Congress should require agents to get a warrant or FISA Title I order (a type of warrant issued by the FISA Court in foreign intelligence investigations) before accessing Americans’ private communications that have been “incidentally” collected under Section 702”: https://www.brennancenter.org/media/15482/download/bcj-one-pager-on-new-fisc-opinion.pdf?inline=1
- Senator Mike Lee (Republican of Utah), introducing the Government Surveillance Reform Act with Senator Ron Wyden (Democrat of Oregon) on March 12, 2026: “It is imperative that Congress enact real reforms to protect our civil liberties, including warrant requirements, and statutory penalties for privacy violations, in exchange for reauthorizing Section 702”: https://www.lee.senate.gov/2026/3/lee-introduces-bipartisan-government-surveillance-reform-act
- The European Union (institution), whose Artificial Intelligence Act, in force for this provision since February 2, 2025, bans police use of real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces except for three narrow purposes, and requires prior authorization by a judicial authority or an independent administrative authority whose decision is binding for each use: https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/
- Ada Lovelace Institute (institution), whose May 2025 report recommended “establishing an independent regulatory body for biometrics” and a risk-based legal framework for police and private use, without calling for a ban (reported by Biometric Update): https://www.biometricupdate.com/202505/ada-lovelace-institute-questions-legality-of-facial-recognition-in-uk
- Also in this camp: the Center for Democracy & Technology, which supports warrant requirements and notice to defendants for police facial recognition and a ban only on untargeted scanning (https://cdt.org/2025-annual-report-curbing-facial-recognition/); the Electronic Frontier Foundation on Section 702, which says the program should require a warrant before the FBI can look at Americans’ communications or else be allowed to expire (https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/06/victory-702-has-expired); EPIC; Liberty in the UK, which welcomed a dedicated facial recognition law; Representatives Warren Davidson, Zoe Lofgren, Andy Biggs and Pramila Jayapal; and the majority of the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board in its 2023 report, which recommended court approval for most FBI US person queries.
Variation inside the camp: Members agree that surveillance of citizens needs a specific law and individual outside approval, but differ on how far that reaches. Some want a full probable-cause warrant or FISA Title I order before any agency reads an American’s Section 702 communications; others would exempt metadata or limit the rule to the FBI. A 2026 proposal by Representative Jim Himes, who otherwise belongs to the next camp, would have the surveillance court approve FBI access to query results that are “reasonably likely to include foreign intelligence information,” which the Brennan Center called a watered-down warrant that is the worst of both worlds (https://www.brennancenter.org/media/15492/download/bcj-one-pager-on-watered-down-warrant-4-14-26.pdf?inline=1). On facial recognition, some accept live scanning against a watchlist under a statute and prior approval, as the EU does for serious crimes; others accept only retrospective searches tied to a specific investigation and would ban live crowd scanning. Many also want the data broker loophole closed so agencies cannot buy what they would need a warrant to compel, and some, through the ACLU’s Community Control Over Police Surveillance model, want local councils to approve each new police surveillance technology after public hearings.
Case: Nobody in this camp wants to blind the intelligence agencies or take useful tools from the police. We want the rule that has governed searches for two centuries to apply to the tools of this one: if the government wants to read a citizen’s messages, track her movements or identify her in a crowd, it should show a neutral judge a good reason first, under a law that the people’s representatives actually debated. Section 702 was enacted to collect foreign intelligence from foreigners; using it as a searchable archive of Americans’ communications, without a warrant, turns a foreign program into a domestic one by the back door. Facial recognition, live or retrospective, is spreading through policing on the authority of general common-law powers and vendor contracts, not specific statutes. Internal approvals have been tried and have failed repeatedly, from the 278,000 improper queries the surveillance court found to the uncounted searches through filtering tools after the 2024 reforms. A warrant is not a ban: it is the ordinary price of a search in a free country, and its emergency exceptions handle the urgent cases officials worry about.
Best evidence: The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court’s April 2022 opinion found about 278,000 improper FBI queries of Section 702 data in 2020 and early 2021, including of protesters and 19,000 campaign donors (Lawfare, https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/unsealed-surveillance-court-document-reveals-702-misuse). A March 2025 opinion found the FBI using a filtering tool whose queries were not counted, tracked or audited despite the 2024 reforms, and a March 2026 opinion reportedly found a similar tool still in use, by the NSA and CIA as well (Brennan Center, April 2026). The oversight board concluded in 2014 that it had “not identified a single instance involving a threat to the United States in which the program made a concrete difference in the outcome of a counterterrorism investigation” for bulk phone-records collection (PCLOB, https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/download/privacy-and-civil-liberties-oversight-board-report?download=1). In the UK, the Court of Appeal ruled South Wales Police’s use of live facial recognition unlawful in 2020 for lack of clear rules. A warrant amendment failed in the House by a 212 to 212 tie in 2024, and the House passed a ban on warrantless data purchases 219 to 199 the same week, showing support across both parties.
Answer to objections: To those who say a warrant requirement would cripple the program: the NSA’s foreign targeting would be untouched, the FBI holds data on only about 3 percent of Section 702 targets, and the bills include exceptions for emergencies, consent, cybersecurity signatures and searches to protect a victim; and the FBI already runs about 50,000 fewer US person queries a year than it did in 2023. The Brennan Center cites polling in which 76 percent of Americans favor a warrant requirement. The claim that a query is just Ctrl-F on lawfully held data ignores that the data was collected without a warrant on the promise that Americans were not the targets. To those who would ban facial recognition and end programs outright: some uses, such as finding a missing child or identifying a suspect in a murder from video, are valuable and can be confined by law and judicial approval, and a ban that later collapses leaves no rules at all, as when Virginia replaced its ban on local police use with what Biometric Update called broad guidelines in 2022 (https://www.biometricupdate.com/202207/facial-recognition-moratoriums-bans-and-un-bans-wash-across-the-united-states).

Camp: Use surveillance tools widely under institutional oversight
Adherents:
- US Attorney General Todd Blanche, Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton and CIA Director John Ratcliffe (institutions), in a letter to congressional leaders reported on November 13, 2026, calling Section 702 “the single most indispensable tool we have to keep our homeland and the American people safe” and warning that “No other authority can replace it”: https://foxnews.com/politics/trumps-spy-chiefs-put-republican-leaders-clock-huge-priority-power-shift-looms-congress
- Stewart Baker, former general counsel of the National Security Agency, in answers to the Senate Judiciary Committee dated February 20, 2026: “A warrant requirement is not a reform intended to prevent abuses. It’s an ideological project, sweeping and formless”: https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/download/2026-01-28_qfr-responses_bakerpdf?download=1
- UK Home Office (institution), announcing a law and £11 million to triple live facial recognition vans on November 4, 2026; Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood: “Facial recognition technology has been the biggest breakthrough for catching criminals since DNA matching”: https://gov.uk/government/news/stronger-laws-for-police-using-facial-recognition-technology
- Metropolitan Police (institution); Lindsey Chiswick, its lead for live facial recognition, on its 2025 annual report: “This is a powerful and game-changing tool”: https://www.theregister.com/2025/11/03/metropolitan_police_hails_facial_recognition/
- Also in this camp: Representative Jim Himes, ranking Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, who calls Section 702 “our country’s most important and irreplaceable foreign intelligence collection tool” (https://himes.house.gov/2026/04/29/himes-statement-on-house-passage-of-fisa-702-reauthorization/) while supporting some added judicial review of FBI queries; Senators John Cornyn, Mike Rounds and Mark Warner; FBI leadership across administrations, including former Director Christopher Wray, who told the Senate in 2023 that a warrant requirement would amount to a de facto ban on US person queries (Roll Call, https://rollcall.com/2023/10/31/surveillance-authority-change-could-harm-ability-to-stop-attacks-fbi-director-warns/); the Security Industry Association, whose chief executive said in 2020 of calls to end police and private use of facial recognition, “We respectfully but firmly disagree” (https://www.securityindustry.org/2020/08/18/security-industry-association-releases-policy-principles-to-guide-the-use-of-facial-recognition-technology/); and police leaders in many US cities that have restored facial recognition after earlier limits.
Variation inside the camp: Members agree that these tools should be widely available and that oversight should come from inside the system, through internal approvals, audits, inspectors general, program-level court review, commissioners or a regulator, rather than a warrant for each use. They differ on how much new law is needed. The UK government wants a dedicated statute, independent bias testing and a regulator with injunction powers, to give police “greater confidence” to expand use; US intelligence officials want Section 702 renewed with the 2024 reforms and no more, and some would welcome a longer reauthorization. They differ too on the Trump administration: Democrats such as Himes and Warner back renewal while calling for oversight of how the current administration uses the powers, and Himes has proposed limited court review of FBI access to query results that the administration has not embraced. On facial recognition in immigration enforcement, members split along party lines.
Case: The threats these tools address are not hypothetical: foreign intelligence services recruiting Americans, ransomware gangs attacking hospitals, cartels moving fentanyl, terrorists planning attacks, violent offenders who have skipped bail. Section 702 is the source for a majority of the President’s Daily Brief, and no other authority can replace it at the speed threats move. A query of data the government already lawfully holds is not a new search; requiring a warrant for it would mean the FBI could only check whether a foreign spy had contacted an American after it had already proven probable cause, which is backwards. Live facial recognition finds people already wanted by the courts, without stopping or searching anyone else, and the Met’s system produced 10 false alerts out of more than three million faces in a year. The right safeguards are the ones that now exist and work: compliance rates above 99.8 percent, attorney approvals, full audits of FBI queries, independent bias testing, watchlists limited to wanted people and a regulator with teeth. The abuses critics cite are old and were found by this very oversight system, which is how a self-correcting democracy should work.
Best evidence: The oversight board’s 2026 staff report found that 63 percent of President’s Daily Brief articles in 2025 contained Section 702 information, that targeting compliance stayed above 99.8 percent and that FBI queries of Americans fell about 87 percent from 2023 to 2025 after the reforms (PCLOB, April 2, 2026). The Met’s live facial recognition led to 962 arrests in a year, among them 549 people wanted by the courts, with no arrest from any false alert (The Register, November 2025), and fixed cameras in Croydon led to 173 arrests in six months alongside a 10.5 percent fall in overall crime there (GOV.UK, November 2026). In a 2025 Home Office survey, 88 percent of adults in England and Wales found live facial recognition to locate suspects acceptable at least sometimes, and 59 percent always or usually. NIST’s testing shows the most accurate algorithms are also the most equitable, so careful procurement addresses bias.
Answer to objections: To those who demand a warrant for every search: the Constitution requires a warrant to collect communications, not to search information already lawfully collected; appeals courts have upheld collection under Section 702, and the one trial court to require a warrant for a query left the evidence in and is under appeal; a warrant rule would slow the FBI exactly when speed matters, which is why administrations of both parties have called it a red line. Oversight did catch the old abuses, and the steep fall in queries shows the fixes work. To those who would ban facial recognition: a ban protects no one’s privacy that a statutory code with an independent regulator would not, and it leaves wanted offenders on the street; the documented wrongful arrests came from departments ignoring their own rule that a match is only a lead, and the answer is to enforce that rule. Where bans have been tried, some have been rolled back, as in Virginia in 2022.

Camp: Ban facial recognition and end mass surveillance
Adherents:
- Electronic Frontier Foundation (institution): “Regardless of your race or gender—and even if these disparate error rates were addressed—face surveillance must be stopped to protect our communities”: https://www.eff.org/aboutface
- Big Brother Watch (institution), UK, which says it is “fighting to urgently stop facial recognition surveillance from being used in our public spaces” and is backing a legal challenge to the Met’s use by Shaun Thompson, a Black anti-knife-crime volunteer wrongly flagged in 2024: https://bigbrotherwatch.org.uk/campaigns/stop-facial-recognition/
- Amnesty International (institution), “calling for a total ban on the use, development, production, and sale, of facial recognition technology for mass surveillance purposes”: https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2021/01/ban-dangerous-facial-recognition-technology-that-amplifies-racist-policing/
- Senator Ed Markey (Democrat of Massachusetts), introducing the ICE Out of Our Faces Act with Representative Pramila Jayapal and Senators Jeff Merkley and Ron Wyden on February 5, 2026, to bar ICE and CBP from using facial recognition and delete what they have collected: “ICE and CBP agents are using facial recognition technologies to track, target, and surveil individuals across the country”: https://jayapal.house.gov/2026/02/05/markey-jayapal-merkley-wyden-introduce-bill-to-ban-ice-and-cbp-use-of-facial-recognition-technology-amid-trumps-rapidly-growing-surveillance-state/
- Also in this camp: Fight for the Future, which endorsed the ICE bill and campaigns for a federal ban; European Digital Rights and the Reclaim Your Face coalition, which campaigned for a full EU ban on biometric mass surveillance and regard the AI Act’s exceptions as loopholes; London Assembly Member Zoë Garbett, whose February 2026 report urged a pause in the Met’s expansion (https://www.london.gov.uk/new-report-zoe-garbett-am-urges-immediate-pause-mets-rapid-expansion-live-facial-recognition); the local campaigns, including DeFlock, that have persuaded dozens of US cities to cancel license-plate camera contracts since 2025; and the members of Congress who voted against any 702 extension without a warrant rule.
Variation inside the camp: All members hold that some surveillance technologies are too dangerous to regulate and should not exist in government hands. They differ on scope. Most target facial recognition, especially live scanning of public spaces, and biometric mass surveillance generally; some would also ban police use of retrospective facial recognition, license-plate reader networks and predictive policing. On intelligence powers, some want Section 702 allowed to expire unless rebuilt around warrants, while others, such as EFF, frame their demand as a warrant rule or expiry. Some seek permanent statutory bans, others moratoriums until strict laws exist, and many in the US now focus on immigration enforcement, where the risk is most immediate.
Case: Some tools cannot be made safe by rules, because their danger lies in what they are for. Facial recognition in public space turns every camera into a checkpoint and every crowd into a lineup; it ends the practical anonymity that lets people protest, worship, seek medical care or meet a journalist without the state knowing. Accuracy is not the core problem: a perfectly accurate system is a more efficient instrument of control. Rules and regulators are promises made by today’s government and kept, or not, by tomorrow’s, and the infrastructure stays. The United States now shows what that means: immigration agents scanning faces in the street with a phone app that, according to a federal lawsuit, people cannot decline and whose scans, citizens’ included, are kept for 15 years (Biometric Update, January 13, 2026, https://www.biometricupdate.com/202601/lawsuit-casts-new-light-on-ice-cbps-expanding-biometric-visual-surveillance-dragnet); license-plate data from local cameras reaching federal agencies that cities never agreed to share with; an oversight board left without a quorum. Bulk and programmatic surveillance follow the same pattern: built for one purpose, expanded to others, exposed by leaks, trimmed, and expanded again. The only safeguard that does not depend on who holds power is a capability that does not exist.
Best evidence: In the Met’s 2025 report, 8 of the 10 people wrongly flagged by live facial recognition were Black (The Register, November 2025). NIST found false-positive rates for some demographic groups 10 to 100 times higher than for others across many algorithms (NIST, 2019), and a Washington Post investigation documented at least eight wrongful US arrests after matches, in nearly all of which police ignored contradicting evidence (Fortune, January 2025). Big Brother Watch reports deployments outside London that scanned hundreds of thousands of faces with few or no arrests, such as about 400,000 in Northamptonshire with no arrests. A lawsuit alleges immigration agents used the Mobile Fortify app more than 100,000 times by January 2026 (The Register, February 2026), and the GAO found in 2021 that only one of 14 federal agencies using facial recognition in criminal investigations knew which non-federal systems its staff used. The 2014 oversight board found no instance in which bulk phone-records collection made a concrete difference to a counterterrorism investigation, and Jon Penney’s 2016 study recorded a drop of close to 30 percent in views of terrorism-related Wikipedia articles after the Snowden disclosures, evidence of a chilling effect on lawful activity.
Answer to objections: To those who would regulate rather than ban: regulation of facial recognition has so far meant legitimizing expansion, as Britain’s plan to triple its vans alongside its new law shows, and the EU AI Act’s exceptions for serious crime, terrorism and missing persons are broad enough to justify routine use. Warrants help for targeted searches but do nothing about cameras that scan everyone, which is what live facial recognition does. To those who say the tools save lives: arrest counts are not evidence of crime prevented, police found wanted people before these systems existed, and a democracy should not trade the anonymity of millions for a modest gain in efficiency. Bans are workable; San Francisco, Boston and other cities have lived with them for years, and the EU already prohibits most real-time police use.

## Crux

When the state gains the power to search its citizens’ communications, movements and faces at scale, do the oversight systems now in place, from internal approvals and audits to secret courts and regulators, actually keep that power confined to serious threats across changes of government, or does only prior approval by a judge for each use, or the absence of the capability altogether, reliably do so; and how much security is worth the risk that remains?

What evidence could settle: How much the programs actually contribute, measured independently rather than by the agencies that run them: how many terrorism, espionage, cyber and violent-crime cases depended on Section 702 queries of Americans or on facial recognition, and whether the same result was available through a targeted court order or ordinary investigation. Whether the post-2024 controls work, which needs complete counts of FBI, NSA and CIA queries including those run through filtering tools, published audit results and the surveillance court’s 2025 and 2026 opinions declassified. How often warrant requirements, where they exist, have slowed or stopped urgent investigations, which states and countries with such rules could show. Real-world error rates and demographic effects of the facial recognition systems police actually use, at the thresholds they actually set, tested by independent bodies. Whether live facial recognition reduces crime in an area rather than moving it, and whether being scanned changes lawful behavior such as protest attendance, both of which controlled studies could measure.

What stays a value choice: How much privacy everyone should give up to catch a small number of dangerous people, when those who bear the cost and those who benefit are not the same. Whether being identified in a public place by a machine is the same as being seen by an officer, or a different kind of intrusion. Whether a search of information the government already holds is a new search that needs a judge, which courts may answer for the law but not for what citizens should want. How far to trust institutions, and whether to design rules for the government in office or for the worst government that might inherit the tools. Whether foreigners’ privacy counts for less than citizens’.
