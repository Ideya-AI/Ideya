# Trade & Industrial Policy — research

## Overview

Trade and industrial policy is the question of how freely goods, money and production should move across a country’s borders, and how much its government should shape where things are made. At one end is free trade: low tariffs, common rules enforced through the World Trade Organization (WTO), and production located wherever it is cheapest and best. At the other is economic nationalism: tariffs to shield or rebuild domestic industry, pressure on companies to bring production home (reshoring), a preference for local production, and state subsidies for industries a government judges strategic, such as semiconductors, steel, shipbuilding and clean energy. Between them sit hybrids that keep trade open with allies while subsidizing chosen sectors and fencing off a few technologies from rivals. This topic covers tariffs, trade agreements and the multilateral trading system, reshoring and “friend-shoring,” localism, and subsidies for strategic industries, in the United States and abroad. It deliberately leaves to their own pages the sourcing of critical minerals (the critical minerals page), the distribution of income and the bargaining power of workers except where trade drives them (the economic inequality and labor power pages), the wider contest between a liberal international order and a multipolar one, and immigration, which is also a question of openness but a separate debate.

Why now: on 30 September 2026 a three-judge panel of the US Court of International Trade heard the lead challenge, brought by two small importers, to the tariffs of 10 or 12.5 percent that the US Trade Representative imposed from 24 July 2026 on 60 economies supplying about 99.4 percent of US imports, on the ground that they fail to ban imports made with forced labor (USTR, 23 July 2026, https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/july/ustr-takes-action-forced-labor-section-301-investigations; Liberty Justice Center, https://libertyjusticecenter.org/mediaalert/court-of-international-trade-to-hear-challenge-to-sweeping-section-301-tariffs/). A parallel suit by Democratic-led states, led by Oregon, is stayed while that sample case is decided (Oregon Department of Justice, https://www.doj.state.or.us/oregon-department-of-justice/federal-oversight/federal-litigation-tracker/tariffs-oregon-v-trump-court-of-international-trade-2/). The forced-labor duties replaced a 10 percent global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which ran from 24 February to 24 July 2026 and which the same court had ruled unlawful on 7 May in a 2 to 1 decision that was stayed on appeal (Skadden, May 2026, https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/us-trade-court-strikes-down-section-122-tariffs). That tariff in turn replaced the duties the Supreme Court struck down on 20 February 2026, when it held 6 to 3 in Learning Resources v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the President to impose tariffs (slip opinion, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1287_4gcj.pdf). In the same weeks the administration put 50 percent tariffs on some Canadian goods from 22 August, the first time a President has cited Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 to impose tariffs, banned imports of others from 29 September, and declined to renew the US-Mexico-Canada Agreement in its current form (Congressional Research Service, 14 September 2026, https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-14_R49349_4ab4516f3ec92e51d16778d14a72d3403c0add08.html); and at a 23 to 25 September state visit, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping launched a US-China “Board of Trade” without resolving disputes over Chinese export controls or industrial overcapacity (Brookings, 29 September 2026, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-did-the-trump-xi-summit-actually-accomplish/). Multilateral rule-making has stalled alongside: the WTO’s ministerial conference in Yaoundé in March 2026 ended without agreement on a reform declaration, and the moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions, in place since 1998, lapsed (European Commission, 30 March 2026, https://policy.trade.ec.europa.eu/news/outcome-14th-wto-ministerial-conference-2026-03-30_en).

The debate is live because the postwar bargain of falling tariffs and expanding trade is being renegotiated on several fronts at once. The United States has raised its average statutory tariff rate from 2.4 percent at the start of 2025 to 11.0 percent in August 2026, by the Yale Budget Lab’s estimates (the Lab put a comparable 11.8 percent rate in April 2026 at the highest since the early 1940s, setting aside 2025 itself), and is rebuilding its tariff wall statute by statute after court defeats; China ran a goods trade surplus of about $1.19 trillion in 2025, by its customs administration’s count, while its share of US goods imports fell to about 9 percent by the end of the year (Chad Bown, Peterson Institute for International Economics, 16 March 2026); and governments from Washington to Brussels to Beijing have returned to industrial subsidies on a scale not seen in decades. The participants include the Trump administration and the national-conservative movement, which see tariffs as the tool to reindustrialize; most academic economists, free-market think tanks, importers and many farm and retail groups, who see tariffs as a tax on Americans; the center-left and much of the European policy establishment, which favor targeted subsidies and alliances over broad tariffs; labor unions, which have backed both Democratic industrial policy and some of Trump’s tariffs; developing-country governments and development economists, who want room to build their own industries under rules written by richer countries; and localist and food-sovereignty movements that reject the goal of maximizing trade at all.

The administration has argued that foreign producers and the strength of the dollar share the burden, and that the revenue is a fair charge for access to the American market; a weaker dollar or lower foreign wages could shift some burden abroad over time in ways shipment prices do not capture.

Note: The United States had about 12.65 million manufacturing jobs in September 2026, about 7 million fewer than at the 1979 peak and roughly the same number as when 2025’s tariffs began.
Context: Manufacturing now accounts for about 8 percent of US payroll jobs (12.65 million of 159.0 million total nonfarm jobs in September 2026, both BLS preliminary). The steepest loss came in one decade: about 5.9 million jobs between mid-2000 and early 2010, spanning China’s entry into the WTO in 2001 and the Great Recession, after which employment recovered by about 1.3 million to 2019. Since January 2025 the count has been roughly flat, falling to 12.58 million in December 2025 before edging back up; both sides of the tariff debate cite this, one as evidence that tariffs have not yet delivered jobs, the other that factories take years to build. Output and jobs tell different stories: manufacturing output has grown far faster than employment since 1979 because of automation and productivity gains, which is why economists who stress technology and those who stress trade read the same job chart differently. Announcements run ahead of hiring: the Reshoring Initiative, a group that campaigns for reshoring, counted about 244,000 reshoring and foreign-investment manufacturing jobs announced in 2024 (https://reshorenow.org/content/pdf/2024_1Q2025_RI_DATA_Report.pdf), a count of plans rather than filled jobs.

Label: US jobs lost to Chinese import competition, 1999 to 2011
Value: 2.0 to 2.4
Unit: million jobs (central estimates, whole economy)
Viz: bar
Data year: 1999 to 2011
Source label: Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Price, “Import Competition and the Great US Employment Sag of the 2000s,” Journal of Labor Economics (2016)
Source URL: https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/import%20competition%20unemployment%20sag%202016.pdf
Note: Economists estimate that rising imports from China cost the United States 2.0 to 2.4 million jobs between 1999 and 2011, across manufacturing and the rest of the economy.
Context: This study and its companions (often called “the China shock” literature) changed the economics profession’s view of trade’s costs. The authors estimate that the direct effect within manufacturing was about 560,000 jobs, roughly 10 percent of the 5.8 million manufacturing jobs lost over the period; counting supply-chain links raises the manufacturing loss to 985,000, and adding effects on other sectors brings the economy-wide total to about 2.0 to 2.4 million. The losses were concentrated in particular places, furniture, textile and electronics towns in the South and Midwest, and later research by Autor, Dorn and Hanson found that lower employment-to-population ratios and incomes in the most exposed commuting zones persisted through 2019 (“On the Persistence of the China Shock,” Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, fall 2021, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/on-the-persistence-of-the-china-shock/). Readers draw different lessons: those who favor protection cite the study as proof that trade, not only automation, emptied factory towns; those who favor open trade note that even these estimates attribute most of the manufacturing decline to other causes, that the shock was a one-time event as China joined the world economy, and that the failure was in adjustment policy rather than trade itself. The journal version is at https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/682384.

Context: Bown calculates that real US imports from China fell 28 percent in 2025 alone and ended 40 percent below their level on the eve of the first trade war in 2018, while real imports from all other countries rose more than 9 percent in 2025. Bown also notes that some dependencies have not moved: twice in 2025 Chinese export restrictions on rare earth magnets and certain chips nearly halted US auto production.

## Questions

Question: Can tariffs rebuild domestic manufacturing at a cost worth paying?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Tariffs rebuild industry / Tariffs cost more than they build
Why contested: The evidence so far comes from narrow or short-lived tariffs (steel and washing machines in 2018, the China tariffs of 2018 and 2019) and from the first eighteen months of a broad regime that courts keep striking down and the administration keeps rebuilding. Studies of the earlier episodes found high consumer costs and net job losses, but supporters say a permanent, broad and predictable tariff is a different policy that has never been tested in modern conditions, and factories take years to build.
Case for “Tariffs rebuild industry”: Every country that industrialized, including the United States in the nineteenth century, did it behind tariff walls. A tariff changes the arithmetic of where to build: if selling into the world’s largest consumer market requires producing there, companies will produce there, as foreign automakers and appliance makers did after earlier US trade actions. The costs economists measure are front-loaded and visible, while the benefits, a deeper supplier base, skilled workers, production know-how and the innovation that grows near factories, accrue over decades and do not show up in a two-year study. Uncertainty, not tariffs, has held back investment; make the tariff permanent and predictable and the investment follows.
Case for “Tariffs cost more than they build”: The record is consistent. The 2018 washing machine tariffs cost buyers over $815,000 a year per job reported; the 2018 and 2019 tariffs were associated with fewer manufacturing jobs, because protection for some firms raised input costs for others and invited retaliation against exporters; and 96 percent of the 2025 tariffs was passed through to US buyers. US manufacturing employment has been flat since tariffs began and factory construction has fallen. Most imports are inputs to American production, so a broad tariff taxes the very manufacturers it is meant to help, and the money that firms must spend on tariffs is money not spent on new plants.

Question: Was trade or technology the main reason factory jobs disappeared?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Trade drove the losses / Automation drove the losses
Why contested: US manufacturing output kept rising for decades while employment fell, which points to productivity; yet the sharpest job losses came in the 2000s, exactly when imports from China surged, and they were concentrated in the places most exposed to Chinese competition. The answer decides whether trade policy can bring the jobs back.
Case for “Trade drove the losses”: The China shock research found that rising Chinese imports cost the United States 2.0 to 2.4 million jobs between 1999 and 2011, and that the hardest-hit communities still had lower employment and incomes in 2019. Official output figures overstate manufacturing’s health because they are inflated by measured quality gains in computers; outside that sector, output has been weak. Automation happens everywhere, including in Germany, Japan and South Korea, which kept far larger manufacturing workforces; what distinguished the United States was its willingness to let production move offshore.
Case for “Automation drove the losses”: Manufacturing employment has fallen as a share of jobs in nearly every rich country, including those with trade surpluses, because factories need fewer workers to produce more. Even the China shock estimates attribute only about a tenth of the 2000s manufacturing decline to direct import competition, and the shock was a one-time event that is long over. Bringing production home today would bring back highly automated plants with few jobs, so trade restrictions cannot restore the employment of 1979; policy for affected workers and places would do more.

Question: How much efficiency should a country give up for security and resilience?
Tag: value
Poles: Resilience first / Efficiency first
Why contested: Concentrated supply chains broke down during the pandemic, China used export controls on rare earth magnets and chips in 2025 to halt foreign car plants, and defense planners worry about munitions and shipbuilding capacity. But insurance has a price, and almost any product can be labeled strategic. There is no market price for national security, so the tradeoff is a judgment.
Case for “Resilience first”: A country that cannot make its own chips, ships, drugs or munitions is at the mercy of whoever does, and the main supplier of many of these goods is a strategic rival that has already used dependence as leverage. Markets price the cost of a product, not the cost of losing access to it in a crisis, so firms systematically underinvest in redundancy. Paying somewhat more for domestic or allied supply is the premium on an insurance policy that the market will not write.
Case for “Efficiency first”: Diversification, not self-sufficiency, is what makes supply chains resilient; a country that makes everything at home is exposed to its own disasters and has no alternative suppliers. The genuinely critical items are few and can be handled with stockpiles, targeted support and agreements with allies at modest cost. Labeling whole industries strategic invites every lobby to claim protection, raises costs for every household and firm, and weakens the economy whose size and dynamism are the real foundation of national power.

Question: Can governments pick industries to support without wasting the money?
Tag: empirical
Poles: States can target / Markets allocate better
Why contested: East Asia’s industrial rise and China’s dominance of solar, batteries and electric vehicles are read as proof that industrial policy works; failed subsidy programs, overcapacity and politically chosen projects are read as proof that it does not. Because successes are visible and the counterfactual is not, both sides can point to cases.
Case for “States can target”: Nearly every advanced technology, from the internet to GPS to mRNA vaccines, had public funding at the start, and the countries that caught up fastest, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and China, directed credit and protection to chosen industries. The US CHIPS Act drew roughly $450 billion in announced private chip investment, by the industry’s count, and its share of leading-edge production is projected to rise from zero. The goal is not to pick individual winners but to support sectors with large spillovers or security value, with conditions and performance tests attached.
Case for “Markets allocate better”: Governments know less than markets about which technologies and firms will succeed, and their choices are shaped by lobbying and electoral geography. China’s own industrial policy has produced ghost factories, bankrupt chipmakers and loss-making overcapacity alongside its successes. Subsidies invite foreign retaliation and subsidy races that leave taxpayers everywhere worse off, and a dollar spent propping up a favored industry is a dollar not available to more productive uses.

Question: Do persistent trade deficits harm the economy?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Deficits hollow industry / Deficits are benign
Why contested: The US goods deficit reached about $1.24 trillion in 2025. Standard macroeconomics treats the overall deficit as the counterpart of a country’s saving and investment and its role as issuer of the world’s reserve currency; a school associated with the economist Michael Pettis of the Carnegie Endowment, and taken up by tariff advocates, argues that foreign surpluses, driven by suppressed consumption abroad, are pushed onto the United States and displace its manufacturing.
Case for “Deficits hollow industry”: A trade deficit means goods that could be made at home are made abroad, and it is financed by selling American assets and borrowing, leaving the country with a large net foreign debt. The deficits are not a free choice by American savers: surplus countries such as China and Germany hold down domestic consumption and export the excess, and the United States, with the most open market and the reserve currency, absorbs it. Tariffs that push trade toward balance would shift demand back to American producers.
Case for “Deficits are benign”: The overall deficit reflects how much Americans save and invest relative to what they produce, and foreign demand for US assets reflects confidence in the American economy. Bilateral deficits are meaningless in a world of supply chains. Tariffs change the deficit little, as 2025 showed, because they also strengthen the dollar and reduce exports; the way to change the balance is to change domestic saving, including the federal budget deficit.

Question: Should world trade be governed by common rules or by bilateral deals backed by leverage?
Tag: value
Poles: Common rules / Bilateral leverage
Why contested: The WTO system of non-discriminatory tariffs governed most world trade for decades, but its rule-making has stalled, its appellate body has been unable to hear appeals since 2019 because the United States blocked appointments, and the share of trade on its equal-treatment terms fell from about 80 to 72 percent in a year. The United States is now negotiating country by country, using tariffs as leverage.
Case for “Common rules”: Predictable, non-discriminatory rules let firms invest across borders without fearing that a political decision will wreck their business overnight, and they protect small countries from being bullied by large ones. The postwar system turned trade wars into legal disputes and coincided with the largest rise in prosperity in history. Bilateral deals made under threat produce unstable bargains, invite retaliation and push other countries to build trading blocs that exclude the United States.
Case for “Bilateral leverage”: The multilateral rules were written for a world without a state-directed economy the size of China’s and have proved unable to discipline its subsidies, forced technology transfer or overcapacity. Other countries kept higher tariffs and non-tariff barriers while the United States kept its market open. A large market is leverage, and using it country by country has already won concessions, market access and investment pledges that decades of WTO negotiation did not.

Question: Who should decide tariffs, Congress or the President?
Tag: value
Poles: Congress decides / President decides
Why contested: The Constitution gives Congress the power to lay duties, but over the past century Congress delegated broad tariff authority to the President through statutes on national security, unfair practices and emergencies. The Supreme Court’s February 2026 ruling that the emergency-powers statute does not cover tariffs narrowed one route, and the administration has since used others, which are being challenged in turn.
Case for “Congress decides”: Tariffs are taxes, and taxing is the core power the Constitution reserves to the legislature. When one person can impose, change or lift tariffs on any country overnight, trade policy becomes unpredictable, open to lobbying and favoritism, and usable for purposes unrelated to trade. Bills such as the bipartisan Trade Review Act would require congressional approval of new tariffs within 60 days, restoring accountability.
Case for “President decides”: Trade policy is now inseparable from foreign policy and national security, and negotiating leverage depends on the ability to act quickly and credibly. Congress delegated these powers deliberately, in statutes it can amend, because a 535-member legislature cannot negotiate with foreign governments in real time. Requiring congressional votes on every tariff would hand other countries a veto through their lobbyists in Washington.

Question: Should poorer countries be allowed to protect and subsidize industries that richer countries may not?
Tag: value
Poles: Same rules for all / Room to develop
Why contested: WTO rules give developing countries some flexibility (“special and differential treatment”), but countries self-declare their status, and China’s claim to it is disputed by the United States and the European Union. Meanwhile rich countries have themselves returned to subsidies and tariffs, which developing-country governments see as kicking away the ladder they once climbed.
Case for “Same rules for all”: Infant industry protection usually produces industries that never grow up, sheltered firms and costly goods for poor consumers. The developing countries that grew fastest did so by exporting into open world markets, which depends on everyone keeping theirs open. Special treatment for large economies such as China, now the world’s biggest manufacturer, has let the most powerful exporter claim the privileges of the weakest.
Case for “Room to develop”: Britain, the United States, Germany, Japan, South Korea and China all protected and subsidized young industries before embracing free trade, and today’s rules forbid poorer countries the tools that made the rich rich. Low-income countries that liberalized fastest have often stayed exporters of raw materials. Now that the rich countries are again using tariffs and subsidies for their own purposes, insisting that poor countries alone refrain is a double standard.

Question: Should the goal be more trade at all, or shorter, more local supply chains?
Tag: value
Poles: Expand exchange / Localize production
Why contested: Both free traders and economic nationalists measure success in output, jobs and growth. Localists and food-sovereignty movements reject that yardstick, arguing that long supply chains carry ecological, cultural and democratic costs that prices do not capture; their critics say localization would make people poorer and food supplies less secure.
Case for “Expand exchange”: Trade lets each place specialize in what it does best and gives consumers and firms access to goods, ideas and technologies from everywhere. It has helped lift hundreds of millions out of poverty and, by diversifying sources, protects against local crop failures and disasters. Shipping accounts for a small share of most goods’ emissions, so local production is often not greener.
Case for “Localize production”: Global supply chains reward the largest corporations, ship goods back and forth across oceans, push farmers everywhere into export monocultures and leave communities dependent on decisions made far away. Local economies keep money, skills and control close to home, strengthen democratic accountability and make food and essentials more secure. The aim is not autarky but a reversal of policies that subsidize distance and scale.

## Positions

Camp: Keep trade open under shared rules
Adherents: Cato Institute, whose trade scholars led by Scott Lincicome argue that the administration’s tariffs impose serious economic harms, burden small business and breed cronyism (Cato Institute 2025 annual report, “Fighting to Win Against Protectionism,” https://www.cato.org/annualreport25/fighting-win-against-protectionism); Kimberly Clausing and Maurice Obstfeld of the Peterson Institute for International Economics, who argue that US households and businesses have borne most of the burden of the tariffs and that practical problems keep so-called optimal tariffs from improving national welfare (PIIE, 3 August 2026, https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/who-pays-tariffs-insights-recent-research). Also in this camp: most academic trade economists, including Douglas Irwin and Chad Bown (Foreign Affairs, March 2025, https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/incoherent-case-tariffs-trump), business groups such as the US Chamber of Commerce, many farm and retail associations, and most governments of open trading economies such as the European Union, Japan, Canada and the members of the Comprehensive and Progressive Trans-Pacific Partnership.
Case: Trade makes nations richer for the same reason that specialization makes people richer: each place does what it does best and buys the rest. Tariffs are taxes on a country’s own consumers and producers. They raise the cost of living, raise the cost of the parts and materials American factories use, and invite retaliation against American farmers and exporters. The decline of factory employment is mostly the result of rising productivity, which has happened in every rich country, and the communities hurt by import surges were failed by too little help with adjustment, not by too much trade. The postwar system of shared, predictable rules turned trade disputes into legal cases rather than trade wars, and it coincided with the greatest reduction in poverty in human history. The answer to China’s subsidies is to work with allies under common rules, not to wall off the American market and push the rest of the world to trade around it.
Best evidence: Shipment-level data show that about 96 percent of the 2025 tariffs was passed on to US buyers (Kiel Institute, January 2026), echoing the research on the 2018 and 2019 tariffs; Federal Reserve economists found that the 2018 tariffs were associated with fewer manufacturing jobs, because higher input costs and retaliation outweighed protection; the 2018 washing machine tariffs cost buyers over $815,000 per job reported; US manufacturing employment has been flat since the 2025 tariffs began, factory construction has fallen 19 percent from a year earlier, and taxpayers paid farmers $23 billion to offset retaliation in 2018 and 2019. Polling shows 82 percent of Americans see foreign trade as an opportunity (Gallup, February 2026).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that open trade devastated particular places and left the country dependent on a strategic rival. Open-trade advocates accept that the China shock was real and that the United States did far too little for the workers and towns it hit; their answer is wage insurance, retraining, relocation support and place-based investment, which help the people affected at a fraction of the cost of taxing every consumer. On security they favor stockpiles, diversification among allies and narrowly targeted export controls for genuinely critical goods, arguing that a handful of strategic items does not justify tariffs on everything from coffee to clothing, and that the most resilient supply chains are diverse ones, not domestic ones.
Variation: Libertarians such as Cato favor free trade even unilaterally and are skeptical of trade agreements’ regulatory chapters; center-left economists such as Clausing pair open trade with a stronger safety net and progressive taxation to share its gains; most open-trade governments now accept targeted trade defense against Chinese overcapacity while opposing broad tariffs.

Camp: Use broad tariffs to rebuild home industry
Adherents: The Trump administration, as set out by US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, who writes that persistent trade deficits in manufactured and farm goods have been devastating for the country and that reversing them “demands a strategy of tariffs and deals” to reindustrialize (Financial Times op-ed reprinted by USTR, 22 December 2025, https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2025/december/op-ed-ambassador-jamieson-greer-year-tariff); American Compass, the conservative think tank founded by Oren Cass, which proposes a uniform global tariff starting at 10 percent and rising by five points a year until trade is balanced (February 2025, https://americancompass.org/implement-a-10-global-tariff/). Also in this camp: former US Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer, the Coalition for a Prosperous America, and many in the national-conservative movement; some unions, including the United Steelworkers, back particular tariffs, as shown in the statements USTR collected after its July 2026 forced-labor action (https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/july/american-steelworkers-manufacturers-and-farmers-praise-president-trumps-tariff-action-combat-forced).
Case: For forty years the United States ran its trade policy for consumers and multinational corporations and let its industrial base be hollowed out. Factories closed, towns emptied, the country came to depend on a rival power for medicines, chips and the components of its weapons, and it borrowed trillions from abroad to pay for the imports. Every other major economy protects its producers through tariffs, value-added taxes, subsidies or currency management; only the United States treated its market as a free resource. A broad, predictable tariff changes the decision of where to build: if you want to sell to American customers, make it in America. The revenue can replace other taxes, and the leverage has already won market access and investment pledges that decades of multilateral talks never delivered. International trade is neither good nor bad; the question is whether its pattern serves the national interest.
Best evidence: The China shock research found that rising Chinese imports cost the United States 2.0 to 2.4 million jobs between 1999 and 2011 and that the hardest-hit communities had not recovered by 2019; China’s share of world manufacturing value added rose from under 9 percent in 2004 to about 28 percent in 2024; the US goods deficit reached about $1.24 trillion in 2025. China’s share of US imports fell from 22 percent in 2017 to 9 percent at the end of 2025, showing that tariffs can move supply chains. Tariff leverage produced agreements with the European Union, Japan, South Korea and others committing them to cut barriers and invest in the United States, and customs receipts reached $194.9 billion in fiscal 2025, up from $77.0 billion the year before.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that Americans pay the tariffs and that factory jobs have not come back. Advocates answer that a tariff is meant to change behavior, not to be absorbed by foreigners, and that some price increase is the cost of rebuilding capacity the country should never have lost; studies of short, narrow tariffs say little about a permanent broad one. Plants take years to plan and build, and investment has been held back by the legal uncertainty created by court challenges, which is why making tariffs durable matters. Job counts miss what matters most: the ability to make critical goods, the skills and supplier networks that come with production, and the communities that depend on them.
Variation: The administration combines a baseline tariff on nearly all imports with sector tariffs under national-security law and country-specific tariffs as negotiating leverage; American Compass and Lighthizer favor a fixed, rule-based tariff tied to the trade balance and are more skeptical of deal-by-deal bargaining; national-security conservatives focus on China and defense industries and support allied trade more readily.

Camp: Back strategic industries with subsidies and allies
Adherents: Jake Sullivan, President Biden’s national security adviser, who in an April 2023 speech set out a “modern American industrial strategy” of public investment in chips and clean energy, close coordination with allies, and a “small yard and high fence” of tailored restrictions on the most sensitive technologies, while rejecting decoupling from China (White House archive, 27 April 2023, https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/speeches-remarks/2023/04/27/remarks-by-national-security-advisor-jake-sullivan-on-renewing-american-economic-leadership-at-the-brookings-institution/); the Roosevelt Institute, which argues that industrial policy needs a targeted “trade backstop,” describing the 2024 tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles, chips and solar cells as “an insurance policy” on more than $860 billion in public and private investment (Todd N. Tucker, https://rooseveltinstitute.org/blog/section-301-tariffs-on-electric-vehicles/). Also in this camp: much of the Democratic Party’s policy establishment, economists such as Dani Rodrik, the European Commission’s competitiveness agenda following Mario Draghi’s 2024 report, Japan and South Korea’s economic security policies, and, with a stronger labor emphasis, the AFL-CIO and Rethink Trade, led by Lori Wallach (https://rethinktrade.org/).
Case: Markets alone will not build the industries that a secure, low-carbon economy needs. China spent at least 1.73 percent of its GDP on industrial policy in 2019 and now dominates solar panels, batteries and much of electronics; matching that requires public investment, not slogans. The right tools are targeted: grants and tax credits for semiconductors, clean energy and defense industries, with conditions on wages and investment; narrow tariffs and export controls where a rival’s subsidies or military ambitions threaten a strategic sector; and partnership with allies, so that democracies together have the scale China has alone. Broad tariffs on friends get this backwards: they raise costs for the factories the subsidies are building and drive allies away. Trade should stay open for most goods and with most partners, while the state shapes the few markets that matter most.
Best evidence: Factory construction spending tripled after the CHIPS and Science Act and Inflation Reduction Act, from an annual average of $75.5 billion in 2021 to $235.6 billion in 2024, and fell after the 2025 rollback of clean-energy credits and new tariffs on materials; the chip industry projects the US share of world chipmaking capacity rising from 10 percent in 2022 to 14 percent by 2032 instead of falling to 8 percent, with leading-edge US production rising from zero. Governments around the world adopted more than 2,500 industrial policy measures in 2023 (IMF), so unilateral restraint means losing the race, not stopping it.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that governments are bad at picking winners and that subsidies waste money and provoke subsidy races. Supporters answer that they are not picking firms but supporting sectors with clear spillovers or security value, where private investors systematically underinvest, as with leading-edge chips that had almost entirely left the country. Conditions, clawbacks and competitive grant processes discipline the spending, and coordinating with allies turns potential subsidy races into shared investment. The alternative is not a free market but one shaped by China’s far larger subsidies.
Variation: Security-focused supporters, including many China hawks in both parties, would go further on tariffs and export controls against China; climate-focused supporters center clean energy and want carbon border adjustments; the labor wing, including the AFL-CIO and Rethink Trade, puts enforceable labor rights and an end to corporate privileges in trade deals at the center, has backed some of the Trump administration’s tariffs, and is more skeptical of new free trade agreements even with allies.

Camp: Give developing countries room to build their own industries
Adherents: UN Trade and Development (UNCTAD), which argues that reforms to trade rules should strengthen special and differential treatment and preserve developing countries’ policy space for industrialization (Global Trade Update, March 2026, https://unctad.org/news/reforming-global-trade-rules-key-supporting-developing-economies); Ha-Joon Chang, the Cambridge and SOAS economist whose book “Kicking Away the Ladder” argued that today’s rich countries industrialized behind the protections they now deny poor ones, and who distinguishes protection of genuinely infant industries from tariffs for a mature economy like the United States (Kyunghyang Shinmun interview, 15 April 2025, https://www.khan.co.kr/en/article/202504151802207). Also in this camp: the South Centre, many governments of the Group of 77, India and South Africa in WTO negotiations, and development economists in the structuralist tradition.
Case: The free trade versus nationalism argument is mostly a quarrel among rich countries about how to keep what they have. Every country that grew rich, Britain, the United States, Germany, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and China, protected and subsidized its young industries until they could compete, and only then preached open markets. The rules of the WTO and of most free trade agreements now forbid poorer countries many of the same tools: subsidies tied to local content, controls on foreign investment, flexible patent rules. Meanwhile the rich countries are again subsidizing and protecting their own industries on a vast scale, and imposing tariffs on poor countries’ exports. The answer is not a return to a rule book that kept poor countries exporting raw materials, nor a free-for-all in which the strongest set the terms, but rules that let countries at different stages use different policies.
Best evidence: The countries that cut poverty fastest, China, Vietnam, South Korea and Taiwan, combined export orientation with heavy state direction, protection and controlled capital flows; the share of East Asia and the Pacific living in extreme poverty fell from 67 percent in 1990 to 2 percent in 2024 (World Bank). Rich economies themselves adopted most of the 2,500-plus industrial policy measures recorded in 2023 (IMF), and UNCTAD counts around 18,000 discriminatory trade measures since 2020.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that infant industries often never grow up, protecting inefficient firms at the expense of poor consumers. Advocates agree that protection without discipline fails, and point to the East Asian practice of tying support to export performance and withdrawing it from firms that did not deliver; the lesson is to design industrial policy well, not to forbid it. They add that the alternative, rapid liberalization under the structural adjustment programs of the 1980s and 1990s, was followed by deindustrialization and slow growth in much of Africa and Latin America.
Variation: Some in this camp, such as the governments of India and South Africa, push mainly for flexibility within the WTO; others favor regional trade among developing countries, such as the African Continental Free Trade Area, as a route to scale; China’s claim to developing-country status is contested within the camp as well as outside it.

Camp: Shift production back to local economies
Adherents: La Via Campesina, the international peasant farmers’ movement, whose March 2026 Yaoundé Declaration, issued alongside the WTO ministerial conference, holds that the WTO and free trade cause hunger, poverty and inequality and calls for food sovereignty and the removal of agriculture from WTO negotiations (https://viacampesina.org/en/2026/03/yaounde-declaration-the-wto-and-free-trade-cause-hunger-poverty-and-inequality/); Helena Norberg-Hodge, founder of Local Futures, whose book “Local Is Our Future” argues for a systemic shift from a globalized economy to a network of decentralized, localized economies (https://www.localfutures.org/publications/local-is-our-future-book-helena-norberg-hodge/). Also in this camp: parts of the degrowth and environmental movements, buy-local and community-economics organizations, and small farmers’ unions in many countries.
Case: Free traders and economic nationalists argue over which country gets the factories, but both want an economy built on ever-larger flows of goods controlled by ever-larger firms. Long supply chains ship food and goods back and forth across oceans, push farmers everywhere into export monocultures, let corporations play communities against each other and leave people dependent on markets and decisions they cannot influence. Localization, producing food, energy and basic goods closer to where they are used, keeps money and skills in communities, shortens the distance between producers and those who hold them to account, cuts energy use and makes essential supplies more secure. The aim is not to end trade but to stop subsidizing distance and scale, and to trade in what cannot reasonably be made nearby.
Best evidence: Advocates point to the pandemic-era breakdown of long supply chains, to research linking concentrated corporate control of food systems to farmer distress, and to food price spikes such as those of 2008 and 2022 that hit import-dependent countries hardest. They also note that in Gallup’s trend, the share of Americans seeing trade as a threat reached 52 percent in 2008, showing recurring public unease with globalization when it fails.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that localization would make people poorer, raise prices and make food supplies more fragile by eliminating alternative sources when a local harvest fails. Localists answer that the efficiency of global supply chains rests on unpriced costs, carbon emissions, depleted soils and abandoned communities, and that diverse local and regional food systems are more resilient, not less, than dependence on a few global commodity traders and exporting countries. They favor regional trade and reserves alongside local production rather than self-sufficiency in everything.
Variation: Food-sovereignty movements focus on agriculture and the rights of small farmers; degrowth advocates frame localization as part of reducing total material throughput in rich countries; some on the nationalist right share the goal of local and national self-reliance but not the ecological rationale.

## Crux

The disagreement under the disagreements: how much should a society pay, in higher prices and forgone efficiency, to choose where things are made, and does the payment actually buy the factories, jobs, skills and security it is meant to buy?

What evidence could settle: Whether tariffs and subsidies durably add domestic production, employment and capability, and at what cost once higher input prices for other industries and foreign retaliation are counted. The current US tariff regime, the CHIPS and Inflation Reduction Act investments, China’s state-directed industries and the European Union’s new subsidy programs are large natural experiments whose results will accumulate over the rest of the decade: factory construction and employment, the cost per job and per unit of capacity, the prices consumers pay, and whether subsidized plants survive without permanent support. Evidence can also narrow how much of past factory job loss came from trade rather than automation, how far trade deficits displace domestic production rather than reflect national saving, how vulnerable concentrated supply chains really are in a crisis, and whether developing countries that used industrial policy outperformed those that liberalized fastest.

What no data can close: How to weigh the interests of consumers against those of producers, and of the economy as a whole against particular towns and workers; how much national autonomy and security are worth relative to income; whether a government owes more to its own workers than to workers abroad, including those in poorer countries whose path out of poverty runs through exporting; whether the rules of the world economy should be the same for every country or vary with its stage of development; how much weight to give local control, ecological limits and community stability against growth; and who should hold the power to make these choices, an elected legislature, an executive able to bargain quickly, or international institutions. Two people who agree on every estimate can still disagree about whether a 1 percent rise in prices is a fair price for a steel mill, a chip plant or a farming town.
