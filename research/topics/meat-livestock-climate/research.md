# Meat & Alternatives — research

## Overview

Raising animals for meat, milk and eggs is one of the largest human sources of greenhouse gases, mostly through methane from the digestion of cattle, sheep and goats, nitrous oxide from manure and fertilizer, and carbon released when forests and grasslands are cleared for pasture and feed. It is also how billions of people get protein, iron, zinc and vitamin B12, and how hundreds of millions of farmers and herders make a living. This topic covers the three main answers to that tension in climate and food strategy: changing what people eat, especially eating less beef and other ruminant meat in high-consuming countries; producing meat and dairy with lower emissions, through feed additives, breeding, better feed and health, manure management or grazing practices that store carbon in soil; and replacing animal products with plant-based, fermentation-made or cultivated (cell-grown) meat. It includes the politics around them: meat taxes and dietary guidelines, how livestock methane should be counted, and the laws that approve or ban cultivated meat. It deliberately leaves to their own topics the ethics of killing and confining animals (moral status of animals; welfare reform versus abolition), the broader argument over whether farms should be intensive to spare land for nature or extensive to share it (land sparing versus sharing), and genetically modified feed crops; those appear here only where a climate or food-strategy argument depends on them.

Why now: on 7 July 2026 the European Commission adopted the EU’s first Strategy on Livestock together with a Protein Action Plan (European Commission, https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/setting-sustainable-livestock-farming-strong-footing-2026-07-07_en). The strategy treats livestock as a strategic pillar of European farming and sets five priorities, from crisis preparedness and competitiveness to “harmonised methods for calculating livestock emissions at farm level”; the protein plan aims to raise the share of EU-grown oilseeds and protein crops in animal feed from 25% in 2025 to 35% by 2035. It sets no target for lowering meat consumption, and the European Environmental Bureau called it “a defence of a destructive status quo” the same day (EEB, https://eeb.org/en/stuck-in-the-past-eu-livestock-strategy-presents-no-route-out-for-a-sector-in-crisis/). Four weeks later, on 4 August 2026, the Singapore Food Agency approved Aleph Farms’ cultivated beef steak, the second regulatory approval for cultivated beef anywhere after Israel’s in 2024 (FoodNavigator, https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/04/cultivated-beef-steak-to-hit-singapore-food-scene-with-historic-approval/). Earlier in the year, South Dakota became the eighth US state to ban or pause the sale of cultivated meat (Green Queen, March 2026, https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/south-dakota-lab-grown-cultivated-meat-ban-state-moratorium/), and the US Dietary Guidelines for 2025–2030, released in January 2026, raised recommended protein intake and placed red meat among “high-quality” protein foods without considering environmental impact (Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2026/01/09/dietary-guidelines-for-americans-2025-2030/).

The debate is live because the evidence points one way on emissions per kilogram and another on what people, farmers and governments are willing to do. Life-cycle studies find that beef produces many times the emissions of poultry, pork or plant proteins per gram of protein, and the 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission says red meat production must fall sharply in rich countries. Yet global meat production keeps rising, plant-based meat sales in the United States have fallen for several years, cultivated meat remains a restaurant novelty, and support for meat taxes is falling in countries that have polled it. Climate scientists, health researchers and environmental groups press for diet change; livestock industries, farm ministries and many agricultural scientists argue emissions can be cut on the farm while demand grows; alternative-protein advocates and investors argue that only cheaper, tastier substitutes will move billions of eaters; “less but better” coalitions want fewer animals raised on pasture; and a growing set of governments, from Italy and Hungary to Florida and Texas, frame meat as a cultural and economic good to be protected from substitutes and diet-change policy.

Source URL: https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/new-fao-report-maps-pathways-towards-lower-livestock-emissions/en
Note: The UN Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that livestock supply chains emitted about 6.2 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent in 2015, roughly 12% of human-caused emissions, with cattle responsible for about 62% of that.
Context: The estimate comes from FAO’s life-cycle model GLEAM and includes on-farm emissions (chiefly methane from digestion and manure), feed production, land-use change for feed, input manufacturing, and some processing and transport. FAO’s own earlier figure was higher: its 2013 assessment put livestock at 7.1 billion tonnes, 14.5% of human-caused emissions (FAO, 26 September 2013, https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/Major-cuts-of-greenhouse-gas-emissions-from-livestock-within-reach/en). The two estimates use different model versions, data and reference years, so the lower number does not by itself show that emissions fell. Other studies count more: Xu and colleagues estimated in Nature Food in 2021 that animal-based foods, including feed, account for 57% of all food-production emissions, twice the share of plant-based foods (https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/global-greenhouse-gas-emissions-from-animal-based-foods-are-twice/). Differences come from what is counted as land-use change, which year is used, and how methane is converted to CO2-equivalents. FAO projects that, without new interventions and productivity gains, emissions would rise to about 9.1 billion tonnes by 2050 as demand for animal products grows about 20%.

Context: The figures are global means from a meta-analysis of about 570 studies covering roughly 38,700 farms in 119 countries, and include land-use change, farming, processing, transport, packaging and retail. Beef from dairy herds is lower because part of the animal’s footprint is assigned to milk. Transport is a small part of most foods’ footprint; for beef-herd beef Our World in Data puts it at about 0.5% (https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local). These numbers convert methane with the standard 100-year warming potential; those who favor alternative methane metrics argue that this overstates the ongoing warming from herds that are not growing (see the crux).

Note: Livestock produce about 32% of the methane emitted by human activity, almost as much as fossil fuels at 35%.
Context: Methane is a short-lived but potent gas: it breaks down in about a decade but traps far more heat than carbon dioxide while it lasts, so cutting it slows warming quickly. The Global Methane Pledge, launched in 2021, commits its participants to cutting methane 30% below 2020 levels by 2030. Agriculture as a whole, including rice, is about 40% of human-caused methane.

Those who favor diet change read the same data as showing that even the best beef is far more emissions-intensive than plant protein. Our World in Data notes that beef from dairy herds, 44% of global beef production, has about 60% lower emissions because its footprint is shared with milk.

Note: Across 14 experiments in dairy cattle, the feed additive 3-NOP, sold as Bovaer, cut methane yield by about 31% on average.

Other approaches under study include red seaweed (Asparagopsis), breeding low-methane animals and methane vaccines, none yet in wide use.

New Zealand, which once planned to price farm emissions, decided in October 2025 not to introduce on-farm pricing by 2030 and cut its 2050 biogenic methane target from 24–47% to 14–24% below 2017 levels, a range its independent review linked to “no additional warming” from methane (DairyNZ, 12 October 2025, https://www.dairynz.co.nz/news/dairynz-welcomes-revised-methane-target/).

## Questions

Question: Can farm-level improvements cut livestock emissions enough without people eating less meat?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Better farming suffices / Diets must change
Why contested: Both sides cite the same studies and read them differently. Emissions per kilogram of meat and milk have fallen for decades in many countries and vary enormously between the best and worst producers, which suggests large room for improvement. But total livestock emissions are still rising because output grows faster than intensity falls, the most promising technologies are expensive or hard to use on grazing animals, and modelers assess the potential of diet change as several times larger than that of methane-reducing measures.
Case for “Better farming suffices”: The spread between producers is the opportunity. FAO estimated in 2013 that bringing producers up to the practices of the most efficient could cut sector emissions by as much as 30% with existing technology, and the largest gains are in low-productivity herds in South Asia, Latin America and Africa, where a cow may produce a fraction of the milk of a European one while emitting nearly as much methane. Feed additives such as 3-NOP already cut methane by about a third in dairy trials, and breeding, seaweed-based feeds and vaccines are coming. Demand for animal products is set to grow 20% by 2050 whatever rich-country campaigners say, so the realistic climate question is how to produce that food with fewer emissions per unit. Persuading billions of people to change what they eat has never worked at that scale, and in poorer countries it would be the wrong advice for health.
Case for “Diets must change”: Efficiency gains have been real for decades, yet total livestock emissions keep climbing, because each gain is outrun by more animals. The IPCC assessed the technical potential of shifting diets at about 3.6 billion tonnes CO2-equivalent a year against about 0.8 billion for measures against enteric methane, only 0.2 billion of which is available at up to USD 100 a tonne. Even the lowest-emitting tenth of beef has more than ten times the footprint of the highest-emitting tenth of peas, so no plausible improvement closes the gap. Diet change also frees land that can return to forest and store carbon, which no feed additive can do. Without lower consumption of ruminant meat in high-consuming countries, food emissions alone threaten to use up most of what remains of the 1.5°C carbon budget.

Question: Should governments actively steer people away from eating meat?
Tag: value
Poles: Steer diets through policy / Leave diets to people
Why contested: Governments already shape diets through dietary guidelines, school and hospital menus, farm subsidies and food taxes, so the question is less whether they influence what people eat than in which direction and by what means. Meat is tied to culture, religion, rural livelihoods and identity, and public support for meat taxes is falling where it has been measured, while health and climate researchers argue that leaving diets to habit and price signals that ignore emissions is itself a policy choice.
Case for “Steer diets through policy”: Food prices do not include the damage livestock emissions cause, and farm subsidies in many rich countries actively lower the cost of meat and dairy. Correcting that is no more paternalistic than taxing tobacco or carbon in fuel. Governments can also act without forbidding anything: plant-forward public procurement, honest dietary guidelines, support for farmers who switch, and funding for alternatives. Denmark has shown that a livestock emissions tax can be agreed with farmers at the table. The health case adds weight: diets high in red and processed meat are linked to heart disease and some cancers, and the 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission estimated that dietary shifts could prevent up to 15 million premature deaths a year.
Case for “Leave diets to people”: What people eat is among the most personal choices they make, and governments that tell citizens to give up meat for the climate are asking ordinary families to carry costs that wealthier, more influential sectors avoid. Meat taxes are regressive, hitting low-income households hardest; in the UK support for one fell to 32% in 2025, with 42% opposed. Livestock farming sustains rural communities and cultural traditions that are not interchangeable with factory-made substitutes. Agriculture can and should cut its emissions through research and voluntary adoption, as New Zealand chose in 2025 when it dropped plans to price farm emissions, without governments engineering the dinner plate.

Question: How should livestock methane be counted: by its tonnes in CO2-equivalents or by the additional warming it causes?
Tag: value
Poles: Count warming added / Count every tonne
Why contested: Methane breaks down in about a decade, so a herd whose emissions stay constant keeps the atmosphere’s methane roughly level rather than adding to it year after year as carbon dioxide does. A metric called GWP* reflects this and shows a stable herd adding little new warming, while the standard 100-year metric (GWP100) counts each year’s methane as if it were long-lived. The science of methane’s lifetime is not in dispute; the disagreement is over which question a climate target should answer and who gets credit for past emissions.
Case for “Count warming added”: Climate targets are ultimately about temperature, and GWP* describes temperature effects more accurately than GWP100 for short-lived gases. Under GWP100, a farm that has cut its methane by a third still looks like a large emitter, while under GWP* such cuts actually lower temperatures, giving farmers a credible goal of “climate neutrality.” New Zealand set its 2050 methane target in 2025 at 14–24% below 2017 levels, a range its independent review linked to no additional warming. Treating a constant flow of methane as though it accumulates like carbon dioxide overstates livestock’s ongoing contribution and drives policies that could shift production to less efficient countries.
Case for “Count every tonne”: GWP* measures change from a baseline, so it rewards whoever already emits the most: a country with a large herd can hold emissions steady and claim no new warming, while a poor country starting to grow its herd from a low base is counted as warming the planet. In May 2026, 42 climate scientists issued a statement warning against such targets, and one signatory, Drew Shindell of Duke University, said the approach “gives a free pass to countries with large existing herds” (Inside Climate News, 20 May 2026, https://insideclimatenews.org/news/20052026/global-warming-potential-star-livestock-emissions/). Methane already in the air is still warming the planet, and the Paris goal requires reversing warming, not just freezing it, which means deep methane cuts. Using a different metric for one sector also lets that sector look better without any change on the farm.

Question: Will cultivated meat become cheap and clean enough to replace a meaningful share of animal meat?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Scalable within decades / Niche at best
Why contested: Cultivated meat is approved for sale in a handful of countries but is not yet produced at commercial scale anywhere, so every claim about its price and climate footprint is a projection. Techno-economic studies disagree by an order of magnitude about whether growth media and bioreactors can ever be cheap, and life-cycle studies disagree on whether the product will be far cleaner or dirtier than beef. Private investment has fallen more than 90% since 2021.
Case for “Scalable within decades”: Every transformative technology looked expensive at first; solar panels and lithium batteries fell steeply in price once production scaled. Cultivated meat has gone from a single, enormously expensive demonstration burger in 2013 to approvals in Singapore, the United States, Israel, Australia and New Zealand, and in 2026 Singapore approved cultivated beef. Companies report steep falls in growth-medium costs, and a 2021 life-cycle assessment by CE Delft found that at scale and with renewable energy cultivated meat could have a carbon footprint 92% below beef and use far less land. If it reaches price and taste parity, consumers will switch without being asked to sacrifice anything, avoiding the political fight over diets entirely.
Case for “Niche at best”: Animal cells are fragile, grow slowly and need sterile conditions closer to pharmaceutical manufacturing than to brewing. A widely cited techno-economic analysis funded by Open Philanthropy, by the chemical engineer David Humbird, estimated that even a large facility with cells engineered for efficient growth would produce wet cell mass at about $37 per kilogram, above his $25 affordability target, and that unstructured products made from such cells might reach a minimum of about $50 a kilogram at the supermarket (Humbird, 2021, https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/version/2182/2969/2171). UC Davis researchers found in 2024 that if cells need pharmaceutical-grade purified media, cultivated meat’s footprint could be 4 to 25 times that of retail beef. Investors have retreated, cultivated-meat funding fell to $74 million in 2025, and eight US states, Italy and Hungary have moved to ban it. Betting climate strategy on it diverts attention from proven measures.

Question: Can people be persuaded to eat substantially less meat at the scale climate goals would require?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Norms can shift / Demand is stubborn
Why contested: Many people say they are willing to eat less meat, and per-person meat consumption has flattened in some rich countries, but global production keeps rising, the share of vegetarians in the United States has not changed in a decade, and plant-based meat sales are falling. Evidence from interventions such as menu defaults and pricing is promising in trials but rarely tested at national scale.
Case for “Norms can shift”: Diets change faster than people think: what people eat has been transformed within a generation by urbanization, supermarkets and marketing, and some of that has been in the direction of less red meat; beef consumption per person in the United States is well below its 1970s peak. Behavioral studies show that making plant-based dishes the default on menus, improving their placement and naming them appealingly reduces meat ordering substantially without restricting choice. Cultural leaders, chefs and public institutions can normalize flexitarian eating as they normalized not smoking. Young people in many surveys are more open to eating less meat than their parents.
Case for “Demand is stubborn”: The record is sobering. Gallup found 4% of Americans vegetarian in 2023, as in 2012 and 2018; US plant-based meat sales fell 10% in 2025; and worldwide meat production rose about 17% in the decade to 2024. Where meat consumption has fallen in rich countries, it has mostly shifted from beef to chicken rather than to plants. Rising incomes in Asia, Africa and Latin America will drive demand up for decades, and people there have every right to eat as well as Europeans and Americans. Campaigns that ask for sacrifice also provoke backlash, visible in falling support for meat taxes and in laws banning substitutes.

Question: Is beef from well-managed pasture better or worse for the climate than beef from intensive feedlot systems?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Pasture is better / Intensive is better
Why contested: Grass-fed cattle grow more slowly and so emit more methane over their lives per kilogram of meat, and they need more land, which intensive-farming advocates count as a cost. Regenerative grazing advocates argue that well-managed pasture stores carbon in soil, avoids the emissions of growing feed grain, and supports biodiversity. The answer depends on how soil carbon is measured, how long it stays, and what the land would otherwise be used for.
Case for “Pasture is better”: Industrial feedlots depend on grain grown with synthetic fertilizer and pesticides, often on land cleared for soy and maize, and concentrate manure into pollution. Animals on well-managed pasture eat grass people cannot, fertilize the soil naturally, and through rotational grazing can rebuild soil organic matter; a peer-reviewed study of White Oak Pastures in Georgia found that soil carbon gains gave its beef a footprint 66% lower than conventional US beef. Grasslands evolved with grazing animals, and their removal can harm ecosystems. Counting only methane per kilogram misses these benefits, along with animal welfare and rural livelihoods.
Case for “Intensive is better”: Per kilogram of meat, faster-growing grain-finished cattle emit less methane and use far less land, and the land saved can be left to forest or wild grassland that stores more carbon than any pasture. The Oxford-led “Grazed and Confused?” review found that grazing’s soil carbon sequestration could offset only 20–60% of grazing systems’ own emissions at best, and only 4–11% of total livestock emissions, and that the gains are time-limited and reversible. The same White Oak study found the system needed about 2.5 times more land per kilogram of beef; scaling grass-fed beef to current demand would require clearing more land, which would release carbon.

Question: Should cultivated meat be allowed on the market once regulators find it safe?
Tag: value
Poles: Let consumers choose / Ban or restrict it
Why contested: Food-safety regulators in several countries have approved cultivated meat after review, yet legislators in Italy, Hungary and eight US states have barred or paused its sale, citing protection of farmers, food traditions and public health. Supporters of cultivated meat call the bans protectionism; supporters of the bans see an untested, factory-made product being pushed by investors and global institutions.
Case for “Let consumers choose”: If a product has passed a safety review by expert regulators, people should be free to buy it or not. Bans protect incumbents from competition, the kind of measure free-market conservatives usually oppose; the governor of South Dakota first vetoed a permanent ban in 2026, warning it would burden interstate commerce and invite expensive legal challenges. Cultivated meat may prove useful for climate, animal welfare, food security in countries with little farmland, or pandemic risk from livestock; banning it before it reaches the market forecloses that knowledge. Labels can address concerns about transparency without prohibition.
Case for “Ban or restrict it”: Food is not just another product; it is bound up with national culture, rural communities and trust built over generations. Cultivated meat is a novel, highly processed product whose long-term health effects have not been studied, made with technology concentrated in a few well-funded companies, and its promotion as a climate solution is seen by these lawmakers as part of an effort to discredit livestock farmers. Italy’s farmers’ federation Coldiretti gathered about 350,000 signatures against “synthetic” food before the 2023 ban, and Florida’s governor framed the state’s 2024 ban as protecting farmers and ranchers from a “global elite” agenda. Governments have a right to decide what is sold within their borders.

Question: Are diets with little or no red meat healthier for most people?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Less red meat is healthier / Meat is nutritionally vital
Why contested: Large observational studies link high intakes of red and especially processed meat with heart disease, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer, and the World Health Organization’s cancer agency classifies processed meat as carcinogenic and red meat as probably carcinogenic. But the evidence comes mostly from observational studies that struggle to separate meat from other lifestyle factors, effects at moderate intakes are small, and animal-source foods are dense sources of protein, iron, zinc, vitamin B12 and other nutrients that many people, especially in poorer countries, lack.
Case for “Less red meat is healthier”: Decades of cohort studies across many countries find that people who eat more red and processed meat have higher rates of chronic disease, and replacing meat with legumes, nuts and whole grains is associated with lower risk. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified processed meat as Group 1 (carcinogenic) and red meat as Group 2A (probably carcinogenic) in 2015. The 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission, based on health evidence, set a reference intake of about 15 grams of red meat a day. In rich countries where people eat several times that, the health and climate cases point the same way.
Case for “Meat is nutritionally vital”: Animal-source foods provide high-quality protein and nutrients in forms the body absorbs readily, and FAO’s 2023 evidence review emphasized their role in preventing undernutrition, especially for young children, pregnant women and older people. In low-income countries the problem is too little animal-source food, not too much: the OECD and FAO project average intake there at 143 kcal a day in 2034, below the 300-kcal benchmark for a healthy diet. Observational links between red meat and disease are weak and confounded, absolute risks at moderate intakes are small, and the new US dietary guidelines place red meat among high-quality proteins. Plant-based meat substitutes are often ultra-processed and high in salt.

## Positions

Camp: Cut livestock emissions without cutting meat
Adherents: Global Dairy Platform, the industry body that coordinates Pathways to Dairy Net Zero, launched in September 2021 to “accelerate climate change action and reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions across the dairy sector” while continuing to produce nutritious food and support livelihoods, with founding supporters accounting for about 30% of world milk production (https://globaldairyplatform.com/pathways-to-dairy-net-zero-initiative-launched/); Frank Mitloehner, animal scientist and director of the CLEAR Center at the University of California, Davis, who wrote that “even if Americans eliminated all animal protein from their diets, they would reduce U.S. greenhouse gas emissions by only 2.6 percent” and argued for spreading efficient livestock practices to developing countries instead (The Conversation, 2018, https://theconversation.com/yes-eating-meat-affects-the-environment-but-cows-are-not-killing-the-climate-94968). In its regenerative form, the Savory Institute, which argues that Holistic Planned Grazing of livestock on degraded grasslands has the “potential to remove excess atmospheric carbon” (https://savory.global/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/restoring-the-climate.pdf).
Case: Meat, milk and eggs are among the most nutritious foods people eat, demand for them is growing as billions escape poverty, and livestock support the livelihoods of hundreds of millions of farmers and herders. The climate task is therefore to produce that food with far lower emissions, not to tell people to stop eating it. The tools exist and are improving fast. Emissions per liter of milk and per kilogram of meat have fallen steeply in countries with modern herds, and FAO found the gap between the most and least efficient producers so large that spreading existing best practices could cut sector emissions by as much as 30%. Feed additives cut methane from dairy cows by about a third; better feed, animal health and breeding mean fewer animals for the same food; methane digesters turn manure into energy. Because methane is short-lived, steady cuts of even a few percent a year mean livestock stop adding to warming and then begin to reverse it, a real and measurable goal for farmers. Diet campaigns aimed at rich countries, by contrast, would achieve little: in the United States, removing all animal products would cut national emissions by only a few percent, and the production would simply move to countries with dirtier herds.
Variation: The camp has an intensification wing and a regenerative wing that agree consumption need not fall but disagree sharply on how to farm. The intensification wing, which includes most large meat and dairy companies, industry bodies such as Global Dairy Platform and the US National Cattlemen’s Beef Association (which set a goal in 2021 to demonstrate climate neutrality of US cattle production by 2040), and many agricultural scientists, emphasizes productivity, feed additives, genetics and confinement or feedlot finishing that lowers emissions per kilogram. The regenerative wing, including the Savory Institute, grass-fed ranchers such as White Oak Pastures and many smaller farm groups, argues that well-managed grazing stores carbon in soil and restores grasslands, and rejects feedlots and grain feeding. The camp also divides on methane accounting: many members favor the GWP* metric and targets of “no additional warming,” while others accept standard CO2-equivalent accounting and aim for absolute cuts.
Best evidence: FAO’s 2023 assessment concluded that “enhancing productivity and production efficiency across the entire value chain is the most promising way to mitigate and reduce livestock emissions,” and that better grassland practices such as agroforestry could offset nearly a third of current livestock emissions (FAO, 8 December 2023, https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/new-fao-report-maps-pathways-towards-lower-livestock-emissions/en). A meta-analysis of 14 experiments found 3-NOP cut dairy methane yield by 30.9% (Kebreab et al., 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9868067/). The OECD-FAO outlook projects direct agricultural emissions rising only 6% over the decade to 2034 while output grows faster, because emission intensity keeps falling (FAO, 15 July 2025, https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/oecd-fao-agricultural-outlook-2025-2034--emerging-economies-will-drive-growth-in-animal-source-food-consumption-and-production/en). New Zealand’s dairy sector reports that dairy cattle methane is down 4.1% since 2017 (DairyNZ, 12 October 2025, https://www.dairynz.co.nz/news/dairynz-welcomes-revised-methane-target/).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that efficiency gains have never kept up with rising output, so total emissions keep climbing, and that the IPCC rates the potential of diet change several times higher than that of methane-reducing measures. The camp answers that the diet potential is a technical maximum that assumes billions of people change their food habits, which has never happened at that scale, while farm measures are real, adoptable and improving; the IPCC itself noted that models do not capture the full potential of the newest methane inhibitors. Output growth is coming mainly from poorer countries with the least efficient herds, which is exactly where productivity gains do the most good. And demand-side cuts in rich countries risk “leakage,” shifting production to places with higher emissions per kilogram.

Camp: Shift diets toward plants
Adherents: World Resources Institute, whose researchers Richard Waite and Daniel Vennard wrote that “limiting the global rise in meat consumption – in particular, beef, lamb and goat – is critical for reining in runaway warming” and that “shifting the diets of higher-income consumers toward plant-based foods remains essential” (WRI, 17 October 2018, https://www.wri.org/insights/without-changing-diets-agriculture-alone-could-produce-enough-emissions-surpass-15degc); the 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission, co-chaired by Johan Rockström, Walter Willett and Shakuntala Thilsted, whose planetary health diet “emphasises a plant-rich diet, with optional, moderate amounts of animal-source foods” and which finds that transforming food systems “could cut these emissions by more than half” (EAT, 3 October 2025, https://eatforum.org/update/eat-lancet-commission-warns-food-systems-breach-planetary-limits/).
Case: The numbers leave no room to avoid it. Beef produces roughly 50 kg of CO2-equivalent per 100 grams of protein, against under 6 kg for poultry and under half a kilogram for peas, and even the best beef is far more emissions-intensive than plant protein. Livestock use about 80% of the world’s farmland to supply 17% of its calories. Farm technologies help but cannot close a gap that large, and their cumulative effect has been outrun by growth in animal numbers for decades. The fastest, cheapest and most certain cut available is for people who eat the most meat, especially beef and lamb, to eat much less of it, replacing it with legumes, grains, nuts, vegetables and, where they wish, poultry, fish or alternatives. That would also free hundreds of millions of hectares that could return to forest and other ecosystems, drawing carbon from the air, and would improve health: diets high in red and processed meat raise the risk of heart disease and some cancers. The responsibility lies with the rich: the 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission found the richest 30% of people drive more than 70% of food-related environmental impacts. People in poorer countries who lack animal-source foods should get more, not less.
Variation: Members differ on how far and how fast. WRI asks the roughly 20% of people who would otherwise be high ruminant-meat consumers to cut their consumption of beef, lamb and goat by about 40% by 2050 relative to 2010 (https://research.wri.org/sites/default/files/2019-07/creating-sustainable-food-future_2_5.pdf); Greenpeace calls for halving global production and consumption of meat and dairy by 2050, and for the remaining production to come from ecological farming (https://www.greenpeace.org/international/publication/15093/less-is-more/); vegan advocates want animal products phased out. They differ on means, from information and better menus to removing subsidies and taxing emissions, and on whether the replacement protein should be beans or high-tech alternatives. Some in the camp accept efficient intensive production of the meat that is eaten as the lower-emission option; others side with pasture-based farming.
Best evidence: The IPCC assessed, with medium confidence, a technical mitigation potential of 3.6 billion tonnes CO2-equivalent a year (range 0.3–8.0) from shifting toward sustainable healthy diets, against 0.8 billion for measures reducing enteric methane (IPCC AR6 WGIII, Chapter 7, https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-7/). Poore & Nemecek’s meta-analysis of about 38,700 farms found the lowest-impact tenth of beef emits about 9 kg CO2-equivalent per 100 grams of protein, against 0.8 kg for the highest-impact tenth of peas (Our World in Data, https://ourworldindata.org/less-meat-or-sustainable-meat). The 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission estimated that shifting to its reference diet could prevent up to 15 million premature deaths a year.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that people will not change what they eat, so a strategy built on diet change is wishful thinking, and that it imposes elite preferences on ordinary families and on farmers. The camp answers that diets have always changed with prices, availability and norms, and that governments already shape them through subsidies, procurement and guidelines that currently favor meat; changing those signals is not coercion. The shift asked for is concentrated in high-consuming populations and is compatible with eating some meat. Farmers can be supported through the transition, as they were not in other industrial shifts, and plant-rich diets can be built from familiar foods rather than sacrifice. Finally, the alternative is not neutral: relying on farm technology alone leaves food emissions on course to consume much of the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget.

Camp: Protect conventional meat and farmers from replacement
Adherents: Coldiretti, Italy’s largest farmers’ federation, which gathered about 350,000 signatures against “synthetic” food, argued that it “consumes more water and energy than many traditional farms” and offers no guarantee of safety, and campaigned for the 2023 law banning cultivated meat in Italy (Coldiretti, 21 December 2022, https://www.coldiretti.it/salute-e-sicurezza-alimentare/ue-nel-2023-arriva-la-carne-sintetica-350mila-firme-contro); Ron DeSantis, governor of Florida, who on signing the state’s 2024 ban said “Florida is fighting back against the global elite’s plan to force the world to eat meat grown in a petri dish or bugs to achieve their authoritarian goals” and pledged to keep “investing in our local farmers and ranchers” (Florida Governor’s Office, 1 May 2024, https://www.flgov.com/eog/news/press/2024/governor-desantis-signs-legislation-keep-lab-grown-meat-out-florida).
Case: Raising animals for food is one of humanity’s oldest and most valuable traditions. It feeds families with nutritious, natural food, keeps rural communities and landscapes alive, and in places like Italy is inseparable from national culture. The campaign to make people eat less meat, tax it, or replace it with products grown in steel tanks is driven largely by investors, multinational technology companies and international institutions, not by farmers or the public, and it treats the people who raise animals as a problem to be eliminated. Cultivated meat is a novel, highly processed product with no long-term safety record, controlled by a few companies, and its environmental benefits are unproven; some studies find it could be worse for the climate than beef. Governments have a duty to protect their farmers, their food heritage and their citizens’ health from such products, and to keep climate policy from reaching onto people’s plates. Where livestock emissions matter, farmers are already improving and should be supported, not punished.
Variation: The camp includes national governments (Italy, whose 2023 law also restricts meat names such as “salami” and “steak” on plant-based products; Hungary, whose parliament voted for a ban in November 2025), US state legislators who have passed bans or moratoria in eight states, farm federations and some cattle-producer groups. Some members want permanent bans; others, such as Indiana, Texas and South Dakota, have chosen temporary moratoria, and South Dakota’s governor vetoed a permanent ban before signing a five-year one. Members differ on whether they accept that livestock emissions are a real problem: some farm groups support methane research and efficiency programs, while some politicians reject climate-driven food policy altogether. In the United States, the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines, which emphasize “high-quality” protein including red meat and do not consider environmental impact, reflect a similar view at the federal level. Free-market conservatives who oppose meat taxes but also oppose bans on cultivated meat stand at the edge of this camp.
Best evidence: Public opinion is on the camp’s side in several countries. In the UK, opposition to a food tax that would raise the price of red meat and dairy rose to 42% in 2025, exceeding support at 32% (King’s College London & Ipsos, 12 February 2026, https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/uks-sense-of-urgency-on-net-zero-and-support-for-climate-policies-falls-sharply-study-finds). Peer-reviewed research supports its doubts about cultivated meat’s environmental promise: UC Davis researchers found that with pharmaceutical-grade growth media its footprint could be 4 to 25 times that of retail beef (https://www.ucdavis.edu/food/news/lab-grown-meat-carbon-footprint-worse-beef). FAO’s 2023 evidence review of animal-source foods stated that “evidence suggests that these products cannot replace TASF [terrestrial animal source food] in terms of nutritional composition,” referring to plant-based and cell-cultured alternatives (FAO, https://openknowledge.fao.org/handle/20.500.14283/cc3912en).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that banning a product regulators have found safe is protectionism that denies consumers a choice and shields incumbents from competition. The camp answers that every country decides what food may be sold within its borders, and that safety approval by regulators abroad, after short reviews of a brand-new process, does not settle questions of long-term health, food sovereignty and the survival of farming communities. Novel foods have been restricted before while evidence accumulates. Many of the laws are moratoria, not permanent bans, pending more evidence. And consumers are not clamoring for these products: plant-based meat sales are falling and cultivated meat is barely on sale anywhere.

Camp: Replace animal meat with alternative proteins
Adherents: The Good Food Institute, a nonprofit that works to “transform how meat is made—satisfying rising food demand in ways that accelerate climate progress, protect animals and ecosystems, and strengthen public health,” by making plant-based, fermented and cultivated proteins as delicious and affordable as conventional meat (https://gfi.org/about/); Bill Gates, who told MIT Technology Review in 2021, “I do think all rich countries should move to 100% synthetic beef,” while adding “I don’t think the poorest 80 countries will be eating synthetic meat” (https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/02/14/1018296/bill-gates-climate-change-beef-trees-microsoft/).
Case: Meat demand is rising and campaigns to persuade people to eat less have barely moved it, so asking billions of people to sacrifice what they enjoy is not a climate strategy. The answer is to change how meat is made. If plant-based, fermentation-made and cultivated meats can match animal meat on taste and price, people will choose them for the same reasons they choose any food, and emissions, land use, pollution, antibiotic use and animal suffering fall together, without the political fight over diets. Electric cars are displacing petrol cars not because drivers were shamed but because the product got better and cheaper; food can follow the same path. The technology is real: regulators in Singapore, the United States, Israel, Australia and New Zealand have approved cultivated products, and in 2026 Singapore approved cultivated beef. What is missing is the public investment in open research, production capacity and fair regulation that every other climate technology received.
Variation: The camp spans plant-based food companies, fermentation start-ups, cultivated-meat developers and their investors, and the governments that fund them, including Singapore, Israel, the Netherlands and Denmark. Members disagree about which technology will matter most: plant-based products are already on shelves but have stalled in the United States; precision fermentation now attracts the most investment; cultivated meat is the most ambitious and furthest from scale. Many in the camp, such as Gates, see substitutes as a solution for rich and middle-income countries and support more productive livestock in poorer ones. Some members are motivated mainly by animal welfare, others by climate or food security.
Best evidence: A 2021 life-cycle assessment by CE Delft, commissioned by GFI and GAIA, found that cultivated meat made at scale with renewable energy could cut the carbon footprint of beef by up to 92% and use far less land (GFI Europe, https://gfieurope.org/blog/cultivated-meat-could-slash-climate-impact-of-meat-production-by-92/). Plant proteins such as tofu and peas emit 2 kg and 0.4 kg CO2-equivalent per 100 grams of protein against about 50 kg for beef-herd beef (Our World in Data, https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/ghg-per-protein-poore). Singapore’s August 2026 approval of Aleph Farms’ cultivated steak extended approvals from poultry and seafood to beef (FoodNavigator, https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/04/cultivated-beef-steak-to-hit-singapore-food-scene-with-historic-approval/).
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection is that the market has rendered its verdict: US plant-based meat sales fell 10% in 2025, investment in alternative proteins is down more than 80% from its 2021 peak, and cultivated meat may never be cheap or clean. The camp answers that every clean technology passed through a trough when early hype met engineering reality, and that solar power and batteries survived theirs because governments kept funding research and deployment. Current plant-based products are a first generation that has not yet reached price or taste parity, which consumers name as the main barriers. The pessimistic life-cycle results assume pharmaceutical-grade inputs that companies are already replacing with food-grade ones, and the UC Davis study itself found food-grade production could be up to 80% below beef. Investing in alternatives is a hedge against the possibility that neither farm technology nor diet persuasion delivers enough.

Camp: Eat less meat from better farms
Adherents: Eating Better, a UK alliance of more than 60 organizations working toward a “50% reduction in meat and dairy consumption in the UK by 2030” and a transition to “better meat and dairy as standard,” with a just transition for farmers (https://www.eating-better.org/better-by-half/); Philip Lymbery, global chief executive of Compassion in World Farming, who argues that “ending industrial animal agriculture and reducing meat overconsumption – especially in regions like the UK, Europe and America – are essential steps toward genuine sustainability,” and urges “choosing pasture fed, organic and regenerative meat and dairy products” (4 November 2025, https://philiplymbery.com/from-storybook-farms-to-factory-realities-why-compassion-must-lead-the-way/).
Case: The problem is not that people eat meat but that rich countries eat far too much of it, produced in industrial systems that confine animals, depend on imported feed grown on cleared forest, pollute rivers and breed antibiotic resistance. Cutting consumption roughly in half in high-consuming countries would cut emissions and free land; the meat that remains should come from animals raised on pasture and in mixed farms, where they eat grass and food waste rather than human-edible grain, fertilize soil, support wildlife and are treated humanely. That combination answers climate, nature, health and animal welfare together, which neither pure efficiency nor pure diet change does. It also keeps farmers in business: they can earn more from fewer, better animals if public money and procurement reward quality rather than volume, and it keeps alive the landscapes and traditions that livestock farming sustains.
Variation: Members differ on the size and pace of the reduction, from Eating Better’s 50% by 2030 in the UK to more gradual targets elsewhere, and most want the largest cuts in grain-fed pig and poultry meat as well as beef. They differ on what “better” means, with some emphasizing organic and pasture-fed systems, others animal welfare standards and others local or regenerative production, and on whether plant-based and cultivated alternatives are welcome supplements or industrial products to be avoided. Friends of the Earth, Compassion in World Farming, the Soil Association and many public-health and farming groups are members of this camp or of Eating Better; Greenpeace’s call to halve meat and dairy and move to ecological farming is close to it.
Best evidence: The Oxford-led “Grazed and Confused?” review found that well-managed grazing can offset 20–60% of grazing systems’ emissions through soil carbon, a real if partial benefit (https://www.tabledebates.org/sites/default/files/2022-04/Grazed%20and%20Confused%20summary.pdf). A peer-reviewed study of White Oak Pastures found multi-species rotational grazing gave beef a footprint 66% lower than conventional US beef (Rowntree et al., 2020, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2020.544984/full). Our World in Data notes that about 38% of cropland is used for animal feed (https://ourworldindata.org/global-land-for-agriculture); animals fed on grass and residues rather than grain free that cropland for people.
Answer to the strongest objection: The strongest objection, from both the diet-change and efficiency camps, is that pasture-raised meat produces more methane per kilogram and needs more land, so “better” meat is worse for the climate per bite. The camp answers that the comparison is only fair at the level of the whole food system: if total consumption falls by half, extensive systems can supply what remains without more land, while eliminating feed-crop emissions, deforestation for soy and the pollution of intensive units. Counting methane per kilogram ignores soil carbon, biodiversity, animal welfare, antibiotic resistance and the value of grassland that cannot grow crops. The real choice is not between grass-fed and feedlot beef at today’s volumes but between a smaller, nature-friendly livestock sector and an ever larger industrial one.

## Crux

Can the climate impact of meat be brought down far enough by changing how it is produced, by farm technology, better grazing or substitutes, or does it also require that people in high-consuming countries eat much less of it; and how much should governments shape that choice?

Every camp on this page accepts that livestock are a large source of methane and that beef and lamb have far higher emissions per gram of protein than plant foods. They split over whether the gap can be closed on the supply side. If feed additives, breeding, productivity gains and better grazing can cut livestock emissions steeply while output grows, or if alternatives can win on taste and price, then the case for asking people to eat less meat weakens, and diet change becomes a personal choice rather than a climate necessity. If they cannot, then holding to climate targets means lower consumption of ruminant meat in rich countries, whoever finds that unwelcome. Beneath this lies a second question that evidence cannot answer: even if lower consumption would help, whether governments should use taxes, guidelines, procurement or bans to steer what people eat and which foods may be sold, or leave it to individuals, markets and tradition.

What evidence could settle: how much enteric methane inhibitors, seaweed feeds, breeding and vaccines cut emissions in practice, including on grazing animals, at what cost and how durably; how much carbon well-managed grazing stores in soil, for how long, and how reliably it can be measured; whether cultivated and fermentation-made proteins can reach price parity with food-grade inputs and low-carbon energy; how far production would shift to other countries if rich-country demand fell; how strongly moderate red-meat intake affects health; and which policies, from menu defaults to taxes, actually change diets at scale and with what public support.

What no data can close: whether climate targets for methane should aim to stop adding warming or to reverse it, and from which baseline, which decides how much credit large existing herds receive; how to weigh food traditions, rural livelihoods and personal freedom against emission cuts; whether it is legitimate for governments to steer diets or to forbid new foods; how the burden should be shared between rich countries that eat the most meat and poorer countries where many people need more animal-source food; and how much weight animal welfare and wild nature should carry alongside climate when choosing between intensive, pasture-based and substitute systems.
