## Overview

Local news is the reporting that covers a particular town, county or region: city councils and school boards, courts and police, local business, zoning, elections for offices most national outlets never mention. For most of the twentieth century in the United States, it was paid for by local newspapers whose classified, retail and legal-notice advertising also funded reporters who attended the meetings, read the budgets and asked officials questions. Since about 2005 that model has collapsed. Advertising moved to Craigslist, Google and Facebook, readers moved online and stopped paying, and the industry shrank by roughly three quarters of its jobs, leaving hundreds of counties with no local news source at all and over a thousand with only one. This topic covers what that loss means and how, if at all, to sustain local watchdog reporting as newspapers vanish: whether communities measurably suffer, and the main remedies on offer, from state tax credits and public media to making technology and AI companies pay, nonprofit and philanthropic newsrooms, letting broadcasters consolidate, and clearing legal barriers for new kinds of outlets. It is centered on the United States, where the decline is best measured and most debated, with Canada and Australia as the main comparisons for policy. It deliberately leaves aside trust in and bias of national media, misinformation and deepfakes (the epistemic crisis topic), content moderation on platforms (the content moderation topic), and AI training and copyright in general (the AI copyright topic), touching each only where it bears on local reporting. Antitrust appears only where it concerns who owns local outlets.

The why-now event: on 13 October 2026 Northwestern University’s Medill Local News Initiative published The State of Local News 2026, which counted 238 “news desert” counties with no locally based news source, up 26 in a year, “the largest single-year rise by far since Medill began keeping records five years ago,” and 170 newspapers closed or merged in the preceding year, close to three a week (Medill, https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2026/report/; Benton Institute summary, 14 October 2026, https://www.benton.org/headlines/state-local-news-2026; Nieman Lab, 13 October 2026, https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/10/from-bad-to-worse-2026-is-shaping-up-to-be-a-super-el-nino-year-for-local-news/). The report found traffic to 100 of the largest local newspaper websites down almost 50 percent since 2022, a fall its researchers linked in part to AI and changing search interfaces, and for the first time counted local TV newsrooms: 678 commercial newsrooms, three quarters controlled by ten companies. It landed in a year of other recognizable markers. In January 2026 the owners of the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette, founded in 1786, announced it would cease operations on 3 May 2026, citing more than $350 million in cash losses over 20 years (Editor & Publisher, 8 January 2026, https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/pittsburgh-post-gazette-to-publish-final-edition-and-cease-operations-may-3,259533). The Corporation for Public Broadcasting, which distributed federal money to more than 1,500 local public radio and TV stations, moved to dissolve that same week after Congress rescinded $1.07 billion in July 2025 (Columbia Journalism Review, 6 January 2026, https://www.cjr.org/analysis/what-the-dissolution-of-the-corporation-for-public-broadcasting-means.php; Poynter, 1 August 2025, https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/corporation-for-public-broadcasting-to-close-after-congress-pulls-funding/). On 20 August 2026 Australia’s Parliament passed a News Bargaining Incentive charging large platforms that do not strike deals with news publishers (Australian Treasury, https://ministers.treasury.gov.au/ministers/daniel-mulino-2025/media-releases/support-australian-journalism-passes-parliament), and on 30 September 2026 California’s governor signed the Community NEWS Act, refundable tax credits for newsrooms that keep or hire local journalists worth about $200 million over five years (Poynter, 1 October 2026, https://www.poynter.org/commentary/2026/california-community-news-act-local-journalism-tax-credits/). On 10 November 2026 the philanthropic coalition Press Forward announced it had invested nearly $600 million in local news, two years ahead of schedule (AP via WFTV, https://www.wftv.com/news/business/press-forward/BZJ6ARVJMAZQBCMNMEKF46NF4E/?outputType=amp), and on 17 November a federal appeals court heard Nexstar’s appeal of an injunction holding apart its $6.2 billion merger with Tegna, the country’s largest combination of local TV stations (NewscastStudio, 18 November 2026, https://www.newscaststudio.com/2026/11/18/court-questions-nexstar-effort-to-limit-restrictions-tegna-merger/).

The debate is live because almost no one disputes the decline and almost everyone disputes the remedy. Research links newspaper closures to higher municipal borrowing costs and less split-ticket voting, and private equity buyouts of papers to thinner local government coverage and lower turnout in county elections, but most Americans do not pay for local news and many assume their local outlets are doing fine. The argument runs between journalism advocates, press reformers and state legislators who want public policy, such as tax credits, government advertising and public media, to fund local reporting as a public good; news publishers and governments in Australia and Canada who want platforms and AI companies to pay for the news they distribute; broadcasters and the current Federal Communications Commission leadership, who argue local stations need scale to survive against Big Tech; and free-market and libertarian analysts who argue that subsidies and link taxes would turn watchdogs into clients and prop up a dead model, and that law should instead clear the way for new kinds of outlets. Philanthropy, which has poured hundreds of millions into nonprofit newsrooms, sits across these camps, and its own leaders say it cannot close the gap alone.

### 13. Municipal borrowing costs after a newspaper closes
- Label: Increase in municipal borrowing costs after a local newspaper closes
- Value: 5 to 11
- Unit: basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) on municipal bond yields
- Comparison rows: none
- Data year: closures 1996 to 2015; published 2020
- Source label: Pengjie Gao, Chang Lee and Dermot Murphy, “Financing dies in darkness? The impact of newspaper closures on public finance,” Journal of Financial Economics 135(2), 2020, pp. 445–467
- Source URL: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v135y2020i2p445-467.html

## Questions

### 1. Does losing local news measurably harm a community’s government and civic life?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: real civic harm / harm overstated
- Why contested: Every camp invokes the evidence, but it is thinner than the rhetoric. The best-identified studies find effects that are real but modest: municipal borrowing costs up 5 to 11 basis points after a daily closes (Gao, Lee and Murphy, Journal of Financial Economics, 2020), split-ticket voting down 1.9 percent (Darr, Hitt and Dunaway, Journal of Communication, 2018), and, after private equity buyouts, 23 percent fewer local government articles and turnout in county elections down 0.82 points from a mean of 9 percent (Ewens, Gupta and Howell, NBER, 2022). Most of this evidence comes from daily newspapers between the late 1990s and 2015, while most recent losses are rural weeklies, and none of it measures whether a startup, a TV station or a Facebook group restores what was lost.
- Real civic harm: Those who see real harm argue that the studies, using different methods and data, point the same way: when the reporter stops attending the meetings, officials pay themselves more, borrow more expensively and face fewer challengers, and voters fall back on national partisan cues. A few basis points on every bond issue, multiplied across thousands of counties, is real taxpayer money; small shifts in turnout and ticket-splitting add up across a country. The harms are also the hardest to see, because no one reports on the stories that go uncovered, which is why most Americans assume their local outlets are fine. Fewer than 50 US counties without a newspaper have a digital or broadcast outlet instead (Medill, 2026), so the gap is not being filled.
- Harm overstated: Those who think the harm is overstated argue that the effects found are small, come from a period when the local daily was the only source, and may partly reflect the economic decline that also kills newspapers. Many of the papers lost were thin weeklies full of press releases and wire copy rather than watchdog reporting, and local TV, which most people prefer for local news, has held its newsrooms. Residents now learn about their towns through local TV sites, newsletters, community Facebook groups, government livestreams and public records online. On this view the policy question is narrower than advocates make it: which specific functions, such as covering courts and councils, are actually going undone, and what is the cheapest way to supply them.

### 2. Should government money pay for local journalism?
- Tag: value
- Poles: fund it publicly / keep it independent
- Why contested: State programs have grown fast, from Illinois’s and New York’s payroll tax credits to California’s Community NEWS Act, signed in September 2026 and worth about $200 million over five years, while Congress ended federal funding for public broadcasting in 2025. Only 10 percent of Americans say government funding should be the main way news organizations make money (Pew, December 2025), and the press’s role is to check the government that would pay it.
- Fund it publicly: Those who favor public funding argue that local reporting is a public good, like roads or schools, that markets underprovide because its benefits go to everyone, including people who never read it. Other rich democracies, from the United Kingdom to Canada, spend far more per person on public media, and the United States itself subsidized newspapers through postal rates from 1792 and legal-notice requirements for two centuries. Programs can be designed to keep officials away from content: automatic, formula-based tax credits per journalist employed, as in Illinois and California, involve no one judging which outlet deserves money. Rebuild Local News’s Steven Waldman writes that these programs “don’t involve government officials making decisions about content” (Dædalus, summer 2026). Without some public money, the evidence of the past twenty years says the reporting simply will not exist in most of the country.
- Keep it independent: Those who put independence first argue that a newsroom dependent on the state for its payroll will hesitate to bite the hand that feeds it, and that politicians will learn to use eligibility rules, renewals and budget threats as leverage, as California’s cut to its own share of the Google deal and the federal defunding of public broadcasting both show in different ways. Subsidies tie journalists to officials rather than to readers they must persuade; “Just wait until the real audience is government,” J.D. Tuccille wrote (Reason, July 2024). Philanthropy, readers, advertising and new business models should carry the load; if government acts at all, it should remove legal barriers rather than write checks.

### 3. Can nonprofit and digital startups replace what local newspapers did?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: replacements are coming / gap keeps growing
- Why contested: Nonprofit newsrooms in the Institute for Nonprofit News network took in more than $750 million in 2025 (INN Index 2026), Press Forward has invested nearly $600 million since 2023, and hundreds of startups have launched in five years. But newspapers closed at more than twice the rate outlets launched in 2026 (AP’s account of Medill’s report), nearly 80 percent of the 287 startups of the past five years are in urban areas, and only nine new INN members began publishing in 2025.
- Replacements are coming: Those who expect replacement argue that the startups are young and growing: nonprofit revenue rose 14 percent in a year, individual giving is a rising share, and outlets such as the Texas Tribune, Spotlight PA, the Baltimore Banner and hundreds of smaller sites now do the statehouse and city hall reporting that chain-owned dailies abandoned. Networks that share back offices, statewide collaborations and philanthropic hubs in local community foundations are spreading the model beyond cities, and the obstacles, such as startup capital, legal status and access to credentials, are fixable. The comparison should be with what the hollowed-out papers were actually producing in their last years, not with their peak.
- Gap keeps growing: Those who see a growing gap argue that the numbers do not come close. The whole nonprofit sector’s revenue is under a tenth of what newspapers took in from advertising alone in 2022, philanthropy goes overwhelmingly to metro areas (98 percent of the dollars from the largest journalism grants, Medill 2025), and the places losing papers fastest are rural, poorer counties that cannot sustain a donor base. Press Forward’s own director says “You can’t nonprofit your way out of this problem.” Without a new and broad source of money, this camp argues, the map of news deserts will keep spreading regardless of how good the startups are.

### 4. Should technology and AI companies be made to pay for the news they use?
- Tag: value
- Poles: make platforms pay / keep links free
- Why contested: Australia’s 2021 bargaining code pushed Google and Meta into payment deals with Australian publishers, and in August 2026 Australia added a charge on large platforms that do not make deals; Canada’s 2023 Online News Act yields CA$100 million a year from Google, while Meta responded by blocking news in Canada. AI search makes the stakes larger: users clicked a traditional result in 8 percent of Google visits with an AI summary against 15 percent without (Pew, 2025), and traffic to large local newspaper sites fell almost by half from 2022 to 2026 (Medill). The US Journalism Competition and Preservation Act and California’s link-payment bill both stalled.
- Make platforms pay: Those who favor payment argue that Google, Meta and now AI companies captured the advertising that once funded local reporting and still profit from the news they display, summarize and train on, while sending ever less traffic back. Individual publishers cannot bargain with companies of that size, so law must set the terms, as Australia and Canada have. “Journalism is an essential part of a strong democracy, it holds people, businesses and governments to account,” Australia’s communications minister Anika Wells said when the incentive passed. Payment is not a subsidy but a price for a valuable input, and AI makes the case stronger, because a chatbot answer built on local reporting replaces the visit entirely.
- Keep links free: Those who oppose payment mandates argue that links send publishers traffic they choose to receive, that charging for them breaks the open web, and that the money flows mostly to large national publishers and broadcasters rather than to the small local outlets the laws claim to save. The advertising loss came from technological change, not wrongdoing: “Local media has not lost ad revenue because of anticompetitive practices by big tech,” Reason Foundation’s Max Gulker wrote (July 2023). Canada’s law produced a news blackout on the platforms where many people found local stories, and a charge that applies whether or not news appears on a platform is, critics say, a tax by another name.

### 5. Do readers value local news enough to pay for it?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: readers will pay / readers won’t pay
- Why contested: Only 15 percent of US adults paid or gave money to a local news source in a year, and 63 percent thought their local outlets were doing well financially (Pew, 2024); 8 percent say Americans have a responsibility to pay for news (Pew, December 2025). Yet individual giving is now a third of nonprofit newsroom revenue (INN, 2025), and some local outlets have built paying audiences.
- Readers will pay: Those who think readers will pay argue that low payment reflects an awareness gap, not indifference: most people do not know their outlet is in trouble, and when they learn, as membership drives and matching campaigns show, many give. Eighty-five percent say local news matters to their community, and trust in local news is higher than in any other source Pew asks about. Digital subscriptions, memberships, events and donations can sustain lean newsrooms if they offer something readers cannot get elsewhere, and outlets that have rebuilt around readers are less exposed to platform and government decisions.
- Readers won’t pay: Those who doubt it argue that local news was never paid for mainly by readers; subscriptions covered a fraction of costs, and advertisers bought the rest. A product whose benefits go to the whole community will always be underpaid by individuals who can free-ride, especially when free alternatives, from TV websites to social media, feel good enough. Paying audiences concentrate among older, wealthier and more educated readers, so a reader-funded model produces good journalism for those who can afford it and deserts everywhere else.

### 6. Should policy try to save existing newspapers or build new kinds of outlets?
- Tag: value
- Poles: rescue newspapers / build replacements
- Why contested: Many proposals, from advertising tax credits to link payments, flow mostly to existing publishers, including chains and hedge fund owners. R Street Institute’s Eli Lehrer argues that “a restorationist approach — focused on reviving legacy institutions — risks reinforcing the very dynamics that produced the collapse” (National Affairs, fall 2026), while advocates of employment tax credits answer that most of the remaining local journalists still work at newspapers.
- Rescue newspapers: Those who would sustain existing papers argue that they still employ most local reporters, hold archives, legal standing and community recognition, and cover places startups have not reached. Closing a paper destroys institutional knowledge that takes decades to rebuild, and once a county becomes a news desert, as Medill’s 2026 report puts it, the news “stays gone.” Policy that pays per journalist employed, regardless of owner or format, keeps reporters at work now while new models develop, and nothing in it stops a startup from qualifying too.
- Build replacements: Those who would build new outlets argue that the newspaper was an industrial-era bundle sustained by advertising monopolies and legal privileges that no longer exist, and that propping it up rewards owners who have already cut the newsroom to the bone. The better use of policy is to lower barriers to entry: a tax status that fits civic journalism, legal cooperation on back-office services, press credentials and shield-law protection based on reporting rather than employer, and help for public media stations, newsletters and community reporters to take on the watchdog role. On this view the goal is the function, not the institution.

### 7. Does consolidated ownership preserve local news or hollow it out?
- Tag: empirical
- Poles: scale preserves it / consolidation guts it
- Why contested: Ten companies control three quarters of local commercial TV newsrooms (Medill, 2026), and the FCC waived the national ownership cap in March 2026 to approve Nexstar’s purchase of Tegna, which states and DirecTV are fighting in court. In newspapers, private equity ownership rose from about 5 percent of papers in 2002 to roughly a fifth by 2019, and the study of its effects found fewer papers closing but less local government coverage (Ewens, Gupta and Howell, 2022).
- Scale preserves it: Those who see scale as preservation argue that local news production has strong economies of scale and that small owners cannot compete for advertising with Google, Meta and streaming services. “Scale is Not the Enemy of Localism. It is Often What Makes Localism Possible,” the National Association of Broadcasters told the Senate in February 2026. Shared technology, sales and production free money for reporters, and a struggling paper or station bought by a larger owner is often one that would otherwise have closed, as the finding that private equity ownership lowers the chance of exit suggests.
- Consolidation guts it: Those who see consolidation as the problem argue that buyers financed with debt pay for deals by cutting newsrooms and centralizing coverage. Nexstar projected $300 million a year in savings from combining Tegna, including consolidating programming teams in major markets (NPR, April 2026), and private equity buyouts were followed by fewer reporters and less local government news. A company that owns the stations in many markets can replace local reporting with shared national segments, and fewer owners means fewer independent editorial voices in each community.

### 8. Should federal taxpayers fund public broadcasting?
- Tag: value
- Poles: restore public funding / end federal funding
- Why contested: Congress rescinded $1.07 billion for the Corporation for Public Broadcasting in July 2025 and the corporation dissolved in January 2026, ending direct federal support for more than 1,500 local stations. Public radio is the only local news source in nine counties, and primary public radio stations reach 46 percent of news desert counties (Medill, 2025), and in March 2025, 43 percent of Americans favored continued federal funding for NPR and PBS, 24 percent opposed it and 33 percent were unsure (Pew).
- Restore public funding: Those who would restore funding argue that public media is the one local news infrastructure that already reaches nearly every county, including rural places no commercial outlet will serve, and that rural and tribal stations, which relied most on federal money, are the ones now at risk. The federal contribution was small: CPB’s budget was $535 million (R Street’s Lehrer, National Affairs, fall 2026), roughly a dollar and a half per American, and it leveraged local donations; losing it will close or shrink stations precisely in the places where local news is thinnest.
- End federal funding: Those who supported ending federal funding argue that taxpayers should not be compelled to fund broadcasters whose national programming many see as politically one-sided, and that a free press should not depend on appropriations from the government it covers. Federal money was a small share of the system’s revenue, local stations have loyal donors, and foundations have stepped in for those most at risk. Freed of federal mandates, stations can experiment with new models, collaborations and local reporting, as R Street’s Lehrer argues.

## Positions

Order note: the camp that would fund local reporting through public policy comes first because it is where most enacted US policy now sits: payroll tax credits passed by legislatures of both parties’ states (Illinois, New York, New Mexico, California), state advertising and fellowship programs, and philanthropic coalitions such as Press Forward that fund policy advocacy alongside newsrooms. The camp that would make platforms and AI companies pay comes second; it has the publishing industry and the governments of Australia and Canada behind it, though its US bills have stalled. The camp that would clear the way for new outlets instead of subsidies and mandates comes third: it rejects the question as usually posed, how to rescue the existing press, and argues that the remedy is to replace rather than restore. The camp that would lift ownership limits so local media can scale comes fourth; it is held mainly by broadcasters and the current FCC leadership, but it governs the largest live transaction in local news, Nexstar’s purchase of Tegna. Philanthropy runs across the first three camps rather than forming its own: its leaders say it cannot carry the load alone, and both the first and third camps count it as part of their answer. The camps overlap: Jeff Jarvis, for instance, opposes link payments (third camp) but supports tax credits for hiring journalists (first camp), and Rebuild Local News supports both tax credits and antitrust limits on newspaper consolidation.

### 1. Fund local reporting through public policy
- Adherents: Rebuild Local News (institution), a nonpartisan coalition that “advocates for nonpartisan public policies to increase the number of reporters covering schools, city hall” and sponsored California’s Community NEWS Act — https://www.rebuildlocalnews.org/ ; its president Steven Waldman writes that the new state programs “don’t involve government officials making decisions about content” because they are formula-based entitlements in the tax code (Dædalus, summer 2026) — https://www.amacad.org/daedalus/community-news-is-the-key ; Free Press (institution), which says local news is, “Like safe roads and strong schools,” “a public good,” and works to “protect public and community media” — https://www.freepress.net/issues/future-journalism ; economist Dean Baker of the Center for Economic and Policy Research, who wrote “there is an alternative: investing public dollars into journalism” and that “The key issue is to have the subsidy distributed in a way that protects the content from government interference” (The Nation, March 2024) — https://www.thenation.com/article/society/billionaires-wont-save-local-news-heres-what-will ; media scholar Victor Pickard of the University of Pennsylvania, who argues that “we’ve allowed our information systems to be governed primarily by market imperatives” and that a public media system “needs to be well-funded” and “independent” — https://www.asc.upenn.edu/news-events/news/democracy-without-journalism-qa-victor-pickard
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Local reporting is a public good. Everyone benefits when someone reads the school budget and attends the zoning hearing, including the people who never read the story, so a market will always produce less of it than a democracy needs. For a century advertising hid that fact by paying for newsrooms as a side effect of selling department-store and classified ads. That subsidy is gone and will not return, and twenty years of startups, paywalls and philanthropy have not replaced it: hundreds of counties now have no local news at all. The United States has always supported the press through public policy, from cheap postal rates for newspapers to legal-notice requirements to public broadcasting. The task now is to do it deliberately and safely: refundable tax credits for every local journalist employed, government advertising placed with local outlets, fellowships that put reporters in underserved places, and strong public media, all distributed by formula so that no official decides who deserves support.
- Variation inside the camp: which instrument, from payroll tax credits (Illinois, New York, New Mexico, California) to citizen vouchers that let each taxpayer direct a subsidy to the outlet of their choice (Baker) to a large public media system beyond NPR and PBS (Pickard, Free Press); whether money should reach commercial chains and hedge fund owners or only independent and nonprofit outlets; whether platform payments should feed public funds; and how far to pair funding with antitrust limits on newspaper consolidation, which Rebuild Local News supports.
- Best evidence: In its first year Illinois’s payroll credit supported more than 260 journalist jobs at more than 120 outlets for a little over $4 million (Rebuild Local News report, February 2026). Studies find that losing a local newspaper raises municipal borrowing costs by 5 to 11 basis points (Gao, Lee and Murphy, 2020) and reduces split-ticket voting (Darr, Hitt and Dunaway, 2018). Despite nearly $600 million from Press Forward and more than $750 million a year in nonprofit newsroom revenue, news desert counties rose by 26 in a year to 238 (Medill, 2026), and philanthropy goes overwhelmingly to cities. Canada has run a journalism labour tax credit since 2019, with an independent board advising on which outlets qualify, and its government proposed extending it in November 2026 (Nieman Lab, https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/11/its-either-this-or-death-frankly-canada-renews-the-local-journalism-initiative-and-extends-journalism-tax-credit/).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that money from government compromises a watchdog’s independence): The design is the safeguard. A credit claimed automatically for each journalist on payroll, open to any qualifying outlet regardless of viewpoint, gives officials nothing to reward or withhold, and it is no more corrupting than the tax deduction that underwrites every nonprofit newsroom or the postal subsidy that built the American press. Public broadcasters in many democracies are among the most trusted and independent news organizations in their countries. The real threat to independence is a county with no reporter at all.

### 2. Make platforms and AI companies pay for news
- Adherents: News/Media Alliance (institution), the US news publishers’ trade group, whose 2025 “Support Responsible AI” campaign asks Washington to “Require Big Tech and AI companies to fairly compensate content creators”; its chief executive Danielle Coffey said “We must continue to protect American creators from exploitation and abuse by Big Tech and AI companies” — https://www.newsmediaalliance.org/support-responsible-ai-campaign/ ; Coffey also called Google’s description of publishers as free riders “obviously ridiculous” (May 2026) — https://www.newsmediaalliance.org/google-free-riders-statement/ ; the Government of Australia (institution), whose News Bargaining Incentive, passed 20 August 2026, “encourages digital platforms with significant social media or search services to do deals with news businesses for the content they use” and “fixes a loophole in the News Media Bargaining Code where a digital platform could withdraw news”; Communications Minister Anika Wells said “Journalism is an essential part of a strong democracy, it holds people, businesses and governments to account” — https://ministers.treasury.gov.au/ministers/daniel-mulino-2025/media-releases/support-australian-journalism-passes-parliament . The Government of Canada’s Online News Act (2023) rests on the same principle (Reuters Institute, https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/how-googles-funding-canadian-news-publishers-split-and-who-benefits-most).
- Case in the camp’s own voice: The money that once paid for local reporting did not vanish; it moved to Google and Meta, which now take most digital advertising while displaying, summarizing and recommending the journalism that newsrooms pay to produce. Publishers cannot bargain individually with companies of that size, and refusing to be indexed means disappearing. Now AI search and chatbots answer readers’ questions with reporting drawn from news sites and send almost no one back to read it. This is not a subsidy and not a tax on links; it is a fair price for a valuable input, set through collective bargaining because the market is lopsided. Australia and Canada have shown it can be done: platforms have paid hundreds of news outlets, including small local ones, without the internet breaking.
- Variation inside the camp: whether to use bargaining codes that force negotiation (Australia’s 2021 code, the stalled US Journalism Competition and Preservation Act), a charge that platforms can offset by making deals (Australia’s 2026 incentive), a fixed payment into a collective fund distributed by newsroom size (Canada’s arrangement with Google), copyright litigation and licensing deals with AI companies, or state-brokered settlements such as California’s 2024 deal with Google; and whether money should be distributed by journalists employed, so local outlets get a share, or by negotiated deals, which favor the largest publishers.
- Best evidence: Google pays CA$100 million a year to more than 400 Canadian news organizations under the Online News Act (Reuters Institute, 2025). When Google showed an AI summary, users clicked a traditional result in 8 percent of visits against 15 percent without (Pew, 2025), and Tollbit data cited by the News/Media Alliance found AI chatbot click-through rates 95.7 percent lower than traditional Google search (News/Media Alliance, March 2025, https://www.newsmediaalliance.org/statement-new-report-shows-ai-chat-bots-provide-virtually-no-referral-traffic-to-publishers/). Traffic to 100 of the largest local newspaper sites fell almost by half from 2022 to 2026 (Medill, 2026).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that the money goes to big publishers and platforms respond by dropping news, as Meta did in Canada): Distribution can be designed for local outlets: Canada’s collective divides most of the money in proportion to newsroom size, so small papers receive a share. Meta’s blackout is why Australia’s new incentive applies whether or not a platform carries news: a platform cannot escape by withdrawing it. That a company would rather cut off its users from news than pay for it is evidence of its market power, not an argument against fair payment.

### 3. Clear the way for new outlets instead of subsidies and mandates
- Adherents: R Street Institute (institution), whose president Eli Lehrer argues that “A restorationist approach — focused on reviving legacy institutions — risks reinforcing the very dynamics that produced the collapse,” calls link payment mandates “Among the most misguided proposals,” warns that direct subsidies risk “turning watchdogs into clients,” and proposes “a structural realignment: one that treats journalism as a civic function and enables pluralistic, decentralized, legally protected experimentation” (National Affairs, fall 2026) — https://www.rstreet.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/How-to-Bring-Back-Local-News.pdf ; Reason Foundation (institution), whose Max Gulker wrote that “Local media has not lost ad revenue because of anticompetitive practices by big tech” and that “True adjustment requires innovation from firms experimenting with new business models and receiving market signals” (July 2023) — https://reason.org/commentary/protectionist-laws-will-not-save-local-news/ ; Reason magazine’s J.D. Tuccille, who wrote that with public money “journalists will cater to state officials rather than woo readers and viewers” (July 2024) — https://reason.com/2024/07/08/if-you-dont-trust-media-now-wait-until-its-government-funded/
- Case in the camp’s own voice: The local newspaper was never sustained by readers. It was a profitable accident of printing presses, advertising monopolies and legal privileges, and when the internet removed those, the institution collapsed. Its civic functions, tracking power, explaining events and holding a community’s memory, still matter, and their loss is real. But pouring money into the old form will not bring them back. Link payments flow mainly to large national publishers, invite governments to decide which links are allowed and, as Canada showed, can end with news cut off from the platforms where people find it. Direct subsidies make newsrooms dependent on the officials they are supposed to watch and are nearly impossible to end once started. The better path is to remove the barriers that keep new watchdogs from forming: a tax status that fits civic journalism, safe harbors so small outlets can share back-office services, press credentials and shield-law protection for anyone who does the reporting, and private philanthropy and experiments to show what works.
- Variation inside the camp: how much government involvement is tolerable, from none (Reason) to accepting payroll tax credits for local reporters as “the least-bad idea currently on offer,” best tested at state level (R Street); whether to rely mainly on market experimentation, on philanthropy such as Press Forward, which R Street praises, or on legal reforms such as a new 501(c) category for civic journalism, a replacement for the Newspaper Preservation Act and a national shield law; and whether the end of federal public broadcasting funding is an opening for local stations to experiment (R Street) or simply welcome.
- Best evidence: Digital-native newsroom employment rose 144 percent between 2008 and 2020 while newspaper newsrooms fell 57 percent (Pew, 2021). Canada’s Online News Act was followed by Meta blocking news, and Facebook’s use as a news source in Canada fell (Reuters Institute, 2025). Lehrer notes that by 2024 the global influencer economy, roughly $24 billion, was about the size of the US local newspaper industry, under $21 billion, and growing as newspapers shrink, which he reads as evidence that giving new publishers access and protection can work. Only 10 percent of Americans say government funding should be news organizations’ main source of money (Pew, December 2025).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that markets have had twenty years and the deserts keep spreading): The market that failed was the old one, shaped by rules written for printed newspapers. Startups face legal and institutional obstacles, from tax categories that bar political coverage to credentials and shield laws reserved for established outlets, that have nothing to do with demand. Remove those, let philanthropy test ideas such as subscription vouchers in a single city, and use the least distorting policies, such as hiring credits, only where evidence supports them. That is a faster route to watchdog reporting in rural counties than subsidies that keep hollowed-out chain papers alive.

### 4. Lift ownership limits so local media can scale
- Adherents: National Association of Broadcasters (institution), whose president Curtis LeGeyt told the Senate Commerce Committee on 10 February 2026 that “Scale is Not the Enemy of Localism. It is Often What Makes Localism Possible,” that ownership limits “undermine localism” by “keeping broadcasters artificially weak,” and that “localism is an expensive value” — https://www.commerce.senate.gov/services/files/6772D1F4-B9BE-45D1-9D37-08D85318DE36 ; Nexstar Media Group chief executive Perry Sook, who wrote that competing with Big Tech “requires scale — which is exactly why Nexstar pursued the acquisition of TEGNA” (Fortune, 14 June 2026) — https://fortune.com/2026/06/14/nexstar-tegna-local-tv-big-tech-advertising-perry-sook/ ; FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, who said “If you care about local news, you should care about the future of local broadcast TV stations” (NPR via NHPR, April 2026) — https://www.nhpr.org/2026-04-14/2-local-tv-giants-merged-then-a-court-stepped-in
- Case in the camp’s own voice: Local TV newsrooms held their staffing while newspapers shed more than half of theirs, and they deliver news, weather and emergency information free over the air. But they compete for audiences and advertising against YouTube, streaming services and digital platforms that reach every household and face none of broadcasting’s ownership rules. Local news production has strong economies of scale: shared technology, sales and graphics let a group owner spend more on reporters in each market than a stand-alone station can. Rules written in the analog era, such as the 39 percent national audience cap, keep broadcasters small at exactly the moment they need to grow. Letting stations combine is how local news survives; blocking deals does not preserve independent owners, it leaves weaker ones to shrink.
- Variation inside the camp: whether to lift the national cap entirely or raise it, and whether the FCC can waive it or only Congress can change it; whether to loosen local market limits on owning several stations in one city; and, for newspapers, whether to expand joint operating agreements or let financial buyers consolidate chains. Inside the camp, some favor approving deals with conditions attached, as the FCC’s March 2026 approval of Nexstar and Tegna did.
- Best evidence: Broadcast TV newsroom employment held roughly steady at about 28,000 from 2008 to 2020 while newspaper newsrooms fell by more than half (Pew, 2021). Nexstar and Tegna together employ nearly 9,000 journalists and hold about 15 percent of the more than 1,700 full-power US stations, Sook writes, while one Wall Street analyst predicts five digital companies will control 65 percent of a $260 billion ad market in 2026. In newspapers, private equity ownership lowered the chance that a paper would close (Ewens, Gupta and Howell, 2022).
- Answer to the strongest objection (that consolidation leads to layoffs, centralized coverage and fewer independent voices): The comparison is not between a merged company and a thriving independent station but between a merged company and a stand-alone owner losing audiences and advertisers every year. Savings in back-office functions, technology and programming are what allow continued investment in local newsrooms, and a group owner with scale can afford investigative units and weather and political coverage that small stations cannot. The competitors that matter are no longer the station across town but national platforms that employ no local reporters at all.

## Crux

Without the advertising that once paid for it, can local watchdog reporting be sustained in most communities by readers, philanthropy and new kinds of outlets alone, and if it cannot, which outside source of money, whether government, technology platforms or larger owners, is the least dangerous for a press whose job is to hold power to account?

What evidence could settle: Whether the startups, nonprofits and public media stations of the past decade are spreading beyond metro areas fast enough to cover the counties now losing newspapers, or whether, as in 2026, closures keep outpacing launches by two to one with almost all new outlets and philanthropy in cities; whether formula-based payroll tax credits such as those in Illinois, New York, New Mexico and California add reporters and local coverage, and whether recipients’ coverage of state government changes once they depend on them; whether platform payment schemes in Australia and Canada sustain small local outlets or mainly large publishers, and how platforms respond; whether consolidated broadcast and newspaper owners add or cut local news after mergers, measured in reporters and local stories rather than in promises; how much AI search reduces the traffic local outlets depend on; and how large the civic harms of losing local news are, and whether a replacement outlet reverses them, which the existing studies of daily-newspaper closures before 2015 cannot answer.

What no data can close: Whether local reporting is a public good that every community is owed regardless of what its residents will pay, or a service that should exist where people value it enough to support it; how much risk to a newsroom’s independence is worth accepting in exchange for having a newsroom at all; whether platforms and AI companies owe publishers for the use of their work or are being asked to pay for a decline they did not cause; and whether the goal is to keep existing newspapers and stations alive or to let them go and build something different. Those who would fund local reporting through public policy, those who would make platforms and AI companies pay, those who would clear the way for new outlets instead of subsidies and mandates, and those who would lift ownership limits so local media can scale read the same news desert maps and answer these questions differently.
