# AI & the Future of Work — research

## Overview

This topic is about what artificial intelligence does to paid work: which jobs it takes over, which it makes more productive, which new kinds of work it creates, and what is left for people to do as the systems improve. It covers the evidence on displacement and augmentation so far, the forecasts about the next decade, who is exposed and who is not, and the argument over what, if anything, governments, employers and unions should do about it: retraining, new taxes or funds that share AI’s gains, worker bargaining over how the tools are used, or limits on deployment. It deliberately leaves to neighboring topics the question of when or whether general-purpose AI arrives (AGI timelines), the design of a universal basic income as a policy in its own right, the broader fight over union power and wage inequality, and the use of AI in hiring and workplace surveillance, which belongs to algorithmic fairness. Those questions appear here only where they change the answer about jobs.

Why now: 2026 is the first year in which employers began telling the public, in large numbers, that AI was the reason they were cutting staff. On 26 February 2026 Block, the company behind Square and Cash App, cut about 4,000 of its more than 10,000 employees while reporting growing profits, and its chief executive Jack Dorsey wrote that “a significantly smaller team, using the tools we’re building, can do more and do it better” and predicted that most companies would make similar changes within a year (San Francisco Standard, https://sfstandard.com/2026/02/26/block-lays-off-staff/). From March through July 2026, artificial intelligence was the most-cited reason for announced US job cuts in the monthly tally kept by the outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas, five months running. Its report of 2 September 2026 put AI-attributed cuts for January to August at 116,175, about 22 percent of all announced cuts and more than double the 54,836 attributed to AI in all of 2025, although AI fell to fourth place in August itself (Challenger, Gray & Christmas, https://www.challengergray.com/blog/challenger-report-august-job-cuts-up-58-consumer-products-food-lead/).

The debate is live because the evidence points two ways at once. Announcements of AI-driven cuts are rising, a Stanford team using payroll records found in August 2026 that employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations had fallen 19 percent behind their peers in less exposed work, and 71 percent of Americans now expect AI to mean fewer jobs. Yet the Yale Budget Lab’s tracker, updated on 15 September 2026 with August data, finds no clear sign of economy-wide disruption, unemployment is 4.1 percent, and total announced layoffs in 2026 are running 41 percent below 2025. The argument has moved into legislatures: Connecticut will require employers to say whether AI contributed to a mass layoff from 1 October 2026 (Epstein Becker Green, https://www.ebglaw.com/workforce-bulletin/connecticut-joins-growing-number-of-states-regulating-workplace-ai-and-mandating-notice-for-certain-ai-uses-as-well-as-imposing-new-disclosure-requirements-for-certain-reductions-in-force), a bipartisan Senate bill from Josh Hawley and Mark Warner would require quarterly reporting of AI-related job cuts (https://www.hawley.senate.gov/hawley-warner-to-introduce-bipartisan-legislation-revealing-number-of-jobs-lost-to-ai/), and on 22 September 2026 Senator Mark Kelly said he would introduce a bill making large AI companies pay into a fund for displaced workers (KJZZ, https://www.kjzz.org/politics/2026-09-22/sen-kelly-to-introduce-bill-to-force-tech-companies-to-help-workers-displaced-by-ai). The participants are labor economists and forecasters, AI companies whose chief executives are among the loudest voices warning of displacement, technology investors and trade groups who expect a jobs boom, unions bargaining over how the tools are used, and a public that is growing more worried each year.

Note: Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations stands about 19 percent below where it would be had it kept pace with same-age workers in less exposed jobs; experienced workers show no comparable gap.
Context: This is the most cited piece of evidence that AI has begun to affect jobs, and it comes from ADP payroll records covering millions of US workers rather than a survey. The authors’ six findings cut both ways: they find no evidence of widespread, economy-wide displacement, but a steadily widening gap for the youngest workers in occupations such as software development and customer service, working mainly through fewer hires rather than more firings. The decline is concentrated in occupations where AI tends to substitute for human tasks; where AI mostly complements workers, employment is flat or rising. The gap survives excluding tech firms and controlling for interest rates and remote work, but the authors are candid that it shrinks when controlling for education, that some divergence predates ChatGPT, and that it is stronger in the ADP sample than in national surveys. They call the results descriptive early indicators, not causal estimates. The original August 2025 version reported a 13 percent relative decline using a different specification; the authors’ own restatement puts the gap at 15 percent with data through July 2025. The “0” for experienced workers is the authors’ description (“no comparable gap”), not a separately published figure.

Note: US employers attributed 116,175 announced job cuts to AI in the first eight months of 2026, about 22 percent of all announced cuts, against 54,836 in all of 2025.
Context: Challenger has tracked AI as a stated reason for layoffs since 2023. The count records what employers say in their announcements, not an independent judgment of cause, and Challenger itself cautions that “naming AI in a layoff announcement can win over investors,” so companies have a reason to cite it even when cost-cutting or over-hiring would explain the cut. AI was the leading reason in every month from March through July 2026, then fell to fourth place in August with 3,462 cuts, its lowest monthly total since December 2025. The rise is concentrated in technology companies, and it sits inside a smaller overall total: all announced cuts in January to August 2026 were 529,914, down 41 percent from 892,362 in the same months of 2025, and announced hiring plans were up 37 percent. For comparison, the full 2025 total of announced job cuts was 1,206,374 (https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Challenger-Report-December-2025.pdf). A contrasting record comes from New York State, which added a checkbox for AI-related layoffs to its mandatory WARN notices in March 2025: in the following year more than 160 companies filed notices and not one attributed its layoffs to AI (Hunton Andrews Kurth, https://www.hunton.com/hunton-employment-labor-perspectives/new-york-warn-act-no-ai-related-layoffs-reported-in-first-year-of-adding-ai-related-disclosure-to-the-system).

Note: The IMF estimates that almost 40 percent of jobs worldwide, and about 60 percent in advanced economies, are exposed to AI.
Context: “Exposed” does not mean “will be lost.” The IMF splits exposed jobs into those where AI is likely to complement the worker, such as a surgeon or a judge whose tasks are exposed but whose judgment and accountability are hard to hand to a machine, and those where it is likely to substitute, such as many clerical roles; in advanced economies it estimates roughly half of exposed jobs fall on each side. Rich countries are more exposed because more of their workers do cognitive office work, which also means they stand to gain the productivity benefits sooner. The IMF found women and college-educated workers more exposed but also better placed to benefit, and older workers less able to adapt. The source website blocks automated requests but loads normally in a browser; the 40, 60, 40 and 26 percent figures are from the IMF’s January 2024 blog summary by Kristalina Georgieva and should be confirmed against the note before use.

Context: The ILO index, published 20 May 2025, scores nearly 30,000 occupational tasks and is more conservative than the IMF because it separates a wide band of partial exposure from the small group of jobs where most tasks could be done by generative AI. The exposure is unequal by sex: in the highest-exposure category are 4.7 percent of women’s jobs against 2.4 percent of men’s globally, and 9.6 percent against 3.5 percent in high-income countries, because clerical and administrative work, the most exposed category, is disproportionately done by women.

Label: Evidence of AI-driven change in the overall US labor market
Value: No clear evidence
Unit: finding
Viz: bar
Data year: Current Population Survey data through August 2026, updated 15 September 2026
Source label: The Budget Lab at Yale, Tracking the Impact of AI on the Labor Market
Source URL: https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/tracking-impact-ai-labor-market
Note: Yale’s tracker finds that churn across occupations, AI exposure among the unemployed and AI usage data “all remain flat, lie within historical ranges, or continue along pre-AI trends.”
Context: This is the strongest current evidence against a visible AI jobs shock, and it is a qualitative finding rather than a single number, so the editor may prefer to use it as context for the Stanford tile rather than as its own. The Budget Lab measures how fast the mix of occupations in the US workforce is changing and compares it with earlier technology waves. Its first report, 33 months after ChatGPT’s release, found the occupational mix changing on a path only about 1 percentage point faster than during the arrival of the internet, when at most about 7 percent of workers would have needed to switch occupations between 1996 and 2002 to restore the earlier mix (https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/evaluating-impact-ai-labor-market-current-state-affairs). A New York Fed study of job postings published in May 2026 reached a similar conclusion: postings in AI-exposed occupations had been falling relative to others since before ChatGPT and did not accelerate after it, and entry-level postings in exposed occupations fell no faster than senior ones (https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/do-job-postings-show-early-labor-market-effects-of-ai/). The Yale and Stanford findings are not strictly contradictory: a narrow effect on young workers in a few occupations can exist without moving economy-wide measures.

Label: Productivity gain from an AI assistant in customer support
Value: 14
Unit: percent increase in issues resolved per hour
Viz: ranked
Series: Novice and low-skilled agents 34; All agents 14 (highlight); Experienced and highly skilled agents minimal
Data year: Study of 5,179 agents, published 2025
Source label: Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, “Generative AI at Work,” Quarterly Journal of Economics (NBER Working Paper 31161)
Source URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
Note: Customer support agents given an AI assistant resolved 14 percent more issues per hour on average, and the least experienced agents 34 percent more.
Context: This field study at a real company is the best-known evidence for augmentation, and its most discussed feature is who gained. The assistant appears to have captured the practices of the best agents and passed them to the newest ones, compressing the gap between them, while doing little for the experts. Readers draw opposite conclusions from the same result. For the augmentation case, it shows AI raising the floor and helping less experienced workers. For the displacement case, it shows AI encoding expert know-how so that fewer experienced people are needed, and making junior roles easier to automate outright once the assistant is good enough. A randomized experiment on professional writing tasks with 453 college-educated professionals found similar direction: ChatGPT cut average time taken by 40 percent and raised output quality by 18 percent (Noy & Zhang, Science, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37440646).

Value: 19
Unit: percent longer to complete tasks
Viz: bar
Data year: Tasks done February to June 2025, published 10 July 2025
Source label: METR, “Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity”
Source URL: https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/
Note: In a randomized trial, 16 experienced open-source developers took 19 percent longer on 246 tasks when allowed AI tools, while believing the tools had sped them up by 20 percent.
Context: This small, carefully designed study became the main counterweight to the productivity studies, because it measured experts on their own mature codebases rather than novices on standardized tasks, and because it showed how badly self-reports can mislead: developers expected a 24 percent speedup and still believed in a 20 percent speedup after being slowed down. The tile is useful as a caution about measurement and about how fast results age, not as a current estimate.

## Questions

Question: Is AI already taking jobs, or are the early warning signs something else?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Already displacing / Not yet visible
Why contested: The same period produced rising AI-attributed layoff announcements, a widening employment gap for young workers in exposed occupations, and aggregate data that show no break from pre-AI trends. Each side can point to a careful study, and the studies differ in data source, in which workers they look at, and in how they measure exposure, so they are not simply contradicting each other.
Case for already displacing: The Stanford payroll study follows millions of actual paychecks rather than survey answers, and it finds employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most exposed occupations 19 percent below trend by mid-2026, a gap that has widened every quarter since it was first reported, that is concentrated in occupations where AI substitutes for tasks rather than complements them, and that survives controls for tech-sector layoffs, interest rates and remote work. That is exactly the pattern a displacement story predicts: firms stop hiring juniors for work a model can do, while keeping the experienced staff who supervise the model. Employers are now saying so openly. AI was the most-cited reason for announced US layoffs for five straight months in 2026, and chief executives such as Block’s Jack Dorsey describe their cuts as the direct result of what the tools can do. Recent college graduates have higher unemployment than the workforce as a whole, reversing the historical pattern. Aggregate statistics are the last place a shift like this shows up, because it starts at the margin of hiring in a handful of occupations and takes years to accumulate into the headline rate.
Case for not yet visible: Economy-wide, nothing looks unusual. The Yale Budget Lab’s tracker, updated in September 2026 with August data, finds the occupational mix, the exposure of the unemployed and AI usage all flat, within historical ranges, or on pre-AI trends. A New York Fed study found that job postings in AI-exposed occupations were already falling before ChatGPT and did not accelerate afterward, and that entry-level postings fell no faster than senior ones. The young-worker gap in the Stanford data shrinks when education is controlled for, shows some divergence before generative AI existed, and is larger in the ADP sample than in national surveys, as the authors themselves note. The weak market for new graduates has simpler explanations: the post-2022 hiring freeze in tech after pandemic over-hiring, higher interest rates, and a general slowdown in hiring that always hits first-time job seekers hardest. Layoff announcements that cite AI are a statement to investors, not a measurement; New York’s mandatory layoff notices, which ask the question directly, recorded not a single AI-attributed layoff in their first year. Unemployment is 4.1 percent.

Question: Will AI create enough new work to replace the work it automates, as earlier technologies did?
Tag: empirical
Poles: New work will come / This time is different
Why contested: History strongly favors the optimists: two centuries of automation left employment rates high and most people in jobs that did not exist before. But the case for “this time is different” rests on a specific claim about the technology, that it is aimed at cognition itself, which is also where new work has been created in recent decades. Only the future can settle it, and the relevant future may be decades long.
Case for new work will come: Roughly 60 percent of US employment in 2018 was in job titles that had not existed in 1940. Each wave of automation, from mechanized farming to computers, was accompanied by confident predictions of mass unemployment that did not come true, including the 2013 Oxford estimate that 47 percent of US jobs were at high risk. The mechanism is well understood: automation lowers prices, freed-up spending creates demand elsewhere, and new products create occupations no one could have named in advance. Human wants are not fixed, and many are for things that are valued precisely because a person provides them: care, teaching, performance, craftsmanship, trust. Where AI is deployed as a tool, demand for the humans who use it often rises: radiology, which Geoffrey Hinton said in 2016 should stop training new people, now faces a shortage of radiologists (The New Republic, https://newrepublic.com/article/187203/ai-radiology-geoffrey-hinton-nobel-prediction). Postings requiring AI skills are growing much faster than the job market and pay a large premium.
Case for this time is different: Earlier machines replaced muscle and routine, and people moved into work that required judgment, language and learning. A technology that does judgment, language and learning competes with the very tasks new work has been built from. The history is also less reassuring than it looks: the same study that found 60 percent of jobs are new finds that automation’s job-destroying effect has intensified since 1980 while the creation of new augmenting work has not kept pace, and that new jobs increasingly land at the top and bottom of the wage scale rather than the middle. Horses were an input to the economy for thousands of years until the engine made them uncompetitive at every price; the question is whether any human task stays out of reach once machines improve every year. Even if new work eventually comes, “eventually” can mean a generation, as with the manufacturing towns that never recovered from automation and trade, and the people displaced are rarely the people hired.

Question: Does AI mostly make workers more productive, or mostly do their work instead?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Mostly augments / Mostly replaces
Why contested: The same tool can do either depending on how an employer deploys it, and the studies measure different things: individual productivity in controlled settings, how people use chatbots, and what happens to employment. Firms’ incentives, not the technology alone, decide which way it goes.
Case for mostly augments: The strongest field evidence shows workers with AI doing more, not being removed: customer support agents resolved 14 percent more issues per hour, with the least experienced gaining 34 percent; professionals finished writing tasks 40 percent faster at higher quality. Just over half of conversations with a leading AI assistant involve people iterating and learning rather than delegating. The IMF estimates that in advanced economies about half of exposed jobs are likely to be complemented rather than replaced. Most jobs are bundles of tasks, and automating some tasks usually shifts a worker’s time to the others rather than eliminating the job; the ILO’s index finds only 3.3 percent of global employment in the highest-exposure category. The wage premium for AI skills is rising, which is not what replacement looks like.
Case for mostly replaces: Productivity per worker and the number of workers are two different things: if each agent resolves 14 percent more issues, a firm with fixed demand needs fewer agents. The field study’s own result, that AI helps novices and does little for experts, means the tool is encoding the experts’ know-how, which is the first step toward not needing to hire the novice at all. Augmentation dominates in consumer chat, but automation dominates in the programmatic business traffic through which firms build AI into processes, and the Stanford data show employment falling specifically in occupations where AI substitutes for tasks. The direction of the technology is being set by companies whose stated product goal is agents that complete whole tasks without supervision. Forty-one percent of employers told the World Economic Forum they expect to downsize their workforce as AI capabilities expand.

Question: How fast will AI change the labor market?
Tag: empirical
Poles: Within a few years / Over decades
Why contested: Past general-purpose technologies took decades to show up in productivity and employment statistics, and adoption surveys suggest firms are still early. But AI capability is improving on a timescale of months, workers adopted it faster than any previous workplace tool, and leaders of the companies building it predict large effects within five years. The answer determines whether policy should be designed now or can wait for evidence.
Case for within a few years: Worker adoption went from 21 percent to 52 percent of US employees in three years, and AI is software delivered over the internet rather than machinery that must be built and installed. Capabilities are compounding: on a benchmark of real software engineering tasks, AI performance rose from 4.4 percent to 71.7 percent between 2023 and 2024, as cited by the Stanford researchers from the AI Index, and agents that can work for hours without supervision arrived in 2025 and 2026. People building the systems expect fast change: Anthropic’s chief executive Dario Amodei said in May 2025 that AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs and push unemployment to 10 to 20 percent within one to five years. Companies are already restructuring around the tools, and the Stanford gap for young workers widened every quarter. Waiting for aggregate statistics to confirm the shift means acting after the damage.
Case for over decades: Electricity took roughly forty years to transform factory productivity because firms had to reorganize around it, and the same is true of computers. Only 17 to 20 percent of US businesses report using AI in any function, and only 14 percent of workers in AI-adopting organizations strongly agree it has transformed how work gets done. Deployment in the real economy is slowed by regulation, liability, reliability, data, legacy systems and the need to redesign work, none of which improves at the speed of model capability; Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that transformative economic impacts will be slow, “on the timescale of decades” (https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology). Acemoglu’s estimate that AI will add about 1 percent to US GDP over ten years reflects how few tasks can yet be automated profitably. Past predictions of imminent disruption, including Hinton’s on radiology and the 2013 automation estimates, were wrong mainly about timing.

Question: Who should capture the gains when AI raises productivity?
Tag: value
Poles: Share through public mechanisms / Leave to markets
Why contested: If AI raises output while reducing demand for some labor, the gains flow to the owners of AI systems and the capital that uses them unless something redirects them. Whether that is a problem to correct, and by what means, is a question about fairness and incentives that no forecast can answer.
Case for share through public mechanisms: The models were trained on the accumulated writing, code and knowledge of millions of people who were not paid for it, and they are being deployed to reduce demand for those same people’s labor. If the extreme scenarios are even partly right, labor’s share of national income could fall steeply; one modeled scenario has it dropping from 60 to 45 percent by 2030. The tax system currently makes this worse: labor is taxed more heavily than capital in the United States, so a firm that replaces a worker with software often saves on taxes as well as wages. Mechanisms to share the gains range from a fund paid for by AI companies to retrain displaced workers, through taxes on automation or AI revenue, to a public wealth fund that gives every citizen a stake. Proposals of this kind now come not only from Senator Bernie Sanders but from OpenAI itself and from the chief executive of Anthropic.
Case for leave to markets: The gains from productivity reach people mainly as lower prices, higher wages for workers whose output rises, and new products and jobs, and they do so without any new tax. Taxes aimed at AI or automation are hard to define, since nearly all software automates something, and they would slow the adoption that produces the gains, pushing investment and talent to countries that do not impose them. The history of redistribution designed around a predicted crisis is that the crisis often does not arrive in the form expected; building new permanent programs on scenarios that their own authors decline to attach probabilities to risks large costs for a problem that may not materialize. Existing tools, including unemployment insurance, the tax code and education funding, can be adjusted if displacement actually appears in the data.

Question: Should workers have a say in whether and how AI is used in their jobs?
Tag: value
Poles: Decide jointly / Employers decide
Why contested: The choice between augmenting and replacing workers is made inside firms, and who makes it shapes the outcome. The disagreement is about property, management rights and the role of unions, and it maps closely onto the older argument about labor power.
Case for decide jointly: Workers know which parts of their jobs a tool can improve and which it will degrade, and when they are consulted, deployment tends to augment rather than replace. The 2023 Writers Guild contract, which bars studios from using AI to write or rewrite scripts while allowing writers to use it, shows that bargaining can steer a technology without stopping it. Economists including Daron Acemoglu, David Autor and Simon Johnson argue that AI is currently on a path biased toward automation by tax incentives, by the business models of a few large firms and by managers’ preference for control, and that worker voice is one of the few forces able to push it toward tools that make human expertise more valuable. Employers who decide alone capture the savings and leave the costs of displacement to workers and the public.
Case for employers decide: Firms bear the risk of their investments and answer to customers and competitors; requiring negotiation over every tool slows adoption and hands a veto to incumbents who have reason to resist change regardless of its merits. Most US workers are not in unions, so bargaining-based protections reach few of them and can entrench a gap between protected insiders and everyone else. When productivity rises, competition for workers who use AI well raises their pay without anyone having to bargain for it. Where specific uses cause harm, such as opaque algorithmic firing, targeted regulation works better than a general right to co-decide technology.

Question: Is a future with much less paid work something to prevent or to welcome?
Tag: value
Poles: Preserve work / Welcome less work
Why contested: Almost everyone agrees that losing income is bad. The disagreement is about work itself: whether a job is mainly a means to income, which a transfer could replace, or also a source of purpose, structure, status and social connection that money cannot.
Case for preserve work: Work is how most adults contribute, earn standing and structure their days, and the evidence on what happens when work disappears is grim: the communities that lost manufacturing jobs saw rising deaths of despair, family breakdown and political alienation that income support did not repair. A society in which a minority of AI owners produce most of the output and everyone else lives on transfers concentrates power dangerously, because people who are not needed as workers have less leverage as citizens. Policy should therefore aim to keep people in meaningful work, by steering AI toward augmentation and by investing in the care, education and craft jobs that people value being done by people.
Case for welcome less work: For most of history most people worked because they had to, at jobs they would not have chosen. A technology that can do much of that work is an opportunity to give people more time for family, community, learning and chosen effort, as the shift from six-day weeks to five-day weeks did in the last century. The problems attributed to lost work in declining industrial towns are problems of lost income, status and security, which policy can address directly. Proposals for a four-day week at full pay, which appear in both Senator Sanders’s agenda and OpenAI’s 2026 policy paper, take this view: the gains from AI should be taken partly as time rather than only as output.

Question: Should AI deployment be slowed or limited to protect jobs?
Tag: value
Poles: Slow it down / Let it run
Why contested: The benefits of faster deployment, including cheaper services and faster growth, are spread across everyone, while the costs fall heavily on particular workers and places. Weighing a diffuse gain against a concentrated loss is a judgment about fairness and about how much a society owes those whose jobs it lets go.
Case for slow it down: Transitions that happen too fast overwhelm the institutions meant to cushion them; retraining takes years and people in mid-career rarely recover their earnings after displacement. Requiring a trained human operator in heavy autonomous trucks, as the Teamsters union is seeking in California, Illinois and Colorado, protects both public safety and good jobs while the technology proves itself (https://teamster.org/av/). Slowing the build-out of the infrastructure behind AI, as Senator Sanders has proposed with a moratorium on new data centers, gives democratic institutions time to decide what they want before a handful of companies decides for them. There is no economic law requiring every technically possible substitution to happen as soon as it is profitable.
Case for let it run: Every delay has a cost that is less visible than a lost job: slower medical diagnosis, more expensive legal help, slower science, and forgone growth that would have paid for the support displaced workers need. Restrictions protect the workers in today’s jobs at the expense of consumers and of the workers who would have filled tomorrow’s, and they are hard to lift once an industry is built around them. Other countries will not slow down, so restrictions shift where the technology is developed rather than whether. Better to let productivity rise and help the people affected directly, through income support and retraining, than to freeze the economy’s structure in place.

## Positions

Camp: Speed AI adoption and help workers adapt through skills
Adherents: The White House, America’s AI Action Plan, July 2025, which calls for “a worker-first AI agenda” built on AI literacy, skills development and rapid retraining while accelerating adoption (https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Americas-AI-Action-Plan.pdf); the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, “New Evidence Contradicts Myth that AI Is Destroying Jobs,” 30 June 2026, which urges a national AI adoption plan and argues that “intensive AI adoption enables firms to expand rather than shrink their workforce” (https://itif.org/publications/2026/06/30/new-evidence-contradicts-myth-that-ai-is-destroying-jobs/); Marc Andreessen, “Why AI Will Save the World,” June 2023, which predicts “record job and wage growth – the exact opposite of the fear” (https://a16z.com/ai-will-save-the-world/)
Case in its own voice: Every generation has been told that this machine will be the one that ends work, and every generation has been wrong, because the fear rests on a fallacy: that there is a fixed amount of work to go around. Automation makes things cheaper, people spend what they save on other things, and that spending creates jobs, many of them in occupations nobody could have imagined. Most of the jobs Americans hold today did not exist in 1940. AI is the most powerful productivity tool in generations, and the country that adopts it fastest will have the fastest-growing economy, the highest wages and the most new work. The right response is to get the tools into the hands of every worker and every business, teach AI skills in schools, community colleges and apprenticeships, and help the people whose tasks change to move into the work that grows. What would actually hurt workers is the alternative on offer: taxes and restrictions that slow adoption at home while other countries, above all China, push ahead. Some in this camp, such as the computer scientists Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, are not boosters at all; they argue AI is a “normal technology” whose economic effects will unfold over decades, like electricity, which makes emergency labor policy premature for a different reason.
Best evidence: US unemployment is 4.1 percent, and the Yale Budget Lab’s September 2026 update finds no clear evidence of AI-related labor market disruption. Roughly 60 percent of US employment in 2018 was in job titles added since 1940. The best field studies find large productivity gains for the workers who use AI, especially less experienced ones. ITIF’s analysis of 2024 found AI associated with about 119,900 jobs created against 12,700 layoffs, and PwC’s 2026 barometer found headcount at AI-exposed companies growing faster since 2018 than at less exposed ones, with a 62 percent wage premium for AI skills. Forecasts of mass automation have a poor record: the decade in which 47 percent of US jobs were said to be at high risk has passed with employment near record highs.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that young workers in exposed occupations are already being squeezed out, and that this time the technology is aimed at cognition itself. We take the entry-level data seriously, and they are exactly why the answer is skills: the Stanford gap is concentrated where firms use AI to substitute rather than complement, and the remedy is to teach new entrants to be the people who direct AI, which is what employers are now hiring for. But the evidence does not yet show an economy-wide effect, the gap shrinks when education is controlled for, and the hiring slump for graduates began with the post-pandemic tech correction. Building permanent new taxes and programs on scenarios their own authors will not attach probabilities to would impose certain costs to address a speculative harm, and would slow the very growth that creates new work.

Camp: Give workers a say in how AI is used on the job
Adherents: The AFL-CIO, Workers First Initiative on AI, launched 15 October 2025, whose principles include strengthening collective bargaining, guardrails against harmful workplace AI and worker voice in AI research and development (https://aflciotechinstitute.org/news-media/afl-cio-releases-ai-principles-workers; full report at https://aflcio.org/reports/workers-first-ai, which blocks automated requests); Daron Acemoglu, David Autor and Simon Johnson, “Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence,” NBER Working Paper 34854, February 2026, and their 2023 memo “Can We Have Pro-Worker AI?” (https://www.nber.org/papers/w34854; https://shapingwork.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Pro-Worker-AI-Policy-Memo.pdf)
Case in its own voice: Whether AI makes workers more valuable or makes them unnecessary is not a fact about the technology. It is a choice made by the people who design and deploy it, and right now workers are not in the room. The same model can help a nurse catch errors or be used to schedule fewer nurses; it can make a junior analyst as good as a senior one or eliminate the junior analyst. Left to themselves, firms lean toward replacement, because the tax code taxes labor far more heavily than equipment and software, because a few large companies building AI sell it on the promise of cutting headcount, and because managers value control. That is not a law of economics; it is a set of incentives, and they can be changed. We are not against AI. As the AFL-CIO’s president, Liz Shuler, put it, “we reject the false choice between American competitiveness and respecting workers’ rights and dignity.” We want bargaining rights over the introduction of new technology, transparency about how it is used, protections against surveillance and algorithmic discipline, a tax code that stops favoring automation over hiring, and public research money directed toward tools that expand what workers can do. The deepest version of the argument, which Acemoglu and Johnson make in their history of technology, is that shared prosperity from past innovations came not automatically but because workers had the power to claim a share.
Best evidence: The Stanford payroll data show employment falling for young workers in occupations where AI substitutes for tasks while holding steady or rising where it complements them, which is direct evidence that the mode of deployment, not the technology alone, decides the outcome. The 2023 Writers Guild of America contract, won after a strike, bars studios from using AI to write or rewrite scripts or to reduce writers’ credit while allowing writers to choose to use it (https://www.wga.org/contracts/know-your-rights/artificial-intelligence), a working example of steering rather than stopping. Autor and colleagues found that since 1980 automation’s job-destroying effect has grown while new augmenting work has not kept pace, the imbalance a pro-worker agenda aims to correct. The customer support study found the largest gains when AI helped less experienced workers learn, the model of augmentation this camp wants to make the norm.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that giving workers a say will slow adoption, protect incumbents and reach only the minority of workers who have unions. We are not asking for a veto; the Writers Guild contract did not stop AI in Hollywood, it set terms. Technology introduced with the involvement of the people who use it tends to be adopted more successfully, not less, because they know where it helps. On coverage, the objection is an argument for extending bargaining and enforceable standards to more workers, not for leaving everyone without a voice; transparency rules, disclosure of AI-related layoffs and procurement standards reach non-union workers too. And the alternative is not neutral: it is a decision made by a handful of firms whose business model is labor replacement.

Camp: Share AI’s gains through new public funds and taxes
Adherents: OpenAI, “Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age: Ideas to Keep People First,” 6 April 2026, which proposes a public wealth fund that gives Americans “an automatic public stake in AI companies and AI infrastructure,” higher taxes on AI-driven returns and capital, an automatically triggered safety net and a subsidized four-day week (https://openai.com/index/new-policy-ideas-for-the-intelligence-age/, which blocks automated requests; summarized by TechCrunch at https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/06/openais-vision-for-the-ai-economy-public-wealth-funds-robot-taxes-and-a-four-day-work-week/); Senator Mark Kelly, AI for America and the AI Horizon Fund, a trust fund paid for by frontier AI companies to fund training, unemployment assistance and support for displaced workers, with a bill announced 22 September 2026 (https://www.kelly.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/in-the-news-kelly-releases-ai-for-america-a-roadmap-for-lasting-leadership-that-benefits-all-americans/; https://www.kjzz.org/politics/2026-09-22/sen-kelly-to-introduce-bill-to-force-tech-companies-to-help-workers-displaced-by-ai); Dario Amodei, chief executive of Anthropic, who in May 2025 proposed that perhaps 3 percent of AI companies’ revenue from model use go to the government to be redistributed (Axios, https://www.axios.com/2025/05/28/ai-jobs-white-collar-unemployment-anthropic, which blocks automated requests); Senator Bernie Sanders, whose October 2025 HELP Committee minority report proposed a “robot tax” on automation that replaces workers (https://www.sanders.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/10.6.2025-The-Big-Tech-Oligarchs-War-Against-Workers.pdf)
Case in its own voice: We do not know how fast AI will displace work, but we know which direction the gains flow if nothing changes: to the companies that own the models and the capital that deploys them. These systems were built from the accumulated knowledge of everyone, and they are being used to reduce demand for the labor of the people whose knowledge built them. Even in a good outcome, with growth and new jobs, the transition will be hard for millions, and the old tools, unemployment insurance that runs out in months and retraining programs with modest results, were not designed for it. In a bad outcome, where much cognitive work becomes cheaper to do by machine, wages cannot be the main way most people share in national income. So the public should hold a stake now, while the industry is being built, rather than trying to claw it back later: a fund paid for by the largest AI companies, taxes that shift from labor toward capital and AI returns, and a safety net that expands automatically when displacement shows up in the data. Senator Kelly’s argument is that companies growing rich from a technology trained on humanity’s shared research, knowledge and work owe some of that wealth back to the workers it displaces. The camp runs from a transition fund aimed at getting people back into work, the Kelly approach, which sits close to the adoption-and-skills camp, to permanent dividends that partly separate income from work, as in Sam Altman’s 2021 proposal for an American Equity Fund paying every adult citizen out of taxes on company value and land (https://moores.samaltman.com/). Sanders adds a shorter working week and worker ownership to the list.
Best evidence: In the Anthropic Institute’s extreme scenario, labor’s share of national income falls from 60 to 45 percent by 2030, and even in the substantial scenario cognitive workers’ wages stay flat while the economy grows faster: the gains exist but do not reach wages. The Stanford data show the first effects landing on people at the start of their careers, the group with the least savings and the fewest protections. Seventy-one percent of Americans now expect AI to mean fewer jobs. Alaska’s Permanent Fund has paid every resident a share of state resource wealth since 1982, showing a public stake in a new source of wealth is administratively simple. The most striking evidence is who is proposing it: two of the leading AI developers have publicly called for taxes and public funds of this kind.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that new taxes on AI will slow the adoption that produces the gains, and that the crisis they are designed for may never come. The better designs answer both. A public wealth fund that takes equity rather than taxing use does not raise the cost of adopting AI at all. A safety net that triggers automatically on measured job loss costs little if displacement never materializes and scales up only if it does. Taxing capital and AI returns at rates closer to labor does not penalize AI; it removes a subsidy for replacing workers that exists today. The risk is asymmetric: if we build these mechanisms and AI’s effects are mild, we have spent modestly; if we do not and the effects are severe, we will be designing them in the middle of a crisis, as happened after the loss of manufacturing jobs, when the help came too little and too late.

Camp: Slow or limit AI deployment to protect jobs
Adherents: The International Brotherhood of Teamsters, which is campaigning in California, Illinois and Colorado to require a trained human operator in autonomous commercial vehicles to “protect good jobs” and public safety (https://teamster.org/av/); Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, the AI Data Center Moratorium Act, announced 25 March 2026, which would pause AI data center construction until, among other conditions, the economic gains benefit workers (https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-ocasio-cortez-announce-ai-data-center-moratorium-act/)
Case in its own voice: Nobody voted for this. A handful of companies are racing to deploy a technology whose builders say it could eliminate half of entry-level office jobs within five years, and the rest of the country is told to retrain and hope. The pace is the problem. Displaced workers rarely recover their earnings, communities that lose their main employer can take a generation to recover, and none of the programs meant to cushion the blow can work at the speed these companies are moving. There is no economic law that says every task a machine can do must be handed to a machine the moment it becomes profitable. Societies decide all the time that some things should not be done, or not yet, or not without conditions: we require licensed pilots, licensed doctors and trained drivers of heavy vehicles. Requiring a human operator in an 80,000-pound autonomous truck protects the public and protects a job that supports a family. Pausing the build-out of AI data centers until the gains are shared and the risks understood gives democracy time to set the terms, instead of accepting whatever the market delivers. We are not against technology; we are against letting a few billionaires decide the future of work for everyone else.
Best evidence: Leaders of AI companies themselves forecast rapid displacement, including Dario Amodei’s May 2025 warning of unemployment of 10 to 20 percent within one to five years. Employers attributed more than 116,000 announced US job cuts to AI in the first eight months of 2026, more than double the full 2025 total. The public is on this side of the argument in large numbers: 71 percent of Americans expect AI to reduce jobs, and in 34 of 37 countries surveyed by Pew more people expect fewer jobs than more. Past rapid shocks, from the automation of manufacturing to the surge of imports from China after 2000, left lasting damage to employment and earnings in the places they hit, which retraining programs did not repair.
Answer to the strongest objection: The objection is that slowing AI throws away cheaper services and faster growth, protects today’s workers at the expense of tomorrow’s, and simply hands the technology to other countries. We are not proposing to ban AI; we are proposing that specific deployments meet conditions first, human operators where lives are at stake, and a pause in the physical build-out until the public has a say in how the gains are shared. Those are the kinds of rules every industry lives under. The growth argument assumes the gains will be shared, and so far the companies promising growth are the ones announcing the layoffs. As for other countries, the European Union regulates AI and remains a leading economy; a race to the bottom is a choice, not a law of nature.

## Crux

Beneath every disagreement in this topic sits one question: will AI remain a tool that people use, so that the economy keeps finding new things for human beings to do, or will it become a substitute for human thinking across enough of the economy that demand for most people’s labor falls faster than new work appears? Every camp agrees AI will change jobs. The adoption-and-skills camp expects the historical pattern to hold, with churn, some painful transitions and eventually more and better work, so the task is to help people move. The worker-voice camp thinks the answer is not predetermined and depends on who controls deployment. The gains-sharing camp thinks the pattern may break, or at least that the transition will be long enough and the gains concentrated enough that the public needs a stake either way. The slow-down camp thinks the pace alone will do damage whatever the long-run answer. If it became clear that AI will stay mostly complementary and diffuse at the speed of past technologies, much of the case for new funds, taxes and pauses would fall away; if it became clear that it will substitute broadly within a decade, much of the case for relying on skills and markets would.

What evidence could settle: how much of the work people are paid for AI can actually do at lower cost, how quickly firms reorganize around it, and whether displaced tasks are replaced by new ones. These are measurable, and the measurement has started. Payroll data can track whether the gap for young workers in exposed occupations keeps widening, stabilizes or spreads to older workers and other occupations; occupational-churn trackers can show whether the economy-wide mix of jobs breaks from its historical pace; firm surveys and AI usage data can show whether deployment shifts from assisting workers to completing whole tasks without them; and wage data can show whether the gains reach pay or only profits. Mandatory disclosure of AI-related layoffs, now arriving in Connecticut and proposed in Congress, would add a direct measure that does not depend on what companies choose to tell investors. Three to five more years of these series would narrow the forecasts a great deal. What they cannot do quickly is answer the long-run question of whether new kinds of work will appear, because in past transitions the new work took decades to show up and could not be named in advance.

What evidence cannot settle: how to weigh faster growth against security for the people displaced, and who has a rightful claim on the gains: the firms that took the risk of building and deploying the systems, the workers whose jobs they absorb, or the public whose accumulated knowledge they were trained on. One person looks at a cheaper legal service, a faster diagnosis and a larger economy and counts those gains as the point, with displacement as a cost to be eased. Another looks at the same change and asks why the people whose work trained the systems, and whose jobs they absorb, should bear the loss while a few firms keep the return. Behind that lies a still older question: whether paid work is mainly a means to income, which a fund or a transfer could replace, or a source of purpose, standing and independence that nothing else supplies. Someone who thinks work is chiefly a means to a living will welcome a future with less of it if the income is shared; someone who thinks work is how people become full members of a society will want to protect it even at some cost to growth. Both can be reasoning correctly from what they value, and no forecast will reconcile them.
